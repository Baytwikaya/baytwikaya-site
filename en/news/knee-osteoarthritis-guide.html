Knee osteoarthritis: what is it, and why does it affect so many?

Knee osteoarthritis is a gradual wearing down of the cartilage that covers the ends of the bones inside the joint, so the joint moves less smoothly and pain, stiffness and swelling appear. It is one of the most common joint diseases in the world, increases with age, affects women more than men, and is linked to excess weight, previous knee injuries and work that strains the knees.

This guide explains what knee osteoarthritis is and how it develops, its symptoms and risk factors, how it is diagnosed, what actually helps relieve pain and preserve movement, what role cupping and complementary therapies play, and when surgery becomes an option, so you can live as active a life as possible with your knees.

How does knee osteoarthritis develop?

The knee is a complex joint joining the thigh bone, the shin bone and the kneecap, and cartilage covers the ends of these bones to let them glide smoothly and absorb shocks when walking, running and climbing stairs. Over time, this cartilage can gradually wear down, the bones come closer together, inflammation occurs in the joint, and small bony spurs can form around it.

This wearing down does not happen in a single day, but develops slowly over years, and it can begin without clear symptoms, with pain appearing gradually. The World Health Organization notes that osteoarthritis affects hundreds of millions of people worldwide, that the knee is the joint most affected by it, and that the number of people living with it is rising as populations age and weight increases.

Symptoms and risk factors

The most common symptoms of knee osteoarthritis are pain that worsens with movement and eases with rest, morning stiffness that usually lasts less than half an hour, difficulty climbing stairs and rising from a chair, hearing a cracking or grinding sound in the knee, mild swelling after effort, and weakness in the thigh muscles. As the condition advances, pain can be present even at rest, and the shape of the knee can change or the leg can bend.

Risk factors include advancing age, excess weight that multiplies the load on the knee, previous injuries such as torn ligaments or meniscus, work that requires kneeling or carrying heavy loads, some intense sports, a family history of the disease, and weak thigh muscles. In Morocco, excess weight, little movement and long sitting add to the burden on the knees.

How is it diagnosed?

Diagnosis usually relies on the description of symptoms and a clinical examination: the doctor asks about the pain, when it worsens and about previous injuries, and examines the knee, its range of movement, swelling and stability. An X-ray may be requested to see the narrowing of the space between the bones and the bony spurs, which are characteristic signs of osteoarthritis. Most patients do not need an MRI, unless the doctor suspects another problem.

It is important for the patient to know that the severity of pain is not always linked to the severity of the changes on the image: some people have clear changes on X-ray with little pain, and others have severe pain with only mild changes. Treatment therefore focuses on pain and function, not on the image alone. The doctor should also rule out other causes of pain, such as rheumatoid arthritis, gout or infection.

What actually helps? Movement and muscle strengthening

It may seem strange that movement is the first treatment for a knee that hurts when you move, but the scientific evidence is clear: regular exercise, especially strengthening the thigh and hip muscles, relieves pain and improves movement more than many medicines. Light aerobic exercise such as walking, swimming and riding a stationary bicycle strengthens the heart and maintains weight without straining the knee.

The exercises must be gradual and suited to your condition, learned from a physiotherapist, and continued regularly, as the benefit comes with persistence over weeks, not with a single session. Losing excess weight also helps a great deal: every kilogram you lose reduces the load on your knee several times over while walking. Using a cane or a knee brace can help some people walk more comfortably.

Medicines and other treatments

Simple painkillers and anti-inflammatory medicines are used to relieve pain, but they should be used at the lowest effective dose and for the shortest possible time, under a doctor's or pharmacist's guidance, because they can cause stomach, kidney and heart problems with long use, especially in older people. Anti-inflammatory creams applied to the skin can help with fewer side effects.

There are also injections into the joint, such as corticosteroid injections that relieve pain and inflammation for a time, and hyaluronic acid injections, and opinions differ about their effectiveness from person to person. As for supplements such as glucosamine and chondroitin, the evidence for their benefit is weak and mixed, and most guidelines do not recommend them as a main treatment. If all conservative treatments fail and the pain becomes severe and prevents daily life, the doctor may discuss knee replacement surgery with you, an operation that succeeds in many cases.

Cupping and knee osteoarthritis: what do we know?

Some people with knee osteoarthritis turn to cupping around the knee, hoping to relieve pain and swelling. Some small studies have found temporary improvement in pain after cupping, which is why some practitioners mention it as a complementary option. People who have tried it say they feel lightness in the knee and temporary relief.

But we must be honest: the evidence is limited and of low quality, and there are no large trials proving that cupping treats knee osteoarthritis, stops cartilage wearing down or rebuilds it. Worn cartilage does not return with cupping, and the main treatment remains movement, muscle strengthening and weight loss. If you decide to try cupping, do so after consulting your doctor, with a safe practitioner, while continuing your exercises and treatment plan, and do not stop your medicines without consulting your doctor.

Tip

Strengthen your thigh muscles: strong thigh muscles protect the knee and relieve pain more than any painkiller — start with simple exercises sitting on a chair, and increase gradually with a professional's guidance.

A practical plan for an easier daily life with knee osteoarthritis

Organise your day to protect your knees: avoid sitting for a long time in one position, get up and move every hour, avoid climbing stairs often if it hurts and use the lift when available, avoid sitting on the floor or squatting, wear comfortable shoes that support your foot, and use a slightly raised chair that is easy to rise from. At home, keep the things you use often within reach to avoid bending and kneeling.

Practise your exercises regularly, even on days when the pain is less, as stopping brings back weakness and pain. If the pain suddenly increases, or severe swelling, redness and warmth appear in the knee, or you cannot bear weight on it, ask for a medical assessment, as there may be a new problem that needs treatment.

The bottom line: a stronger knee with more movement, not more rest

Knee osteoarthritis is a common and gradual disease, but it is not a sentence of disability: regular movement, strengthening the thigh muscles and losing excess weight are the basis of treatment, and they relieve pain and preserve movement in most cases. Medicines and injections help relieve pain, and surgery is a successful last option when needed. Cupping may give temporary relief to some people, but it is not a treatment for osteoarthritis and does not rebuild cartilage. Work with your doctor on a long-term plan, and move every day.
