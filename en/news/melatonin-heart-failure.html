Melatonin and the heart: does long-term use really raise heart-failure risk? What the study showed — and what it did not

Share this article:

When a sleep pill makes headlines about the heart

Millions of people around the world take melatonin every night in search of calmer sleep. It is a hormone the body releases as darkness falls, and in many countries it is sold over the counter as a food supplement. That is why a story that spread widely in November 2026 caused so much worry: a study linking long-term melatonin use to a higher risk of heart failure. Does that mean a familiar sleep pill is a danger to the heart? The short answer: the story is real, but it needs careful reading — the study itself is preliminary, and it did not prove that melatonin is the cause.

In this article we read what the study actually said, what it did not say, and what people who take melatonin should do today.

What did the study actually find?

The study was presented at the American Heart Association Scientific Sessions 2025 in November 2025, and its abstract appeared in the journal Circulation as abstract 4371606, before the story returned to wide circulation in November 2026. The researchers used the TriNetX global network of electronic health records, following more than 130,000 adults with chronic insomnia over five years; their average age was about 56. Long-term use was defined as taking melatonin for 12 months or more according to the records.

The results looked striking on paper: over five years, new heart failure appeared in 4.6% of long-term melatonin users, compared with 2.7% of non-users — a roughly 90% higher chance. The risk of hospital admission for heart failure was about 3.5 times higher, and the risk of death from any cause about twice as high. These are large numbers — but they are not the whole story.

Before you worry: what did the study not say?

First, this is a preliminary study presented at a scientific conference, and it has not yet been through peer review — independent experts have not yet fully examined its methods and results. Second, it is an observational study: it found a link between melatonin use and heart failure, but it cannot prove that melatonin caused the heart failure. Third, melatonin users were identified from health records only, so over-the-counter purchases — the most common way people buy melatonin — were missed, and the study had no data on dose at all.

And the most important point: when the researchers adjusted the results for age, body mass index, smoking, high blood pressure, diabetes and sleep problems, the increase was no longer statistically significant — meaning the difference between the two groups could be chance or the result of other factors. The study also had no data on how severe the insomnia was, or on psychiatric disorders, which could explain part of the link. Finally, the link may run the other way round: people with heart failure that has not yet been diagnosed often sleep badly, so they turn to melatonin — making melatonin look linked to heart failure, when the heart problem came first.

Why could this link appear even if melatonin is not the cause?

People who have suffered chronic insomnia for years often carry other heart risk factors as well: obesity, high blood pressure, diabetes, smoking or little physical activity, all of which raise the risk of heart failure on their own. And people who are prescribed melatonin in health records may be older or sicker than those who are not. This is what researchers call confounding: a third factor that explains the link between two things that look connected. That is exactly why a single observational study — however large — is not enough to change medical advice; it needs randomised clinical trials comparing people who take melatonin with people who take a placebo.

None of this means the finding is worthless: the sample is large, and the signal deserves follow-up and further research — but it is an early signal, not a final verdict.

Tip

Do not throw your melatonin away and do not stop it suddenly because of one news story — talk to your doctor first, especially if you have taken it for a long time, or if you suffer chronic insomnia or heart disease. And ask your doctor about the causes of your insomnia: sometimes sleep apnoea, anxiety or another medicine lies behind it, and treating the cause is better than a sleeping pill every night. Help your sleep yourself: fixed bedtimes and wake times, a dark cool bedroom, and no screens or caffeine in the evening — these habits improve sleep without any medicine.

The bottom line: a signal worth following, not a final verdict

The study presented in November 2025 and recirculating in November 2026 found a link between long-term melatonin use and a higher risk of heart failure, but it is a preliminary conference abstract that has not been peer-reviewed, an observational study that cannot prove causation, and after full adjustment for other factors the increase was no longer statistically significant. There is no reason for panic — and no reason to ignore the signal either: anyone who has taken melatonin for a long time, or who suffers chronic insomnia, would do well to discuss their sleep and their health with their doctor, and to look together at the causes of the insomnia — because good sleep is good for the heart, and the way to it starts with the right diagnosis, not a pill taken every night without asking.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the results

  • The study: a preliminary conference abstract (AHA Scientific Sessions 2025, abstract 4371606), not peer-reviewed, and an observational study that cannot prove melatonin causes heart failure; presented in November 2025, recirculating in November 2026.
  • The sample: more than 130,000 adults with chronic insomnia, from the TriNetX health-records network, followed for five years; long-term use = 12 months or more; average age about 56.
  • New heart failure over five years: 4.6% among long-term melatonin users versus 2.7% among non-users — about a 90% higher chance.
  • Hospital admission for heart failure: about 3.5 times higher; death from any cause: about twice as high.
  • After full adjustment for age, body mass index, smoking, high blood pressure, diabetes and sleep problems, the increase was no longer statistically significant.
  • Limitations: no dose data; over-the-counter melatonin purchases not recorded; no data on insomnia severity or psychiatric disorders; the link may run the other way (undiagnosed heart failure causing poor sleep).
