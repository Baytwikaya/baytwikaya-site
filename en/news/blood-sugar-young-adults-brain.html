Blood sugar creeps up from young adulthood: how it affects your midlife memory before diabetes

Share this article:

Blood sugar starts rising long before diabetes

When we hear about blood sugar, we usually think of diabetes, as if the problem begins on the day of diagnosis. But a new study says the story starts much earlier: blood sugar can creep up quietly from young adulthood, slowly over decades, even before it reaches the prediabetes range, and this gradual rise is linked to weaker memory and slower thinking in midlife — even in people who never develop diabetes.

The study, led by researcher Regina Paradela of the University of São Paulo Medical School and the Global Brain Health Institute at the University of California, San Francisco, with senior author Kristine Yaffe of the University of California, San Francisco, was published in the Journal of the American Heart Association on 25 November 2026, and followed thousands of people from their youth for almost thirty years.

A study that followed young adults for thirty years

The study drew on the CARDIA study, a long-running American study that began in 1985 and 1986 and enrolled Black and white adults aged 18 to 30 in four US cities. Participants' fasting blood glucose was measured at baseline, and then after 7, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 years — seven measurements over three decades.

The analysis included 2,808 participants, with a mean age of 55.2 years when the memory and thinking tests were done, 56% women and 44% Black. A total of 2,463 of them remained free of type 2 diabetes throughout follow-up, while 345 participants developed diabetes during that period. It is this long duration that makes the study distinctive: it does not compare a single snapshot in midlife, but traces the course of blood sugar from youth onwards.

Three different blood sugar trajectories

When the researchers plotted blood sugar trajectories over thirty years, three clear groups emerged. The first and largest group included 56% of participants — 1,387 people — whose fasting glucose stayed low and stable, at roughly 80 to 85 mg/dL throughout the period. The second group included 38% of participants — 938 people — whose fasting glucose stayed steady at a moderate level, roughly 90 to 100 mg/dL.

The third and smallest group included 5.6% of participants — 138 people — whose glucose started at about 85 mg/dL in young adulthood and then climbed gradually until it reached the prediabetic range, roughly 110 to 115 mg/dL, by midlife. These are the people who matter most in this study, because their blood sugar did not jump suddenly — it crept upwards slowly over decades.

What did they find for memory and the brain?

When the researchers compared the midlife memory and thinking test results, they found that the group whose blood sugar had risen gradually scored worse on memory, processing speed, executive function and composite cognition than the lower-stable group. The same applied to those who developed type 2 diabetes. Most importantly, the increasing-glucose group scored worse even among those who had not developed diabetes during the 30-year study.

As researcher Regina Paradela put it: “The fact that increasing glucose levels were associated with poorer cognitive performance, even among people who had not developed Type 2 diabetes during the 30-year study, is very important,” adding that “changes in glucose metabolism may be relevant to brain health earlier in adulthood than we typically think about.”

As for the brain MRI scans, carried out in 605 participants, they showed that those who developed type 2 diabetes had lower total and parietal gray matter volume and greater parietal white matter abnormalities than the lower-stable group. In other words, the effect of diabetes also showed up in the structure of the brain itself, not only in the thinking tests.

Read the results carefully: an association is not a cause

Here is the point that separates a good article from an exaggerated one. The study is observational: it records an association between gradually rising blood sugar and weaker memory and thinking, but it does not prove that rising sugar is the cause. People whose blood sugar rises may differ from others in diet, physical activity, weight, blood pressure and other factors, and although the researchers accounted for many of these, observation alone cannot rule out every hidden factor.

The study also does not show that lowering blood sugar in young adulthood prevents cognitive decline — that is a question for future research and intervention trials. And the results come from a US cohort, and may not apply to the same degree everywhere. So nobody should read these findings as a personal diagnosis, or as a promise that a single lifestyle change will definitely protect their memory.

Tip

Do not wait for diabetes to appear before thinking about your blood sugar: regular walking, a balanced diet, enough sleep and a healthy weight all help keep blood sugar steady. If your tests show raised fasting glucose, discuss the result with your doctor rather than interpreting it on your own.

The bottom line: blood sugar is a story that starts in youth, not in midlife

The 2026 study in the Journal of the American Heart Association says that blood sugar can creep up quietly from young adulthood, and that those whose glucose rose gradually into the prediabetic range scored worse on memory and thinking in midlife, even without developing diabetes. The message is not that every small rise means an inevitable decline, but that brain health is built over decades, and that paying attention to blood sugar early — through walking, a balanced diet, sleep and a healthy weight, and following up test results with your doctor — may matter more than we thought.
