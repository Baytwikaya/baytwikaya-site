A Handful of Peanuts a Day: Can It Improve Memory and Blood Flow to the Brain? What a Dutch Trial Found in 2026

Share this article:

A small handful every day — but does it reach the brain?

Forgetting the name of someone you met last week, then the word that was on the tip of your tongue, then why you opened the fridge in the first place. Moments everyone over sixty knows, and most of the time they pass without a second thought. But they raise a question that returns with every new study: can something as simple as a handful of peanuts on a plate change anything inside the brain?

In October 2026, the journal Food & Function published the results of a small but striking Dutch trial: 31 healthy adults aged 60 to 75 ate 60 grams of skin-on roasted peanuts, without salt, every day for 16 weeks. After this period, regions of their brains responsible for memory responded better to insulin. And insulin here is not only the blood-sugar hormone; in the brain it is a chemical messenger involved in memory, and the brain's response to it weakens with age.

What exactly did the Dutch trial do?

A team from the NUTRIM institute at Maastricht University Medical Center in the Netherlands, led by Professor Peter Joris, ran a randomized crossover trial: each participant went through two periods of 16 weeks each — one period eating 60 grams of peanuts a day (about two handfuls), and one period eating no peanuts at all, with an eight-week break in between. With this design, each participant becomes their own comparison, which reduces the effect of individual differences between people.

During the trial, the researchers scanned the participants' brains with magnetic resonance imaging, and gave them insulin through the nose — a method that delivers the hormone directly to the brain — then measured how blood flow changed in different brain regions in response to insulin. And the peanuts used were roasted with their skins and without salt, an important detail: the thin skin around a peanut is rich in polyphenols, and excess salt would have worked in the opposite direction on blood pressure and blood vessels.

The new finding: memory regions respond better to insulin

After the peanut period, insulin responsiveness improved in a group of regions in the right half of the brain: the hippocampus, the parahippocampal gyrus, the lingual gyrus, and the posterior cingulate gyrus. These are not random regions; they are at the heart of the brain's memory network — the areas that store experiences and retrieve them, and that weaken early with age and in Alzheimer's disease.

Why does insulin matter in the brain? Because the brain uses insulin as a signal that regulates glucose uptake and communication between nerve cells, and with age this signal becomes weaker, in what researchers sometimes call "brain insulin resistance". So improved responsiveness of these regions to insulin is a sign that the brain is working better at a physiological level — but it is only a sign, not proof that everyday memory improved, nor that dementia was delayed.

The first numbers: blood flow and memory

The new results in Food & Function are a second analysis from the same trial, building on first results the same team published in 2025 in the journal Clinical Nutrition. After 16 weeks of peanuts, total blood flow to the brain rose by 3.6 percent, blood flow in the gray matter rose by 4.5 percent, and verbal memory improved by 5.8 percent — meaning participants remembered on average about 1.4 extra words on a delayed word-recall test. Systolic blood pressure also fell by about 5 points.

And these numbers look modest — and they are: a word and a half extra on a test, and an improvement of a few percentage points in blood flow. But they are consistent with each other: more blood flowing to the brain, memory regions responding better to insulin, and slightly stronger verbal memory. The consistency between three independent measures is what makes the result worth noticing, despite its small size.

Why might peanuts help the brain?

Peanuts are not just fat and calories; they are rich in monounsaturated fats, protein, fiber, vitamin E, magnesium, the polyphenols in their skins, and arginine — an amino acid involved in producing nitric oxide, which widens blood vessels. The researchers suggest that these components together may improve the health of the brain's tiny blood vessels, and reduce inflammation and oxidative stress, two factors linked to memory decline with age.

But this explanation remains a hypothesis the trial itself did not prove: the trial measured the outcome (blood flow and insulin responsiveness) without identifying which peanut component was responsible, nor whether the benefit came from the peanuts themselves or from replacing less healthy snacks. Also, 60 grams of peanuts provide about 340 calories — so adding them to your diet without cutting something else means gaining weight over time, and excess weight itself harms the brain and blood vessels.

Read the numbers carefully: what did the study not prove?

Here is the point that separates a good article from an exaggerated one. First, the trial is small: only 31 participants, all healthy, and only 16 weeks — so it cannot prove that peanuts prevent dementia or Alzheimer's, nor that the results apply to people who already have memory problems. Second, the improvement in verbal memory appeared in a single laboratory test, while executive function and psychomotor speed did not improve, meaning the benefit is limited to one type of memory. Third, another trial published in 2026 in the journal Frontiers in Nutrition gave peanuts and peanut butter for 12 weeks to thirty adults with memory complaints and cardiovascular risk factors, and found no significant change in thinking or brain activation — so the results are not yet agreed upon.

And fourth, a detail that must not be overlooked: the Dutch trial was funded by The Peanut Institute Foundation, and the press release about it came from the Peanut Institute itself. Funding does not mean the results are wrong — the trial was pre-registered, published in a peer-reviewed journal, and its crossover design is strong — but it does mean we need independent confirmation from teams with no connection to the industry before the result becomes a recommendation.

Tip

If you love peanuts, choose the skin-on roasted kind, without salt or sugar, and make it one handful (about 30 grams) instead of two, replacing a processed snack rather than adding it to your food — extra calories harm the brain just as they harm your weight. Important warning: peanuts are among the foods most likely to cause allergies, and anyone with a peanut allergy must avoid them completely and must never try this advice. And if you take blood-thinning medication or follow a prescribed diet, consult your doctor or dietitian before changing your diet.

The takeaway: a small handful, and a promise that needs confirmation

The Maastricht trial says something modest and exciting at the same time: a handful of peanuts a day for four months improved blood flow to the brain, made memory regions respond better to insulin, and added a word or two to verbal memory. But it is a small trial in healthy people, funded by a party with an interest in peanuts, and another trial did not find the same benefit. So peanuts are not a medicine for memory, nor protection against dementia — but, within a balanced diet, they are a cheap and available option that deserves its place on the plate, provided they replace something less healthy, not added on top of it.
