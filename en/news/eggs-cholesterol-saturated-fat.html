Two eggs a day and lower LDL cholesterol? A 2025 study upends what we thought about eggs and saturated fat

Share this article:

Half a century of fearing the egg yolk

For whole generations, the egg was the prime suspect in every conversation about cholesterol. One large egg holds about 186 mg of dietary cholesterol — two-thirds of the daily limit once recommended — and since 1968 the American Heart Association advised people not to exceed three eggs a week. The equation seemed obvious: you eat cholesterol, so the cholesterol in your blood rises. But modern science says the equation was never that simple, and in 2015 the US dietary guidelines dropped the numerical limit on dietary cholesterol altogether.

Then came a trial published in July 2025 in The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition that put the question under the microscope in a way never tried before: what if we separate two factors that always travelled together — egg cholesterol on one side, and saturated fat on the other? The result was surprising enough to reignite the debate in November 2026: participants who ate two eggs every day, within a low-saturated-fat diet, saw their LDL cholesterol fall instead of rising.

The trial that separated cholesterol from saturated fat

Researchers from the University of South Australia designed a randomised crossover trial, one of the most rigorous designs in nutrition research: 61 adults, mean age 39, with LDL cholesterol below 135 mg/dL at the start, each tried three diets, each for five weeks, with a break in between. The first diet was high in cholesterol and low in saturated fat, and included two eggs a day. The second was low in cholesterol and high in saturated fat, with no eggs. The third, the control, was high in both cholesterol and saturated fat, with only one egg a week. With this design it became possible to know which factor really moves cholesterol, because each participant was their own control across the three diets.

And the result was clear: compared with the control diet, the two-eggs diet lowered LDL cholesterol from 109.3 to 103.6 mg/dL, a statistically significant difference, while the “no eggs” diet rich in saturated fat did not differ from the control at all. Across the three diets, saturated fat intake was strongly and positively linked to LDL cholesterol, while dietary cholesterol was not linked to it at all. The conclusion the researchers themselves wrote is blunt: saturated fat is what raises LDL cholesterol, not dietary cholesterol, and eating two eggs a day within a low-saturated-fat diet may lower LDL cholesterol, which could reduce the risk of heart disease.

Not a full acquittal: one small detail changes the picture

Before this result turns into an open licence to eat eggs without limit, the fine details the researchers themselves mentioned must be read. The two-eggs diet lowered total LDL cholesterol, but at the same time it reduced the large LDL particles and increased the small ones — and the small particles are the ones most able to slip into artery walls. The study is also relatively small: only 48 participants completed all three diets, each diet lasted only five weeks, and the participants were healthy with normal starting cholesterol, so from this trial alone we do not know what happens over years, nor what happens to someone who already has high cholesterol.

And there is a point of scientific honesty that must not be overlooked: the study was funded by the Egg Nutrition Center, a division of the American Egg Board, and three of its six authors declare receiving financial support from the same body, although the funding did not intervene, according to the study’s declaration, in its design, analysis or writing. That does not invalidate the result — the crossover design is strong and the findings are published in a peer-reviewed journal — but it is one more reason to read it alongside other independent studies before changing habits.

A second study in 2026: what about people with high cholesterol?

Here comes the most important question for many readers: what about people who already have lipid disorders and take cholesterol-lowering drugs? A study published in September 2026 in Nutrition & Food Science tested exactly that: 50 adults with stable dyslipidaemia receiving lipid-lowering treatment tried eating three eggs a day versus three eggs a week, for six weeks per diet. And the result was different in tone: LDL cholesterol was 126 mg/dL with three eggs a day versus 121 with three a week — a difference of only about 5 mg, which was not statistically significant, with a wide confidence interval that does not rule out a clinically meaningful increase.

The authors themselves explicitly warned against interpreting the result as proof of equivalence or safety: the study is short, the number of participants is small, and the participants were well controlled on treatment, so its results cannot be generalised to someone with uncontrolled dyslipidaemia or established heart disease. The two messages together say one thing: eggs are not the enemy we pictured, but they are not entirely innocent for everyone in every circumstance.

What does the American Heart Association say?

The American Heart Association’s latest official position, in its scientific guidance published in 2020, is consistent with these results without granting an open licence: healthy people can eat one whole egg a day within a heart-healthy eating pattern, and healthy older adults with normal cholesterol can eat two eggs a day, while anyone with dyslipidaemia, diabetes or risk of heart failure should be cautious with cholesterol-rich foods and agree with their doctor on a number that suits them. And the core message of the guidance is not the number of eggs, but the whole eating pattern: patterns naturally low in saturated fat, rich in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, lean protein, nuts and liquid vegetable oils.

And the Association sets one numerical target worth remembering: saturated fat should not exceed 5 to 6 percent of daily calories — about 13 grams in a 2,000-calorie diet. One egg holds only about 1.6 grams of saturated fat, while a tablespoon of butter holds about 7 grams — meaning what you fry the egg in may add four times more saturated fat to your plate than the egg itself.

So what really raises cholesterol on your plate?

If eggs are relatively innocent, then who is the culprit? The answer lies in the foods that usually accompany eggs on the table. Saturated fat concentrates in fatty meats and processed meats such as sausages, in butter and full-fat cheese, and in foods fried in hydrogenated oils. And the cooking method itself changes the equation: a boiled egg or one cooked in steam with whole-grain bread, avocado and olive oil is something entirely different from an egg fried in butter with sausages, bacon and white bread spread with butter. In the second case, it is not the egg that raises cholesterol, but everything around it.

And this explains why the results of long studies differ between peoples: in studies that followed hundreds of thousands of people for decades, eating one egg a day was not linked to a higher risk of heart disease among healthy people, but the picture changes for people with diabetes, where a daily egg was linked to an increase in risk of about a quarter in some analyses — although that may be coincidence or the result of accompanying factors, such as eating more red meat and using cholesterol-lowering drugs less. The whole dietary context is what decides, not one food in isolation.

So how many eggs? The answer depends on your situation

For a healthy adult with normal cholesterol, one egg a day within a healthy eating pattern is the American Heart Association’s recommendation, and two eggs a day are acceptable for healthy older adults. But if your LDL cholesterol is high, or you have diabetes, or you take a cholesterol-lowering drug, the safest course is to agree with your doctor on a specific number — usually lower — and to monitor your tests eight to twelve weeks after any change. And for anyone who wants the egg’s protein without its cholesterol, egg white is a practical option: it has no cholesterol and no saturated fat, and you can mix two egg whites with one whole egg to increase protein without increasing cholesterol.

And the practical rule that sums up everything above is simple: do not count the cholesterol in the egg; count the saturated fat in your whole plate. If your breakfast is two boiled eggs with whole-grain bread, vegetables and olive oil, you are in the safe zone for most people — but if the same breakfast swims in butter with processed meats, the problem is not the two eggs.

Tip

This week, try one small change to your breakfast: boil the eggs or cook them in steam instead of frying them in butter, keep butter and sausages off the plate, and add vegetables or whole-grain bread instead. Then ask your doctor at your next visit one specific question: how many eggs are right for me, given my latest cholesterol test? The right answer is not one number for everyone, but a number that fits your situation.

The bottom line: eggs are not the enemy — but not entirely innocent either

What the 2025 trial revealed is not that eggs are a superfood, but that we were chasing the wrong suspect for half a century: the dietary cholesterol in an egg has a small and variable effect on LDL cholesterol, while the saturated fat in the whole diet is the real driver. Two eggs a day within a low-saturated-fat diet lowered LDL cholesterol in healthy people, but the study is small, short and funded by the egg industry, and the 2026 study in people with high cholesterol did not give the same green light. So eat your eggs boiled if you like, watch what accompanies them on the plate, and agree with your doctor on your own number — cholesterol is the story of a whole eating pattern, not of a single egg.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the results

  • One large egg: about 186 mg of dietary cholesterol, and only about 1.6 g of saturated fat.
  • 2025 trial (University of South Australia): 61 adults, 3 diets, 5 weeks per diet; 48 participants completed all three diets.
  • Two-eggs-a-day diet (high cholesterol, low saturated fat): LDL cholesterol 103.6 vs 109.3 mg/dL in the control diet (p=0.02); the “no eggs” diet rich in saturated fat did not differ from the control (107.7, p=0.52).
  • Saturated fat was positively linked to LDL cholesterol (β=0.35, p=0.002), while dietary cholesterol was not (β=-0.006, p=0.42).
  • Important caveat: the two-eggs diet increased small LDL particles and reduced large ones; and the study was funded by the Egg Nutrition Center (American Egg Board).
  • 2026 study (dyslipidaemia on lipid-lowering treatment): 50 participants, 3 eggs a day vs 3 a week, LDL cholesterol 126 vs 121 mg/dL, difference 5.0 mg (p=0.25) — not significant, and does not prove equivalence or safety.
  • American Heart Association (2020): one whole egg a day for healthy people, two a day for healthy older adults with normal cholesterol; saturated fat no more than 5–6% of daily calories.
