Why do we get sick more in winter? Cold, noses and dry air — what the science says

Share this article:

A pattern everyone knows every winter

With the first cold spells, the same story begins in every home: sneezing here, coughing there, a cold passed from one person to the next, and longer queues at clinics and pharmacies. Many people sum it up in one sentence: “the cold is the cause.” But science says something more precise: cold alone does not give you an infection — a virus is needed first — yet winter conditions make viruses spread more easily while weakening your body’s first line of defence: your nose.

On 16 November 2026, Al Jazeera published an article by Dr Alia Abu Suleiman, a family doctor and fellow of the Royal College, explaining why we get sick more in winter, drawing on recent studies about the nose, dry air and how viruses travel. So what did these studies actually find? And where do their limits lie?

Cold alone does not make you ill: a virus is needed

The first point doctors stress is that feeling cold is not an infection. Colds, flu and winter respiratory illnesses are caused by viruses, and nobody can catch one without being exposed to a virus in the first place, however cold the weather. But cold creates the conditions that make meeting a virus more likely and the body less ready to fight it off. Winter does not create illness out of nothing; it opens the doors to it.

This distinction matters in practice: prevention is not about warmth alone, but about reducing exposure to viruses, supporting the body’s defences, and avoiding crowded, poorly ventilated places as much as possible.

Your nose is the first line of defence — and cold weakens it

Inside your nose works a clever defence system: nasal cells release tiny particles called extracellular vesicles, which trap viruses before they reach deeper into the body. A study from Harvard and Northeastern universities, published in 2022 in the Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology, found that cold reduces the release of these vesicles and weakens their antiviral function, in laboratory experiments and on human nasal tissue.

The result is that the cold air you breathe in winter may leave your nose less able to stop viruses at the very first moment — the moment that often decides whether a virus takes hold or not. But this finding must be read within its limits: the study was done in the laboratory and on tissue, and it did not measure actual infection rates in people, so it explains a plausible mechanism rather than proving that cold raises infection by a given amount.

Dry air helps viruses travel

The second factor is the air itself. In winter the air becomes cold and dry, indoors and out, and that helps the flu virus survive and spread. A study published in 2007 in PLOS Pathogens, carried out by Mount Sinai researchers on guinea pigs in 20 experiments, showed that flu spreads best in cold, dry air, when relative humidity is between 20 and 35 percent.

A scientific review published in 2020 in the Annual Review of Virology also concluded that low temperature and humidity affect virus stability, transmission and the respiratory immune response. But here too a warning is needed: the guinea pig study is an animal study, and its results cannot be translated directly to humans — it supports the idea without settling it on its own.

Crowding indoors and poor ventilation

The third factor is behavioural rather than biological: in winter we spend more time indoors, close to each other, with windows shut and less ventilation. Every cough or sneeze in a closed room raises the concentration of viruses in the air and increases the chance that someone else breathes them in. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) notes that improving indoor air quality helps reduce the spread of respiratory infections.

So three factors come together in winter: viruses that travel more easily in dry air, a nose less able to defend itself in the cold, and people closer together in closed spaces. No single one of them is “the cause” — it is their combination that makes the season of illness.

Which viruses spread most in winter?

The main winter viruses are four: seasonal flu (types A and B), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), which is dangerous for babies and older people, rhinoviruses, which cause the common cold, and COVID-19, which is still circulating. Each has its own season and severity, but they all spread the same way: through droplets, air, contaminated surfaces and hands.

It is important to remember that antibiotics do not treat viral infections — they are drugs for bacteria, and taking them for a cold or flu does not help and may cause harm. Flu, on the other hand, may need antiviral medicines taken early in people at higher risk of complications, on a doctor’s prescription.

What do the studies say? Read their limits fairly

The strength of this topic is that it rests on real studies, and its weakness is that each study has its limits. The 2022 nose study was done in the laboratory and on human tissue, and did not measure infections in people. The 2007 flu-transmission study was done on animals in 20 experiments, and does not translate directly to humans. And the 2020 review gathers the available evidence without giving a single figure for infection risk. The overall picture is convincing, but the fine details are still being researched.

That does not weaken the practical message — it strengthens it: prevention does not depend on one study, but on a set of simple measures taken together, each reducing risk a little, and together making a big difference.

Tip

To reduce winter illness: get the flu vaccine, especially if you are in a group at higher risk of complications or live with someone who is; ventilate your home regularly and open windows for a few minutes every day; wash your hands often and avoid touching your face; wear a mask when needed in crowded places; and if you feel ill, rest and avoid passing the infection to others. See a doctor urgently if you have breathing difficulty, persistent chest pain, confusion or severe dehydration. And remember: antibiotics do not treat colds or flu, so do not take them without a medical prescription.

The bottom line: cold sets the scene, the virus is the cause

Yes, we do get sick more in winter — but not because cold creates illness. Cold weakens the nose’s defences, dry air helps viruses travel and survive, and gathering indoors brings us closer to each other and to viruses at the same time. The viruses themselves — flu, RSV, cold viruses and COVID-19 — are what make us ill, and protection is possible with simple steps: vaccination, ventilation, handwashing, resting when ill, and seeking urgent care when danger signs appear.
