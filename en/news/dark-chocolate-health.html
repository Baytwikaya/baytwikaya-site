Dark chocolate, your heart and your mood: what three 2026 studies found

Share this article:

A square of dark chocolate… what happens inside your arteries?

Many people love dark chocolate and describe it as the “healthy” choice among sweets. But does that reputation hold up? In 2026, three studies looked at dark chocolate from completely different angles: the first measured blood flow and blood pressure, the second followed mood and gut bacteria, and the third tracked diabetes risk over years. Together, the results paint an encouraging picture — but one far from a licence to eat chocolate without limits.

So what did each study find? And where does each result stop? Let us start with the strongest of the three.

A pooled analysis of 25 trials: a clear improvement in artery flexibility

Researchers published a meta-analysis in 2026 in The Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging, pooling 25 randomised clinical trials with 1,167 participants, after searching the scientific literature up to December 2025. All the interventions were sources of cocoa flavanols: chocolate, a cocoa drink, or cocoa-extract tablets, at doses ranging from 10.6 to 1,680 mg per day, for periods of two weeks to six months.

The results were consistent: flow-mediated dilation (FMD) — a measure of how well arteries widen — improved by 1.31 percentage points (95% confidence interval 1.04–1.59), pulse wave velocity — a measure of arterial stiffness — fell by 0.20 metres per second, and systolic blood pressure fell by 2.7 mmHg and diastolic by 1.8 mmHg. Most importantly, the same direction held in every subgroup: in people with hypertension, in people with normal blood pressure, and in healthy people.

These figures are modest at the individual level — a 2.7 mmHg drop in blood pressure will not replace anyone’s medicine — but they matter at population level, because a small improvement in artery function across millions of people may translate into lower heart risk. The bigger question remains: does this improvement last with ordinary consumption over years, or is it tied to carefully dosed amounts in short trials?

Who paid for the study: a point you should know before believing it

Here comes the serious caveat. The meta-analysis was funded by Meiji Co., a Japanese cocoa-food maker, and five of the study’s authors are employees of that company. That does not mean the numbers are invented — the analysis pooled published trials and measured their results with a standard method — but it does mean readers are right to be cautious: industry funding has historically been linked to more positive results, even if only through the choice of questions or the way findings are presented.

The honest reading, then, is this: the finding is promising and worth attention, but it needs confirmation from independent analyses not funded by the cocoa industry before it becomes an official dietary recommendation.

Are 200 mg of flavanols enough? And what is the problem with labels?

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) had already accepted in 2012 that 200 mg of cocoa flavanols a day helps maintain normal blood flow — an amount equal to about 10 g of high-flavanol dark chocolate, or about 2.5 g of high-flavanol cocoa powder. But the practical problem is that flavanol content is not printed on food labels, and manufacturing can cut it to one ninth of its original value. An expensive dark chocolate bar may carry far fewer flavanols than a cheaper one, and the consumer has no way to tell the difference from the label.

So the name “dark chocolate” alone is not enough: focusing on the cocoa percentage is misleading, because a high cocoa percentage does not necessarily mean high flavanols after processing.

Mood and gut bacteria: a small Korean trial worth noting

In September 2026, a Korean study followed 48 healthy adults aged 20 to 30 for three weeks. Participants ate 30 g a day of 85% dark chocolate, and showed a significant improvement in negative mood — measured with the PANAS questionnaire — and greater gut bacteria diversity, with shifts in two species, Blautia obeum and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii. The group that ate 70% chocolate showed no such effect.

The result is intriguing, but far from settled: the study was small and had no placebo group, so the improvement cannot be separated from expectation or from other lifestyle changes during those three weeks. It is too early to call chocolate a mood treatment.

Dark chocolate and diabetes: what did the Harvard data show?

The third study is the largest in scope: an analysis from the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health followed about 192,000 people in three long-term cohorts — the Nurses’ Health Studies I and II and the Health Professionals Follow-Up Study. It found that eating at least five servings a week of dark chocolate — a serving being about 28 g (one ounce) — was linked to a 21% lower risk of type 2 diabetes. Eating five servings a week of any chocolate was linked to only a 10% lower risk, while eating milk chocolate regularly was linked to a higher risk.

The contrast between dark and milk chocolate matters: the first is rich in flavanols and lower in sugar, while the second adds sugar and fat without the same benefit. But remember that this is an observational study: it links a habit to a risk, it does not prove that chocolate itself is the cause, because dark-chocolate lovers may differ in other habits — diet, physical activity — from everyone else.

Tip

If you enjoy dark chocolate, choose one with a high cocoa percentage (70% or more) and eat a small piece — about 28 g — a few times a week as part of a balanced diet, not as an extra daily habit. And remember it is not a medicine: it does not replace blood pressure or diabetes treatment, and its calories and sugar add up if the portion grows. Milk chocolate is an ordinary sweet, without the same benefit. If you have diabetes or take blood pressure medicine, talk to your doctor before changing your eating habits.

The bottom line: a real benefit, but modest and conditional

The picture drawn by the 2026 studies is balanced: cocoa flavanols improve artery function and lower blood pressure slightly, and dark chocolate is linked to better mood and lower diabetes risk — but the improvement is modest, the strongest study was funded by the cocoa industry, the mood study was small with no placebo, and the diabetes data are observational and cannot prove cause. Dark chocolate is a tasty and useful addition to a balanced diet, not a treatment and not an excuse for excess. The wider lesson is that food works by accumulation: small habits repeated, not one magic square.
