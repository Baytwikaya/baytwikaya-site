The Mediterranean diet and biological age: can what you eat really make you younger?

Share this article:

When your body's age runs ahead of your birth certificate

Two people aged fifty: one climbs the stairs without panting and remembers appointments without effort, while the other feels his body is ten years older than his age. The difference is not always in the date of birth, but in what scientists call “biological age” — the actual state of the body, measured through blood markers, inflammation and memory, and even through chemical changes on DNA measured by what are called “epigenetic clocks”.

And this month's news revives an old question: can a way of eating alone make that biological age younger? A new systematic review in Nutrition Reviews, the DIRECT PLUS trial, and a recent British study say that Mediterranean-style eating is linked to a younger biological age — but the word “linked” carries a lot of weight here, as we shall see.

What did the systematic review find?

The review was published in Nutrition Reviews on 2 November 2026, and gathered 11 human studies from Europe, the United States and the Middle East, carried out between 2013 and 2025, ranging from randomised trials to cohort and cross-sectional studies. It concluded that Mediterranean-style eating — vegetables, legumes, whole grains, olive oil and fish, with little red meat — is linked to a younger biological age, better memory and less frailty.

Its most striking finding comes from the DIRECT PLUS trial, which showed at 18 months an average 8.9-month favourable difference between observed and expected biological age. But the “green” version of the Mediterranean diet was not significantly more effective than other Mediterranean versions. The NU-AGE trial, which lasted a year, showed lower inflammation, better handgrip strength and some better memory measures.

And the review's own warning is clear: this does not prove that diet slows ageing. The studies measured diet and ageing in different ways, and differed in design and population, which makes comparing them difficult, and a cautious conclusion is the only one possible.

DIRECT PLUS: green tea, Mankai and walnuts

The DIRECT PLUS trial lasted 18 months and enrolled 294 adults with abdominal obesity or dyslipidaemia, with a mean age of 51, 88% of them men. Participants were divided into three groups: general healthy guidelines, a calorie-restricted Mediterranean diet with 28 g of walnuts a day, and a “green Mediterranean” diet adding 3 to 4 cups of green tea and 100 g of the Mankai plant a day, with less red and processed meat.

The results were striking for the liver and the brain: for liver fat, the median reduction was 38.9% in the green group against 19.6% in the Mediterranean group. And in the brain, in participants aged 50 and over, the decline in “hippocampal occupancy” — an imaging measure linked to memory — was 0.8% in the green group against 1.3% in the healthy-guidelines group.

As for the epigenetic analysis, published in Nature Medicine and covering 256 participants, it showed that closer adherence to the diet was linked to a smaller increase on 2 of 6 epigenetic clocks, with Mankai and green tea the most associated with this effect. And the most important point: the three groups did not differ from each other on any clock — the signal came from adherence to the diet, not from the group a participant was placed in.

The Airwave study: a small link in a younger sample

A third study, published in the Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging in October 2026, looked at the question from a different angle: a cross-sectional study of 928 British police employees, with a mean age of 41, 59% of them men, who recorded their food in a 7-day food diary, and whose adherence to the Mediterranean diet was scored from 0 to 10.

The study found that a higher score was linked to lower age acceleration on two epigenetic clocks: GrimAge (−0.07 standard deviations, 95% confidence interval −0.13 to −0.01) and Bernabeu (−0.08 standard deviations, −0.14 to −0.02). These are small effects, and the study is cross-sectional and cannot prove causation — it does not say that food is what slowed ageing. It also found that smoking was strongly linked to higher age acceleration, a factor that outweighs diet in this sample.

Why this does not prove diet slows ageing

Three reasons call for caution. First, most of the evidence is associational: people who eat Mediterranean-style also tend to exercise more, smoke less, sleep better and enjoy better living conditions — all factors that affect biological age, and studies cannot fully separate them from diet. Second, “epigenetic clocks” are new measuring tools, they differ a great deal between studies, and small changes in them — such as 0.07 standard deviations — do not necessarily mean more years of health. Third, even in our best trial, DIRECT PLUS, the signal came from adherence rather than from random assignment, which weakens the claim that one specific element of the diet is the cause.

This does not diminish the value of the Mediterranean pattern — it is one of the best-evidenced eating patterns for heart and brain health — but it puts the results in their proper size: a healthy eating pattern linked to better health, not an elixir of youth that stops time.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the results

  • Systematic review, Nutrition Reviews, 2 November 2026: 11 human studies (Europe, US, Middle East, 2013–2025).
  • DIRECT PLUS: an average 8.9-month favourable difference between observed and expected biological age at 18 months.
  • DIRECT PLUS (epigenetic analysis, Nature Medicine): 294 participants, mean age 51, 88% men, over 18 months; epigenetic subset of 256 participants.
  • Liver fat: median reduction of 38.9% (green-MED) versus 19.6% (MED).
  • Hippocampal occupancy decline (age 50+): 0.8% (green-MED) versus 1.3% (healthy guidelines).
  • Epigenetic clocks: closer adherence linked to a smaller increase on 2 of 6 clocks; the three groups did not differ from each other on any clock.
  • Airwave (Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging, October 2026): 928 participants, mean age 41, 59% men; GrimAge −0.07 SD (95% CI −0.13 to −0.01); Bernabeu −0.08 SD (−0.14 to −0.02).

Tip

The Mediterranean diet is a whole way of eating, not a single magic ingredient: vegetables, legumes, whole grains, olive oil, fish and nuts, with less red and processed meat — and there is no need to hunt for Mankai or drink large amounts of green tea in search of youth, as the evidence does not support that. And if you smoke, quitting is the strongest step for your biological age, before any change to your plate. Before any major change to your diet, especially if you take medicines, talk to your doctor first.

The bottom line: an eating pattern worth adopting, not an elixir of youth

The picture painted by the new evidence is consistent: people who eat Mediterranean-style look biologically younger, with better memory and less frailty — an 8.9-month difference in the DIRECT PLUS trial, and small but consistent effects in the Airwave study, support that direction. But it is a picture of association, not proof: the studies measured diet and ageing in different ways, the strongest signal came from adherence rather than assignment, and smoking outweighs diet in effect. So adopt the Mediterranean pattern because it is a healthy pattern with proven benefits for the heart and brain — not because it stops the clock.
