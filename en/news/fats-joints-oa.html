Change your fat to protect your knee? What a 2026 study revealed about fats and osteoarthritis

Share this article:

When your knee starts protesting about your plate

You wake up in the morning to find your knee stiff, you climb the stairs hearing a faint sound with every step, and after sitting for a long time you struggle to stand up. Osteoarthritis — the wearing away of the cartilage that separates bones — is one of the most common causes of disability in the world as we age, and to this day there is no treatment that rebuilds cartilage. That is why scientists have spent years looking for factors that can be changed before the wear begins, including what we eat every day.

The idea is not new: chronic low-grade inflammation is now seen as one of the drivers of cartilage erosion and inflammation of the joint lining, and the fats we eat affect this inflammation — some feed it, some calm it. But does that actually translate into a difference in who develops osteoarthritis and who does not? A new study published in November 2026 in Frontiers in Nutrition tried to answer that question, and it was the largest study of its kind so far: more than 93,000 people in three countries, followed by the researchers for years to see who developed osteoarthritis. The result contains something encouraging — and something that calls for caution in how we read it.

A study from three countries: what exactly did the researchers do?

The study combined data from three large population cohorts: the UK Biobank, with 89,877 participants; the US Health and Retirement Study (HRS), with 1,118 participants; and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), with 2,134 participants — 93,129 people in total, all aged 50 or over, and all free of osteoarthritis at the start of follow-up.

The researchers recorded what participants ate through repeated dietary questionnaires — in the UK Biobank, for example, each participant completed at least two questionnaires before follow-up began — then followed them for years: in the UK from June 2012 to May 2022, about ten years. During follow-up, 11,594 new cases of osteoarthritis were recorded: 11,139 in the UK (12.4% of participants), 134 in the US study (12.0%), and 321 in the English study (15.0%).

The pre-specified primary question was: is the ratio of unsaturated to saturated fat in the diet — the balance of fats, not their amount alone — linked to the onset of osteoarthritis? They then examined each type of fat separately, as a share of daily calories.

First result: the fat ratio was not decisive

Here came the first surprise, and it does not favour sensational headlines: the ratio of unsaturated to saturated fat showed only a very small inverse association with the onset of osteoarthritis — for each one-standard-deviation increase in the log of the ratio, risk fell by only 1.9% (HR 0.981; 95% CI 0.963–0.999; p=0.040). And the number itself reveals its fragility: using a more conservative statistical method (Hartung–Knapp), statistical significance disappeared entirely (HR 0.981; 95% CI 0.942–1.022; p=0.177), and when participants were split into quartiles, the difference between the highest and lowest quartile was not significant (HR 0.957; 95% CI 0.908–1.010; p=0.110).

The researchers wrote this plainly in their discussion: the main hypothesis received only limited support, the association was small and close to null, it weakened after adjusting for body mass index, and it did not appear in the sensitivity analyses. If the story were only about the "ratio", this article's headline would have been "nothing much to report". But the story did not end there.

The stronger result: unsaturated fats themselves

When the researchers looked at unsaturated fats alone — as a share of daily calories — a clearer association appeared: each additional five percentage points in the share of energy from unsaturated fats was associated with a 15% lower risk of developing osteoarthritis (HR 0.847; 95% CI 0.798–0.898; p=3.99×10⁻⁸), equivalent to a 3.3% reduction with each extra percentage point (HR 0.967; 95% CI 0.956–0.979). In the UK Biobank alone, the highest quartile of intake of these fats had an 11.6% lower risk than the lowest quartile (HR 0.884; 95% CI 0.838–0.932), with a clear trend across quartiles (p=2.78×10⁻⁶).

But even this stronger association needs careful reading: the UK Biobank contributed 92.9% of the statistical weight of the pooled analysis, so the result is essentially British; in the US study the estimate was close to null (HR 0.993), and in the English study it pointed in the same direction but without statistical significance (HR 0.868; 95% CI 0.670–1.123).

To understand the size of the difference in practice: at a low level of unsaturated fat (4.00% of calories) versus a higher level (7.39%), the cumulative probability of developing osteoarthritis over five years was 6.81% versus 6.10% — a difference of only 0.71 percentage points — and over nine years 11.74% versus 10.56%, a difference of 1.18 percentage points. A real difference, but small for an individual, and its true meaning shows at the population level.

And what about saturated fats? A small surprise

Who would have expected saturated fats — the fats of butter and fatty meats — to also appear linked to a slight reduction in risk, rather than an increase? That is what the study found: each additional five percentage points in the share of energy from saturated fat was associated with a 4.5% lower risk (HR 0.955; 95% CI 0.925–0.987; p=0.005). This does not mean saturated fat "protects" the joints — the models measured each fat separately, and associations in observational studies are influenced by many unmeasured factors — but it is a reminder that the story is not simple, and that separating one fat from the rest of the diet and lifestyle is difficult.

In an exploratory analysis limited to the UK Biobank, the researchers calculated the effect of "substitution": what if a person replaced 5% of their calories from saturated fat with unsaturated fat, keeping total calories constant? That was associated with a 17% lower risk (HR 0.830; 95% CI 0.765–0.900), and replacing 5% of calories from carbohydrates with unsaturated fat was associated with a 23% lower risk (HR 0.770; 95% CI 0.700–0.845). The second surprise, however, pointed in the opposite direction: replacing saturated fat with monounsaturated fat (olive oil, for example) was associated with a 19.7% higher risk (HR 1.197; 95% CI 1.083–1.323) — an unexpected result, which the researchers themselves present as an exploratory analysis rather than a conclusion, especially since the small US study could not confirm any of these figures given its small sample.

How might fats affect the joint?

The proposed biological explanation runs through inflammation. Unsaturated fatty acids, especially omega-3s, may reduce the production of inflammation-causing substances (eicosanoids) and increase the production of specialised inflammation-resolving mediators such as resolvins and protectins. By contrast, saturated fats have been linked in experimental studies to inflammatory signalling via the TLR4 receptor and the NF-κB pathway. But the researchers acknowledge that most of the evidence for these mechanisms comes from animals and laboratory experiments, and that the epidemiological evidence in humans is still limited and inconsistent — which is precisely the gap their study tried to fill.

And there is a second, simpler pathway: weight. Unsaturated fats are part of dietary patterns — such as the Mediterranean pattern — that are usually linked to a lighter weight and more movement, and every extra kilogram multiplies the load on the knee when walking. The study adjusted for body mass index in sensitivity analyses and the association remained, but statistical adjustment does not completely remove the effect of a whole lifestyle.

Read the numbers carefully: what did the study not prove?

Here is the point that separates a good article from an exaggerated one. First, the study is observational: it followed people as they ate, without randomly assigning them to one diet or another, so it cannot prove that swapping fats is the cause of the lower risk — perhaps people who ate more unsaturated fats also moved more, smoked less, or ate more vegetables. Second, the strongest result depends almost entirely on the UK Biobank, and the other two studies were small and confirmed nothing on their own. Third, the study's estimates are sensitive to the design: when only the first dietary questionnaire was used instead of the repeated average, the association for unsaturated fats weakened considerably (HR 0.936 versus 0.843), and the ratio association disappeared entirely (HR 0.998). Fourth, the participants who completed two dietary questionnaires were more educated, lighter in weight, and smoked less than the others — in other words, the sample itself was selective. And fifth, and most important of all, the researchers themselves said in their conclusion that these observational results "do not prove causality and do not justify setting a dietary target for osteoarthritis prevention".

So there is no "magic amount" of unsaturated fat that prevents osteoarthritis, and no evidence that omega-3 capsules or an extra bottle of olive oil will change your knee's fate on their own. What exists is a small, consistent signal: a dietary pattern rich in unsaturated fats — oily fish, nuts, seeds, and olive oil — is linked to slightly better joints, within a whole lifestyle that includes weight and movement.

Tip

Don't look for a "magic fat" and don't buy a supplement that promises to protect your knee — the study itself does not justify that. The practical step is small, steady swaps: make oily fish (sardines or salmon) part of your table once or twice a week, a handful of walnuts or almonds instead of a processed snack, olive oil as your main fat for cooking and salads, and less fatty meat and fried food. And more important than the plate itself: keep a healthy weight and strengthen your thigh muscles with walking and strength exercises — that is what truly lightens the load on the knee. And if you take any medicine or follow a prescribed diet, do not stop it or change it on your own — consult your doctor or dietitian first.

The bottom line: a small signal, not a magic recipe

The 2026 study — the largest of its kind — says something modest and honest: people who eat more unsaturated fats have slightly fewer recorded diagnoses of osteoarthritis, the difference for an individual is small (less than one percentage point over five years), and the study is observational and does not prove that fat is the cause. So do not turn olive oil into medicine, nor butter into an enemy; look at the whole plate: fish, nuts, seeds, and olive oil, with a lighter weight and more movement — that whole pattern is what the studies actually measure, and it is what serves your knee in the long run.
