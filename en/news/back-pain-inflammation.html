Does your back hurt? The problem may lie beyond your back: inflammation, heart and liver in 3 recent studies

Share this article:

When the back alone is not the problem

Low back pain is the most common pain in the world: the World Health Organization estimates that about 619 million people suffered from it in 2020. Strangely, most cases are classified as “non-specific” — meaning the pain is thought to come from the muscles, joints and tissues of the back, with no clear disease visible on a scan or a test, such as an infection, a tumour or a fracture. So if the back itself looks fine on imaging, why does the pain persist in so many people?

Three recent studies, published between November 2025 and January 2026, suggest a broader answer: the health of the whole body may be part of the story. The first links pain to an inflammation marker calculated from an ordinary blood test. The second finds that the very habits that protect the heart are also linked to a lower risk of back pain. And the third links pain to an index that estimates the likelihood of fatty liver. So what did the numbers really say? And what have they not yet said?

Inflammation: a number from an ordinary blood test linked to pain

Inflammation is, at its origin, a natural reaction that helps the body fight infection and injury. But when it stays silently active for months and years — because of a poor diet, lack of movement, smoking, excess alcohol or chronic stress — it becomes chronic low-grade inflammation, which changes how tissues respond to injury and recover. A study published in January 2026 in the European Journal of Pain measured this inflammation with a simple index calculated from an ordinary complete blood count: the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), which is the platelet count multiplied by the neutrophil count, divided by the lymphocyte count.

The study analysed data from 17,286 American adults in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) for 2001–2004 and 2009–2010. The result: people in the highest quarter of the inflammation index had 66% higher odds of low back pain than those in the lowest quarter (OR 1.66). The association remained clear in both sexes and was strongest in younger people: in the 20–40 age group the odds almost doubled (OR 2.05), and in the 40–60 age group they were 46% higher (OR 1.46). And every increase in the index itself was linked to higher odds (OR 1.41 per log unit).

But the study design calls for caution: it is cross-sectional, meaning it measured inflammation and pain at the same time, so it cannot prove that inflammation causes pain, nor determine which came first. The pain itself may be what reduces movement and increases inflammation, not the other way round. The researchers therefore describe the index as a possible predictive marker worth following up, not a diagnosis or a proven cause.

The heart and the back: the same habits protect both

The second study is stronger in design: a follow-up study published in November 2025 in The Spine Journal, which tracked 140,313 participants from the UK Biobank for a median of 13.54 years, during which 10,036 of them developed low back pain. Its question is simple: do people who follow heart-health habits develop back pain less often?

The habits the study measured are the American Heart Association’s “Life’s Essential 8”: a healthy diet, regular physical activity, not smoking, enough sleep, a healthy weight, healthy blood pressure, healthy cholesterol and healthy blood sugar. The result: in people who followed an ideal lifestyle, the risk of developing back pain was about 12.26% lower, and in those who maintained high cardiovascular health, the risk was 9.29% lower. By contrast, the risk in people whose lifestyle was poor was 25% higher than in those with an ideal lifestyle (HR 1.25), and 28% higher in those with poor heart health (HR 1.28).

When the researchers broke the factors down one by one, the largest contribution came from prolonged sitting (12.62% of cases), followed by excess weight (11.25%), then unhealthy sleep patterns (7.01%), then abnormal sleep duration (5.28%), then current or past smoking (4.61%), then low physical activity (3.64%), then poor blood sugar control (0.58%). This study is a follow-up study, not a cross-sectional one — it watched people before they developed pain, which is stronger — but it is still an observational study: it does not prove that changing any single habit prevents pain, because people who live healthily differ from others in many things the study does not measure.

Fatty liver: an index out of 100 and its link to the back

The third study looks at another corner of metabolic health: fatty liver. The researchers studied 5,339 adults from the same American survey (NHANES 1999–2004) and used the fatty liver index (FLI) — a number from 0 to 100 calculated from four values: body mass index, waist circumference, triglycerides, and the liver enzyme gamma-glutamyl transferase — to estimate the likelihood of fat in the liver without imaging.

The result, published in November 2025: every 20-point increase in the index was linked to 9% higher odds of low back pain (OR 1.09). The link was clearer in people with less physical activity. But this study is also cross-sectional — a snapshot at a single moment — and does not prove that fatty liver causes back pain. The possible link between them is understandable: obesity, lack of movement and chronic inflammation are shared factors that raise both indices together and increase pain sensitivity at the same time.

Even so, the result carries an indirect practical message: metabolic health markers — weight, waist circumference, triglycerides — are not numbers that concern only the heart and liver; they also appear in back research. The body does not work in separate boxes, and what burdens the liver and the arteries may burden the back as well.

Read the numbers with caution: association does not mean cause

Here comes the point that separates a good article from one that exaggerates. Of the three studies, two are cross-sectional (inflammation and fatty liver) and cannot prove causation, and the third is an observational follow-up study, not a randomised trial. None of them proved that lowering inflammation alone, or lowering the fatty liver index alone, heals back pain or prevents it. The pain may be the cause, not the result: when your back hurts, you move less, your weight rises, your sleep worsens and your inflammation increases — so all the markers deteriorate together, without any one of them being the first cause.

And there is another warning: the figures here are relative, not absolute. “66% higher” does not mean that two-thirds of people will develop pain; it means that the odds of pain in the most inflamed group are two-thirds higher than in the least inflamed group — and the original odds themselves may be small. Likewise, the “12% lower risk” in the heart study is an estimate of the share of cases that could theoretically be avoided if everyone followed the ideal lifestyle, not a personal promise to any individual.

And with all this caution, the direction of the evidence remains consistent: three large databases and three different indices say the same thing — people who live a generally healthy lifestyle, move more, sleep better, smoke less and keep a healthy weight, develop back pain less often. This does not erase the role of muscles, flexibility, previous injuries and the physical demands of work, but it adds a layer medicine used to neglect: the health of the whole body.

Tip

Don’t wait for your back pain to disappear with a single treatment, and don’t look for a magic test to explain it — work on the whole foundation: move every day, even just by walking, break up long sitting by standing and stretching every hour, sleep 7–9 hours at regular times, lose weight gradually if it is high, stay away from smoking, and make your diet closer to the Mediterranean pattern — vegetables, fish, olive oil, nuts and legumes — and cut back on sugars and ultra-processed foods. If the pain is severe, lasts more than a few weeks, or comes with numbness or weakness in the legs or problems with urination, see your doctor immediately, and do not stop any prescribed medicine or start a new treatment on your own.

Conclusion: the back is a mirror of the whole body’s health

The picture drawn by the three studies is not that back pain is an “inflammation disease” or a “liver disease” or a “heart disease”, but that the back lives in one body: high inflammation in the blood, poor heart habits and a high fatty liver index — all are linked to the same pain, and all improve with the same habits: more movement, better sleep, a healthier weight, less smoking and cleaner food. The first treatment for back pain remains — as the guidelines say — a structured exercise programme and continued activity, but these studies add that exercise alone may not be enough if the rest of the body remains in a state of inflammation and metabolic strain. Start with movement, and fix what surrounds it: your sleep, your plate, your weight and your habits — for the back, in the end, is a mirror of the whole body’s health.
