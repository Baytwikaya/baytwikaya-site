Injectable gel regrows knee cartilage? What a 2024 sheep study actually showed

Share this article:

When cartilage cannot heal itself

Picture a knee that hurts with every flight of stairs, and aches after sitting still for a while. The cartilage coating the ends of the bones in a joint is a smooth cushion: it absorbs shock and lets bones glide over each other without friction. But the problem is that adult human cartilage barely heals on its own once it is damaged, and as wear accumulates over time, many patients end up needing a knee replacement.

That is why a fairly old piece of science drew a fresh wave of attention: a study published in 2024 showed that an injectable gel helped real cartilage regrow in the knees of sheep. The paper went viral again on social media in 2025 and 2026, until it looked to some readers like a brand-new treatment ready to use. So what actually happened in the laboratory? And where does this technology stand today?

What is this gel? "Dancing molecules" and a rubbery scaffold

The material, developed by a team at Northwestern University led by Professor Samuel I. Stupp, is an injectable bioactive hydrogel made of supramolecular peptide nanofibres and circular proteins that mimic TGF-β1, a growth factor that drives cartilage formation, together with modified hyaluronic acid, a substance found naturally in joints. The team calls its molecules "dancing molecules", a name that refers to how the molecules move within the scaffold.

When the gel is injected into a cartilage defect, it forms a rubbery scaffold that fills the gap and gives cells something to hold on to, instead of leaving the defect empty or letting it fill with weak scar-like tissue.

What happened inside the sheep's knee?

The researchers tested the gel in the sheep stifle joint, a weight-bearing joint similar in function to the human knee. There, the body's own stem cells infiltrated the scaffold and began producing collagen II and proteoglycans, the two main building blocks of healthy cartilage.

Within 6 months, the scaffold had biodegraded as genuine hyaline cartilage filled the defect — the smooth, shock-absorbing kind that covers healthy joints, not the weaker fibrocartilage that usually forms after injury and resembles scar tissue. The results were published in the journal PNAS on 6 August 2024, in work with the Mark Markel lab at the University of Wisconsin–Madison School of Veterinary Medicine.

Why does this matter for people with knee osteoarthritis?

Knee osteoarthritis is one of the most common causes of chronic pain as people age, and today's treatments ease pain and inflammation or replace the whole joint at an advanced stage — but they do not rebuild damaged cartilage. So any technique that regrows real cartilage inside the joint, without major surgery, would be a major shift if it ever worked in people.

The crucial point: this is an animal study only

And here we must stop and be plain: everything published so far is an animal study only, in sheep, and there are no human trials of this gel yet. Success in a sheep's knee does not mean success in a human knee, and we do not yet know whether the gel is safe or effective in people, nor what dose or injection method would suit them.

The wide sharing of the story on social media does not change that fact: the paper is from 2024, and the science remains preclinical. Anyone reading the news today should read it as an early research promise, not as an available treatment.

Tip

If you live with chronic knee pain or osteoarthritis, do not look for this gel outside clinical trials — it is not available to patients at all, and any offer of it outside a trusted research setting should be treated with caution. The right step today: see your doctor to assess your condition, stick to your prescribed treatment, keep a healthy weight, and practise thigh-strengthening exercises regularly — they lighten the load on the knee. And if you take any medicine, do not stop it or replace it on your own — consult your doctor first.

The bottom line: promising science, and a long road

The "dancing molecules" gel achieved in sheep what medicine has long failed to do: regrowing genuine hyaline cartilage where it was damaged within six months, with a scaffold that biodegrades once its job is done. But it remains a laboratory promise, not a treatment: between a sheep's knee and a human knee lie years of safety and effectiveness trials, then regulatory approvals. As for today, the practical message does not change: protecting the knee starts with a healthy weight, strong muscles and prescribed treatment — not with waiting for a story that went viral on social media.
