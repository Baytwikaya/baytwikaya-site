Chronic loneliness raises dementia risk by 38%: occasional loneliness does not

Share this article:

Loneliness is not just a feeling — it may be a risk factor for the brain

Loneliness is common in old age: friends pass away, children move away, and the daily routine narrows little by little. But a question has long puzzled researchers: does loneliness actually raise the risk of dementia, or is it merely an early consequence of dementia itself, as patients gradually withdraw before the disease shows?

A new French study from the Université de Bordeaux, published on 18 November 2026 in the journal Aging & Mental Health, tried to answer with a very long follow-up: 3,777 people aged 65 and over from the Paquid cohort, followed from 1988 to 2018 — thirty years.

The results: the difference between chronic and occasional

The researchers grouped participants by how lonely they felt at several visits over 8 years: those who never felt lonely, those who felt lonely sometimes, and those who felt lonely persistently — at 3 or 4 visits out of 4.

And the result was clear: among people with chronic loneliness, 49.1% developed dementia, compared with 33.1% of those who never felt lonely — a 38% higher risk. Occasional loneliness, by contrast, showed no significant association with higher risk. So the pattern is what matters: not a lonely moment, but years of persistent loneliness.

Why might chronic loneliness affect the brain?

The study does not give a definitive answer about the mechanism, but the researchers suggest several pathways: less social and mental stimulation, chronic stress and the inflammation that comes with it, depression, less healthy habits — from little movement to poor diet — and delayed help-seeking when a person lives alone.

Study limits: read the 38% figure with care

First, the study is observational: an association is not proof of causation, and it cannot rule out that early dementia — before diagnosis — is what pushed some people into isolation. Second, loneliness was measured with a single question, a rough measure of complex feelings. Third, the chronic-loneliness group is small — just 267 people — and the confidence interval around the figure is wide. So the message is not that every lonely person will develop dementia, but that persistent loneliness deserves to be taken seriously as a health factor.

Tip

If you have an older relative who has lived alone for years, regular contact with them is not a courtesy — it is health: a weekly call or visit, a group activity in the neighbourhood, at the mosque or a club, volunteering, or a shared hobby. And if they seem persistently withdrawn or their memory is slipping, encourage them to see a doctor — chronic loneliness is a health problem, not a personal failing.

The bottom line: persistence is the risk, not the moment

After thirty years of follow-up, the French study says chronic loneliness — not occasional loneliness — was linked to a 38% higher risk of dementia. And the figure needs care: the study is observational, loneliness was measured with a single question, and the chronic group is small. But the practical message is clear: regular social relationships are part of brain health, and keeping them up in old age is an investment no less important than food and movement.
