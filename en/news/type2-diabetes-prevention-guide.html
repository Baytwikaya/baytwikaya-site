Preventing Type 2 Diabetes: The Complete Guide to Protecting Your Health

Share this article:

What is type 2 diabetes? And why is prevention possible?

Type 2 diabetes is a chronic disease that happens when the body cannot use insulin well, or when the pancreas does not produce enough of it, so the level of sugar in the blood rises and stays high. Insulin is a hormone that helps sugar move from the blood into the cells to be used as energy, and when its effectiveness falls, which is called insulin resistance, sugar accumulates in the blood and harms blood vessels and nerves over time. Type 2 diabetes differs from type 1 diabetes, which happens when the pancreas stops producing insulin completely, a disease that cannot be prevented by lifestyle.

The good news is that type 2 diabetes can be prevented or its appearance delayed in many cases, because its most important risk factors are linked to lifestyle: excess weight, lack of movement, unbalanced food, smoking and lack of sleep. Large studies have shown that changing lifestyle, especially losing excess weight, daily movement and balanced food, greatly reduces the risk of diabetes in people at risk. This guide explains the disease, its risk factors and its signs, which practical steps protect against it, and when to have a test and consult a doctor.

How does the disease develop? Risk factors

Type 2 diabetes develops gradually over years, and it is often preceded by a stage called prediabetes, in which the level of sugar in the blood is higher than normal but has not yet reached the level for a diagnosis of diabetes. At this stage many people feel no symptoms, so the disease is discovered late in many people. Risk factors include excess weight, especially around the abdomen, lack of physical activity, having a first-degree relative with diabetes, age over forty, high blood pressure or high cholesterol, previous gestational diabetes, smoking, lack of sleep and chronic stress.

It is important to know that a genetic factor does not mean that the disease is inevitable; it means that prevention is more important. A person with a family history of diabetes can greatly reduce his risk with a healthy lifestyle, while a person without a family history may develop diabetes if his lifestyle is unhealthy. Fat around the abdomen is also more dangerous than general obesity, because fat around the internal organs increases insulin resistance. Measuring waist circumference and following weight regularly, as well as regular blood sugar tests, are therefore among the most important tools of prevention, especially after the age of forty or when other risk factors are present.

Signs to watch for

Type 2 diabetes may cause no symptoms at first, and this is what makes it dangerous, because silent high blood sugar harms blood vessels, nerves, eyes and kidneys without a person feeling it. When symptoms appear, they are often: intense thirst and frequent urination, especially at night, constant hunger despite eating, unexplained tiredness, weight loss without a cause, blurred vision, slow healing of wounds, repeated infections, especially skin and urinary infections, and numbness or tingling in the hands or feet.

If these symptoms appear, a blood sugar test should be done and a doctor consulted without delay, because early diagnosis prevents complications or delays them. Blood sugar should also be tested regularly even without symptoms, especially for people with risk factors, such as excess weight, a family history, high blood pressure or previous gestational diabetes. The doctor decides the right type of test, whether fasting sugar, glycated haemoglobin or a glucose tolerance test, and decides how often to repeat the test according to the result. Remember that prediabetes is a stage that can often be reversed by changing lifestyle, so early testing is an opportunity, not just a diagnosis.

Balanced food: the basis of prevention

Balanced food is the basis of preventing type 2 diabetes, and it does not mean harsh deprivation, but balance and moderation. Food should be rich in vegetables, fruit, whole grains and legumes such as lentils, chickpeas and beans, fish, olive oil and nuts in moderation, with fewer sugars and sweetened drinks, including juices, less white bread, white rice and pastries, processed meat, fried foods and saturated fats. Sugars and sweetened drinks raise blood sugar quickly and increase weight, while fibre from vegetables and whole grains slows the absorption of sugar and helps with fullness.

In Morocco, balanced home cooking can be relied on: tagines rich in vegetables and legumes, salads, grilled fish and green tea without sugar or with little sugar, with fewer sweets, soft drinks and large amounts of white bread. Good practical habits include eating slowly, filling half the plate with vegetables, drinking water instead of sweetened drinks, avoiding eating in front of screens, and eating regular meals without skipping meals. There is no need for a harsh diet or one system that suits everyone, only small, steady changes that can be continued, and the doctor or dietitian should be consulted when a tailored plan is needed.

Movement and weight: two steps that protect a lot

Daily movement is one of the strongest tools for preventing diabetes, because it helps the muscles use sugar in the blood without needing a large amount of insulin, improves the body's sensitivity to insulin, and helps control weight, blood pressure and cholesterol. The World Health Organization recommends that adults practise moderate physical activity, such as brisk walking, for at least two and a half hours a week, as well as muscle-strengthening exercises twice a week. Exercise does not need to be in a gym; daily walking, climbing stairs and active housework are enough, with less long sitting in front of screens.

As for weight, losing excess weight gradually, even by a small amount, improves insulin sensitivity and noticeably reduces the risk of diabetes in people at risk. Losing weight should preferably be gradual and healthy, through balanced food and daily movement, not through harsh diets or medicines without medical advice. Weight and waist circumference should be followed regularly, a realistic goal should be set with the doctor, and small progress should be celebrated, because every small step towards a healthy weight is a step towards prevention. If weight is very high, the doctor should be consulted to discuss the right options, including food, exercise and medical support.

Tip

Walk every day, replace sweetened drinks with water, and fill half your plate with vegetables; these three simple steps greatly reduce the risk of diabetes.

Sleep, stress and smoking: factors many people overlook

Lack of sleep increases the risk of diabetes, because it affects the hormones of hunger and fullness, increases insulin resistance, and pushes a person to eat food rich in sugar and fat. Regular sleep at night for seven to nine hours for adults, with a fixed time for sleeping and waking, is therefore an important part of prevention. Chronic stress also raises the hormone cortisol, which raises blood sugar and increases the desire for unhealthy food, so managing stress through sleep, movement, breathing and supportive relationships also helps prevent diabetes.

Smoking increases the risk of diabetes and heart and blood vessel disease, and quitting improves health quickly, with help to quit available from the doctor. Drinking too much alcohol also increases weight, raises blood sugar and affects the liver, so it should be avoided or reduced as much as possible. Remember that preventing diabetes is not one step, but a group of habits that work together: balanced food, daily movement, good sleep, quitting smoking and managing stress, and each habit supports the others.

Testing and follow-up: when and how?

Regular blood sugar testing is the only way to detect prediabetes and diabetes early, before symptoms or complications appear. Health bodies recommend testing for adults with excess weight or other risk factors, starting from the age of thirty-five or forty, and repeating it every three years if the result is normal, or more often if the result is in the prediabetes range. The doctor decides the time and type of test suitable for each person, according to age, weight, family history and health condition.

If prediabetes is diagnosed, it does not mean that diabetes is inevitable; it is an early warning and an opportunity to change. At this stage, changing lifestyle is most effective, and the doctor may recommend an organised programme of food and movement, and regular follow-up of blood sugar, weight, blood pressure and cholesterol. If diabetes is diagnosed, early treatment and regular follow-up prevent complications or delay them, and treatment includes food, movement and medicines according to the case, and regular checks of the eyes, kidneys and feet. Do not hesitate to ask your doctor about the right test for you and your next appointment.

The bottom line: prevention is possible and starts today

Type 2 diabetes is a common and serious disease, but preventing it is possible in many cases, and it starts with simple daily habits: balanced food rich in vegetables, whole grains and legumes, a daily walk and regular movement, a healthy weight, good sleep, quitting smoking, managing stress, and regular blood sugar tests. There is no need for radical changes all at once, only small, steady steps: replacing a sweetened drink with water, walking after dinner, an extra plate of vegetables, and a fixed bedtime. Start today with one step, keep it up, and ask your doctor about the right test for you, because prevention is better than cure, and your health deserves care and follow-up.
