Sardines and Omega-3: The Complete Guide

Share this article:

Sardines: a small fish with big benefits

Sardines are among the most widespread fish in Morocco, present in coastal and inland markets, and sold fresh at prices most families can afford. Despite their small size and low price, sardines are among the richest fish in omega-3 fatty acids, protein, vitamin D and calcium, when eaten with their soft bones. In this guide we explain what omega-3 is and why it matters for your health, what makes sardines an excellent choice compared with other fish, how to choose and prepare them, and who should be careful when eating them. The goal is to get the best from this Moroccan marine treasure, and to make fish a regular part of your weekly meals without complication or great cost.

What are omega-3 fatty acids?

Omega-3 is a family of fatty acids that the body needs and cannot produce in sufficient amounts on its own, so they must come from food. The best known types in oily fish are eicosapentaenoic acid and docosahexaenoic acid, the two types whose benefits scientists study most. Omega-3 is also found in plant sources such as flaxseed and walnuts, but the body converts them to the marine types with limited efficiency, so oily fish remains the best source. Omega-3 enters into the building of cell membranes, plays a role in the health of the heart, brain and eyes, and takes part in regulating the inflammatory response in the body. This does not mean that fish oil supplements are a replacement for fish itself, because whole fish also gives protein, vitamins and minerals, while supplements give only the fats. Health authorities generally recommend eating fish, especially oily fish, twice a week as part of a balanced dietary pattern.

Why sardines in particular?

Sardines are distinctive because they are small fish that live a short time, so mercury accumulates in them in far smaller amounts than in large long-lived fish such as large tuna and swordfish, and this makes them a relatively safe choice for regular eating. Sardines are also usually eaten whole, with their soft bones after cooking, so they give calcium that supports bone health, in addition to vitamin D, which helps the body absorb calcium. They are also rich in complete protein, which the body needs to build and repair muscles, in vitamin B12, essential for nerves and blood formation, and in selenium and iodine. Their low price makes them accessible to everyone, and they are available in fishing seasons in coastal cities such as Agadir, Essaouira and Casablanca, and reach inland cities fresh or canned. Sardines canned in olive oil or water are a good choice when fresh fish is not available, but pay attention to the amount of added salt in some canned types, and choose the less salty types when you can.

Sardines and the health of the heart and brain

Research suggests that eating oily fish regularly is linked to better heart health, because omega-3 helps lower the level of triglycerides in the blood, one of the risk factors for heart disease, and contributes to regulating heart rhythm and blood pressure within a balanced dietary pattern. The brain also contains a high proportion of fats, and omega-3 is an important part of its structure, so scientists study its relationship with memory and concentration as we age. But it is important to be precise: sardines are not a medicine, and they do not prevent disease alone; their benefit appears with a balanced diet, daily movement, avoiding smoking, and controlling blood pressure, sugar and cholesterol. Someone who eats sardines fried in oil used several times, with a lot of bread and a lot of salt, will not get the same benefit as someone who eats them grilled with vegetables and olive oil. The complete dietary context therefore remains more important than a single ingredient, even when that ingredient is excellent.

How to choose and prepare sardines

When buying fresh sardines, pay attention to the following signs: bright clear eyes, red gills, a firm body that leaves no mark when pressed, and a light sea smell rather than a strong unpleasant one. Buy from a seller you trust, eat the fish on the day you buy it, and keep it in the fridge if you must delay it for a few hours. As for preparation, grilling over charcoal or in the oven is one of the best methods, because it preserves omega-3 without adding a lot of fat. Tagine with chermoula, a Moroccan marinade of garlic, cumin, paprika and lemon, is a delicious and healthy method if you reduce the oil and salt. Frying is the least beneficial method, especially if the oil is used or very hot, because it adds fats and damages part of the benefits. If you choose canned sardines, drain the excess oil, serve them with whole bread, salad and lemon, and avoid very salty types if you have high blood pressure.

Who should be careful?

Sardines are safe for most people when eaten in moderation, but there are situations that call for attention. Pregnant and breastfeeding women benefit from omega-3 for the development of the baby's brain, but they should avoid fish rich in mercury; sardines are among the relatively low-mercury fish, yet a pregnant woman should still ask her doctor about the amount suitable for her. People who take blood-thinning medicines should tell their doctor before taking high-dose fish oil supplements, because omega-3 can affect clotting, while fish in ordinary meals is different from concentrated supplements. Anyone with a fish allergy must avoid sardines completely. People with gout should note that oily fish contain purines, so they should ask their doctor about the right amount. Always remember that supplements are not a replacement for food, and do not take high-dose omega-3 supplements without consulting a doctor or pharmacist, especially if you take other medicines.

Sardines in Moroccan cooking: practical recipes

In Morocco, sardines have many beloved recipes: sardines grilled over charcoal with bread and salad, sardines with chermoula in a tagine, sardine balls, and fried sardines, which are best kept to a minimum. For a healthy week, try this pattern: one day for grilled sardines with Moroccan salad and whole bread, and another day for white fish or legumes, so you get variety without boredom. You can prepare chermoula at home easily: garlic, cumin, paprika, lemon, a little olive oil and herbs, then marinate the sardines for an hour before grilling. Always serve fish with vegetables, whether a tomato and onion salad or grilled vegetables, because vegetables complete the meal with fibre and vitamins. In the markets, sardines are sold at reasonable prices in season, so you can buy a quantity for the family and freeze part of it, taking care to freeze quickly and eat it within a few weeks. If you live in an inland city far from the coast, good canned sardines are a practical and useful choice, especially with whole bread and salad.

Tip

Eat grilled sardines or sardines in a tagine twice a week, buy fresh fish from a seller you trust, and eat it on the day you buy it.

The bottom line: a small fish, a big benefit, at an affordable price

Sardines are among the best that the Moroccan sea offers: rich in omega-3, protein, vitamin D and calcium, relatively low in mercury, and available at a reasonable price. Eat them twice a week, grilled or in a tagine, with vegetables and olive oil, and you will give your heart, brain and bones real support within a balanced dietary pattern. If you have a particular health condition or take blood-thinning medicines, consult your doctor before changing your diet or taking supplements.
