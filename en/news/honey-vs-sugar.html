Honey or sugar: which is better for your health? What an analysis of 18 clinical trials found

Share this article:

A spoonful of honey or a spoonful of sugar? An older question than you think

He stirs a spoonful of honey into his tea instead of sugar, and feels he has made the healthy choice. Honey is natural, it comes from bees, and it carries an old reputation as both food and medicine. But is that reputation scientifically true? Or is honey, in the end, just another sugar in a golden colour? A simple question, answered by a scientific analysis that pooled 18 clinical trials — so what did it find?

The analysis comes from the University of Toronto, published in the journal Nutrition Reviews in 2022, and it remains the most complete systematic review comparing honey with sugar directly.

What did the Toronto researchers do?

The researchers gathered 18 controlled clinical trials, covering 33 comparisons and 1,105 participants. They compared eating honey — at a median dose of 40 grams a day, about two tablespoons, for a median of 8 weeks — with a usual diet, sucrose, or high-fructose corn syrup. They then measured its effect on fasting blood sugar, cholesterol, triglycerides and liver function.

What did the analysis find? Small improvements across several markers

The results favoured honey, but by small margins: fasting blood sugar fell by 0.20 mmol per litre, total cholesterol by 0.18, LDL cholesterol by 0.16, triglycerides by 0.13, and the liver enzyme ALT by 9.75 units per litre. HDL, the protective cholesterol, rose by 0.07 mmol per litre — the only result whose certainty was rated high.

And here comes the most important caveat: the certainty of the evidence for most outcomes was low, which means the true figures could differ a lot from these estimates once larger and more precise studies appear. The improvement is there on paper, but it is small and not certain enough to build a strong recommendation on.

Raw and single-flower honey did best

Not all honeys were equal: raw honey and monofloral honey — such as robinia (acacia) and clover honey — showed the most benefit, while processed or mixed honey had less effect. The likely explanation is that raw honey keeps plant compounds with antioxidant effects that are partly lost through processing and heating, though that explanation still needs confirmation.

But remember: honey is sugar too

Before celebrating the results, remember the basic fact: honey is about 80% sugar, and one tablespoon of it contains 64 calories against 49 in a tablespoon of sugar. The World Health Organization counts honey as a free sugar, and advises keeping free sugars below 10% of daily energy — ideally below 5%. Honey is not a free food with no counting: it is a sugar that counts within your daily share.

A small Iranian trial is a reminder of the same context: 55 overweight or obese patients took 70 grams of honey a day versus sucrose for 30 days, and the honey group lost 1.3% of body weight and 1.1% of body fat, while cholesterol fell 3%, triglycerides 11%, fasting blood sugar 4.2%, and protective cholesterol rose 3.3%. Nice figures, but the trial was small, brief and unblinded, from a single source — so do not base a decision on it.

Replacement, not addition: that is the rule

The practical conclusion of all this is not “eat honey”, but “replace with honey the sugar you already eat”. If you sweeten your tea with two spoons of sugar, swapping them for two spoons of honey may improve your markers slightly — but adding honey on top of the sugar you already eat helps you in no way at all; it only adds calories and sugar. The difference between replacement and addition is the whole difference.

People with diabetes should treat honey exactly like sugar: count it within the day’s carbohydrates and ask their doctor before any change. And honey must never be given to children under one year old, because of the risk of botulism.

Tip

If you want to use what the studies found, swap sugar for honey in what you already eat — one spoon in place of one spoon, not one spoon on top of another — and choose raw honey if you can, remembering that the benefit is small and its certainty low. And do not forget that cutting down sugar altogether, honey or sugar, is the step that matters most for your health.

The bottom line: a slightly better swap, not a magic food

An analysis of 18 trials says honey is slightly better than sugar — fasting sugar, cholesterol and triglycerides a little lower, protective cholesterol a little higher — but the differences are small, and the certainty is low except for protective cholesterol. And honey remains sugar: 80% sugar, with more calories than sugar. So swap it for your sugar if you like its taste, do not add it on top, and do not expect a miracle from it.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the results

  • 18 controlled clinical trials, 33 comparisons, 1,105 participants; median dose 40 g a day (about two tablespoons) for a median of 8 weeks.
  • Fasting blood sugar: −0.20 mmol/L (95% confidence interval −0.37 to −0.04, low certainty).
  • Total cholesterol −0.18, LDL cholesterol −0.16, triglycerides −0.13 mmol/L; liver enzyme ALT −9.75 U/L.
  • Protective cholesterol HDL: +0.07 mmol/L — the only result with high certainty.
  • Raw honey and monofloral honey (robinia/acacia and clover) showed the most benefit; processed or mixed honey less so.
  • Honey is about 80% sugar; one tablespoon: 64 calories versus 49 for sugar; the WHO advises free sugars below 10% of daily energy, ideally below 5%.
  • Iranian trial: 55 patients, 70 g a day, 30 days: weight −1.3%, body fat −1.1%, cholesterol −3%, triglycerides −11%, fasting sugar −4.2%, protective cholesterol +3.3% — a small, brief, unblinded trial.
  • No honey for children under one year (botulism risk); people with diabetes should count honey as sugar and ask their doctor.
