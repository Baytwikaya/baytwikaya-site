A pacemaker that follows the heart's natural wiring: what the OptimPacing trial found

Share this article:

When the heart stops sending its signals on time

A healthy heart beats in a steady rhythm because a small electrical signal starts at the top of the heart and travels through a fine network of fibres to the ventricles, which then contract together in perfect coordination. But in some people this pathway breaks down — a condition called atrioventricular block, or heart block — so the signal arrives late or never arrives at all, the pulse slows, and the patient feels dizzy and tired, and may faint. The solution known for decades is a permanent pacemaker, which sends electrical impulses in place of the broken signal.

But one question has remained open for years: where should the pacemaker's wire go? The traditional method sends the impulse directly into the muscle of the right ventricle, so the ventricles contract out of their natural order. The newer method places the wire on the left bundle branch — on the heart's own natural electrical pathway — so the heart contracts as it was designed to. A new international trial, presented at the American Heart Association's Scientific Sessions in November 2026 and published in the New England Journal of Medicine the same day, compared the two approaches head to head — and the result was striking.

What is the difference between right ventricular pacing and left bundle branch pacing?

Traditional right ventricular pacing has been the standard for decades: a wire is fixed at the tip of the right ventricle, and its impulse spreads slowly through the heart muscle from cell to cell, so the right ventricle contracts first and then the left — the reverse of the natural order. For many patients this causes no problem at all, but for some — especially those who need pacing most of the time — years of uncoordinated contraction can weaken the heart muscle, a condition known as pacing-induced cardiomyopathy.

Left bundle branch pacing places the wire in the wall between the ventricles, directly on the left bundle branch, so the signal travels through the natural network itself and both ventricles contract together, as in a healthy heart. The logic is simple: instead of imposing a new rhythm on the heart, restart its original one. In the trial, doctors succeeded in placing the wire in the correct position in 96.8% of cases — a high success rate for this technique.

What did the OptimPacing trial find?

The trial enrolled 702 patients with heart block who needed a permanent pacemaker, whose ejection fraction — the heart's pumping efficiency — was above 35%, with a mean age of 68.4 years, 57.5% men, across 17 centres in China, Hong Kong, the UK, Poland and the United States. Patients were randomised: 342 to left bundle branch pacing and 343 to right ventricular pacing in the final analysis, which covered 685 patients.

The main outcome the trial measured was a composite: death from any cause, hospitalisation for heart failure, or pacing-induced cardiomyopathy. After a median follow-up of 35.1 months — about three years — there were 16 events in the left bundle branch group versus 35 in the right ventricular group. Counting the cumulative incidence over four years, the rates were 8.5% versus 19.2% — a 59% lower risk of complications (hazard ratio 0.41, 95% confidence interval 0.23–0.75, P=0.003).

The strongest result: zero cases of pacing-induced cardiomyopathy

When the composite outcome was broken into its parts, the clearest difference appeared in pacing-induced cardiomyopathy itself: 0% in the left bundle branch group versus 11.7% in the right ventricular group. In other words, more than one in ten patients in the traditional group developed heart-muscle weakness caused by the pacing itself, while nobody in the other group did. Hospitalisation for heart failure was 1.5% versus 2.9%, and deaths from any cause were similar: 7.4% versus 7.7%, with no meaningful difference between the groups.

The trial's lead author, Professor Jiangang Zou of Nanjing Medical University, said that not all pacing options are the same, and that people who need a pacemaker should talk with their healthcare team about which pacing approach is appropriate. It is an invitation to discuss the options with your doctor — not to request one technique by name.

Read the results carefully: early stopping and an open-label design

This is where the difference lies between a good article and one that exaggerates. First, the trial was stopped early because it showed a clear benefit at a planned interim analysis, after only 51 events. Stopping early in favour of a positive result is known to possibly overestimate the true size of the benefit, because early results may look better than the final results would have been if the trial had continued. Second, the trial was open-label: patients and doctors knew which method had been used, which can influence some decisions and assessments, even though the main outcomes were objective. Third, most patients had a normal ejection fraction, so we do not yet know whether the same benefit applies to people who already have a weakened heart muscle. The results are strong and encouraging — but they are not the final word.

Tip

If you need a pacemaker, or already have one, ask your cardiologist about the pacing method that suits your case, how much of the time the pacemaker will be working, and when your follow-up checks are due. Do not request a specific technique from the internet: where the wire goes depends on your heart's anatomy, your condition and the team's experience — it is a medical decision shared between you and your doctor. Keep your pacemaker check appointments, and tell your doctor promptly if you feel dizzy, short of breath, notice swollen feet or unusual tiredness.

The bottom line: a gentler way to pace the heart, awaiting confirmation

The OptimPacing trial is among the most important cardiology news of 2026: pacing that follows the heart's natural wiring cut pacemaker complications by 59%, with no cases of pacing-induced cardiomyopathy, versus more than one in ten patients with the traditional method. But the trial stopped early after only 51 events, it was open-label, and most of its patients had a normal ejection fraction. So the benefit looks real and large, while caution is warranted about its final size — and the practical message today is this: if you need a pacemaker, talk with your healthcare team about which pacing approach is appropriate for you.
