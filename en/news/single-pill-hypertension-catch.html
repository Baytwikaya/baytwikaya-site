One pill for blood pressure instead of three: how a new trial more than doubled blood-pressure control

Share this article:

Three pills every morning — and blood pressure still high

He wakes up every morning to three different blood-pressure pills, takes them faithfully, then measures his pressure and finds the top number still above 140. This is not an exception — it is the situation of millions of patients around the world. High blood pressure is one of the most common diseases, yet it remains uncontrolled in most people who have it, because treatment is complicated, doses are forgotten, and follow-up breaks down.

A new large trial, published in JAMA online on 8 November 2026 and presented at the American Heart Association meeting, tried a solution that looks simple: one pill combining the blood-pressure drugs, together with team-based care, home measurement and patient coaching. Did this simple package change anything? The numbers say yes — and strongly.

What is the CATCH trial?

The CATCH trial is a cluster-randomised trial, meaning the randomisation was at the level of clinics rather than individuals: it covered 40 primary-care clinics in Colombia and Jamaica, and 1,706 adults with uncontrolled hypertension, with a mean age of 61.4 years. 69.9% were women, and 98.2% were already taking blood-pressure medicines — so the problem was not the absence of treatment, but that it was not enough.

In the clinics assigned to the intervention, patients received a single pill combining two or three blood-pressure drugs: valsartan with amlodipine, or valsartan with amlodipine and hydrochlorothiazide; in Jamaica, women of reproductive age received indapamide with amlodipine. And the pill did not come alone: it was accompanied by team-based care from a health team, home blood-pressure measurement, and coaching to help patients understand their disease and treatment. The other clinics continued with usual care.

The result: a bigger drop, and control more than doubled

After 18 months, in the primary analysis covering 1,574 patients, systolic pressure — the top number — fell by 19.1 mmHg in the intervention group, from 145.9 to 126.8, compared with a fall of 9.0 mmHg with usual care, from 145.0 to 136.1. The difference between the groups was 10.1 mmHg in favour of the intervention (95% confidence interval 6.9 to 13.3).

More important than the average fall: the share of patients who reached a pressure below 130/80 — the accepted control target — was 69.5% in the intervention group, versus only 29.2% with usual care. Control more than doubled. Serious adverse events were similar in the two groups: 7.9% versus 9.9%.

Why did the package work — and why we do not know which part did it?

The logic behind the single pill is clear: one pill a day is easier than three, so patients forget it less and stick to it more, and combining drugs that work through different mechanisms lowers pressure more strongly than a single drug at a high dose. Home measurement and coaching, meanwhile, turn the patient into a partner in treatment rather than a passive recipient, and help the health team adjust treatment quickly instead of waiting months for a visit.

But here comes the most important caveat: the pill was never tested on its own. The trial compared a complete package — one pill plus team care, home measurement and coaching — with usual care, so we do not know how much of the benefit came from the pill itself and how much from the follow-up and coaching. The trial was also open-label, meaning patients and doctors knew who was receiving the intervention, which can influence results. Finally, the trial was run in Colombia and Jamaica, so its results may not apply to the same extent everywhere.

Tip

If you take several pills for blood pressure and your pressure is still high, ask your doctor whether your treatment could be simplified into a single combination pill, and about measuring your pressure at home with a reliable device and recording the results to show them. Do not stop any medicine or switch on your own, and do not buy a combination pill by yourself — the choice of drugs and doses differs from one patient to another and needs a medical decision. Alongside the medicine, the basics stay the same: cut down salt, move every day, keep a healthy weight, and measure your pressure regularly.

The bottom line: simplifying treatment may beat adding a new drug

The CATCH trial does not offer a new drug, but a practical idea: when treatment becomes simpler — one pill instead of three — with a team that follows the patient, measures pressure at home and coaches them, the share of patients with controlled pressure more than doubles, and systolic pressure falls by more than 10 mmHg extra. It is true that the pill was never tested alone, and that the results come from Colombia and Jamaica, but the message reaches beyond those two countries: the biggest obstacle to controlling blood pressure is not always the lack of a stronger drug, but the complexity of the treatment itself. And simplification, when paired with good follow-up, may be the strongest treatment of all.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the results

  • CATCH cluster-randomised trial: 40 primary-care clinics in Colombia and Jamaica; 1,706 adults with uncontrolled hypertension; mean age 61.4 years; 69.9% women; 98.2% already on antihypertensives.
  • Intervention: a single-pill combination (valsartan with amlodipine, or with hydrochlorothiazide; in Jamaica, indapamide with amlodipine for women of reproductive age) plus team-based care, home monitoring and coaching.
  • After 18 months (primary analysis: 1,574 patients): systolic pressure fell by 19.1 mmHg (from 145.9 to 126.8) versus 9.0 mmHg with usual care (from 145.0 to 136.1); difference 10.1 mmHg (95% CI 6.9 to 13.3).
  • Blood-pressure control (below 130/80): 69.5% in the intervention group versus 29.2% with usual care.
  • Serious adverse events were similar: 7.9% versus 9.9%.
  • Caveats: open-label trial; the pill was never tested alone, only as part of a package; results from Colombia and Jamaica may not generalise everywhere.
