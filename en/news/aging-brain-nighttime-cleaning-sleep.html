The Ageing Brain Cleans Itself More Slowly During Sleep

Share this article:

What did the MIT researchers discover?

Researchers at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology have discovered that the brain's cleaning process during sleep weakens substantially with age. During deep sleep, waves of cerebrospinal fluid, the fluid that surrounds the brain and carries waste and harmful substances away from it, flow through the brain, but these waves were much smaller in older adults than in younger ones. The study was published on 13 November 2026 in Nature Aging, led by Professor Laura Lewis, with Sydney Bailes and Stephanie Williams as lead authors. It involved 72 healthy volunteers: 31 younger adults aged 18 to 39, and 41 older adults aged 60 to 83. In nighttime sessions lasting about two hours and starting around midnight, participants slept inside an MRI scanner while the researchers simultaneously recorded the brain's electrical activity, blood flow and cerebrospinal fluid movement, a technically demanding feat never before achieved in this form during natural nighttime sleep.

When the younger adults entered non-REM sleep, their slow brain waves grew stronger, and their cerebrospinal fluid waves grew larger along with them. In older adults, the sleep-related rise in fluid-wave power was far smaller: the average increase was 3.82 decibels in younger adults versus only 1.15 decibels in older adults, a statistically significant difference, and older adults in fact showed no significant increase from wakefulness to sleep at all. This means the nightly cleaning mechanism still works, but less efficiently with age, even before any neurological disease appears.

How does the brain clean itself during sleep?

Brain cleaning depends on a precise coordination of three elements: the slow brain waves that appear in deep sleep, oscillations in the brain's blood flow, and waves of cerebrospinal fluid. When slow waves grow stronger, the volume of blood in the brain changes, pushing cerebrospinal fluid into and out of the brain through the fourth ventricle, carrying metabolic waste with it. The Lewis lab first discovered this mechanism in 2019, showing that slow waves generate cerebrospinal fluid movements during sleep. The process matters because the brain produces waste all day, some of it, such as amyloid protein, linked to Alzheimer's disease when it accumulates. In the new study, weaker fluid flow during sleep was linked to weaker frontal slow-wave power and to grey-matter atrophy, connecting brain ageing with weaker nightly cleaning. The researchers also found that some older adults kept youthful fluid waves, suggesting that ageing does not affect everyone to the same degree.

Why does cleaning weaken with age? Two factors, not one

The researchers did more than describe the problem: they investigated its cause with two daytime experiments. In the first, they showed participants a flickering checkerboard pattern to stimulate visual neural activity; in the second, they asked them to hold their breath to change blood flow in the brain without neural stimulation. In both experiments, older adults showed a weaker blood-flow response even though neural activity was similar in both groups, indicating that older adults' blood vessels have lost some of their flexibility and ability to respond to increased oxygen demand. This means the weaker cleaning comes from two factors together: weakened neurovascular coupling, the relationship between neural activity and blood flow, and a weaker vascular response itself. According to a statistical model, these two factors explained much of the decline in fluid flow, but not all of it, meaning other factors remain to be discovered. This finding matters because it identifies two potential targets for intervention: strengthening slow brain waves and improving blood-vessel health.

Can brain cleaning be improved?

This study tested no treatment, but it opens promising doors. A separate recent study showed that bursts of pink noise, a gentle sound similar to rain, increased cerebrospinal fluid movement in younger adults during sleep, but this has not yet been tested in older adults. Healthy sleep habits may also help: regular, sufficient sleep supports the deep sleep this mechanism depends on, while sleep deprivation weakens sleep quality and quantity. The Lewis team now wants to know whether weaker fluid flow raises the risk of neurodegenerative disease, and whether the sleep patterns they observed could help predict an individual's risk. Professor Lewis said: «We know that people age really differently. Some people age very successfully with great cognitive outcomes, and other people develop cognitive decline. If we can figure out who has great trajectories of aging and what are the factors that are associated with that, we might be able to use that to predict future outcomes.»

Tip

Protect your deep sleep the way you protect your heart: sleep enough hours at regular times, avoid caffeine in the evening and screens before bed, and treat snoring and sleep apnoea, because deep sleep is when the brain cleans itself most.

What does this mean for you?

This study does not prove that weaker brain cleaning causes dementia: it is a small study of 72 healthy people, it did not measure waste removal itself directly but fluid flow as a marker of it, and long-term follow-up is needed to know whether weaker flow truly predicts cognitive decline. But it offers a new scientific reason to care about sleep quality as we age, not just its quantity. If you are over 60 and wake often at night, snore loudly or feel sleepy during the day, talk to your doctor about a sleep assessment: you may have sleep apnoea, which is treatable and harms deep sleep. Keep regular sleep times, avoid alcohol and caffeine in the evening, and exercise regularly, since all of these support deep sleep and blood-vessel health together, the two factors the study found behind the brain's nightly cleaning.
