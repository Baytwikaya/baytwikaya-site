Could a blood test detect ovarian cancer years before a tumour appears?

Share this article:

Why is ovarian cancer so hard to catch early?

Ovarian cancer is among the most dangerous cancers affecting women, because it is usually found at an advanced stage, after the disease has already spread beyond the ovary. Its early symptoms are vague and non-specific, such as persistent bloating, pelvic pain and feeling full quickly, symptoms that can easily be mistaken for a passing digestive problem, so diagnosis is often delayed for months.

There is also, to this day, no reliable screening test for ovarian cancer in the general population. Ultrasound and the conventional blood test known as CA125 cannot detect the very first microscopic changes, and usually only reveal a tumour once it has grown large enough to be seen. That is why researchers have been searching for an earlier biological signal, one that appears before the tumour itself.

What is STIC, and where does it begin?

Surprisingly, the most aggressive form of ovarian cancer, high-grade serous ovarian cancer, often does not begin in the ovary itself, but in the fallopian tube. There a microscopic lesion forms, serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma (STIC), a group of abnormal, non-invasive cells too small for imaging or conventional blood tests to see.

STIC is usually discovered only by chance, during surgery to remove the fallopian tubes or ovaries for another reason. While these cells remain confined to the lining of the tube, they can be treated or removed before they turn into invasive cancer, but if they go unnoticed, they may develop over time into widespread ovarian cancer. Finding a way to detect this lesion at its earliest stage, through a simple blood test, matters enormously.

What is Tu-Stroma?

In a study published on 10 November 2026 in Nature Medicine, researchers at the Hoag Family Cancer Institute announced the first-ever discovery of a molecular warning signal in the blood that identifies STIC. The signal is an RNA molecule called Tu-Stroma, a long noncoding RNA found in extracellular vesicles in blood serum.

Tu-Stroma is linked to a fundamental process in cancer development: weakening neighbouring healthy cells to make room for cancer cells to grow. In their earliest stages, cancer cells compete with the healthy tissue around them and release signals that weaken it. By studying this early competition, the researchers identified a molecular warning signal that could reveal cancer development years before conventional tests can detect a tumour.

The study was led by Dr. Rajan Gogna, Director of Translational Research at the Hoag Family Cancer Institute, and Dr. Esha Madan, Principal Investigator and Group Leader of the Cell Competition in Tumor Microenvironment Laboratory at the same institute.

How well did the test perform?

The eight-year study was an international, multi-institutional effort drawing on scientists and clinical samples from several countries. The researchers measured Tu-Stroma levels in the blood serum of 506 participants: healthy women, women with benign lesions, women with isolated STIC, and women with high-grade serous ovarian cancer at stages I to IV.

In a separate group of women used to validate the findings, the test detected STIC in 92.3% of the women who had it and correctly identified 98.2% of healthy women. When the researchers compared Tu-Stroma with the conventional CA125 test in distinguishing STIC from healthy controls, the new molecule performed far better, with a discrimination accuracy of 0.99 compared with 0.59 for CA125.

When the researchers modelled how the test might perform in a high-risk population, they found that approximately 72% of positive results would be accurate, while a negative result would be correct nearly 100% of the time. This suggests the test could be especially useful for women at elevated risk, such as carriers of BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations.

What are the limits of these results?

Promising as these results are, they are still a first step, not a test ready for use. The study analysed blood samples collected previously from women whose diagnoses were already confirmed, so it was retrospective, and it does not yet prove that the test can predict cancer development in real time in women who have not been diagnosed.

The researchers are therefore planning prospective clinical studies that will collect blood samples from women in the local community, with and without elevated genetic risk, and follow participants over time to learn how early the signal can be detected, how reliably the test identifies women with precancerous disease, and whether it can help guide clinical decisions. No routine blood test exists today for this purpose, and no woman should delay her usual check-ups or care while waiting for it.

Tip

Pay attention to persistent symptoms such as ongoing bloating, pelvic pain, feeling full quickly and changes in urination, and see a doctor promptly if they last more than two weeks. If you have a family history of ovarian or breast cancer, or a known BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutation, discuss screening and prevention options with your doctor. Remember that this blood test is not yet available as a routine test, and it does not replace your usual check-ups and care.

Conclusion

The discovery of Tu-Stroma marks a shift in how we think about early detection: instead of looking for smaller tumours, researchers are now looking for the first molecular warning sign that precedes invasive cancer itself. If large prospective studies confirm these findings, this blood test could one day help monitor women at high risk, inform preventive decisions such as the timing of preventive surgery, and perhaps even stop invasive cancer from developing at all.

Until then, the most important practical message remains to pay attention to persistent symptoms, to see a doctor without delay, and to discuss screening and prevention options with a doctor when there is a family history or a known inherited mutation.
