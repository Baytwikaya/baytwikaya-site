Belly fat linked to Alzheimer proteins building up in the brain: what did the new study find?

Belly fat and the brain: what is the link?

Could where your body stores fat say something about the health of your brain years from now? A new study suggests the answer may be yes — and that belly fat in particular is linked to the build-up of the proteins associated with Alzheimer’s disease in the brain, even in people with no memory problems at all.

The study, presented on 23 November 2026 at the annual meeting of the Radiological Society of North America (RSNA), was carried out by a team from the Mallinckrodt Institute of Radiology at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis, led by researcher Mahsa Dowlati with Cyrus Raji, and included 92 people aged 40 to 60, all with no impairment of memory or thinking.

How was the study done?

The researchers used MRI scans to measure visceral fat — the deep fat around the internal organs — and subcutaneous fat, thigh muscle mass and the fat around it, and combined these measurements with body mass index (BMI), cholesterol and insulin resistance. They then used PET scans to measure the accumulation of the proteins amyloid and tau in the brain, the two proteins linked to Alzheimer’s disease. Amyloid plaques can appear in the brain years before symptoms, while tau tangles track the progression of the disease more closely.

Four different groups

The researchers divided the participants into four groups according to their body composition and metabolic health:

  • Group 1: high visceral and subcutaneous fat, with low “good” cholesterol (HDL) and high insulin resistance.
  • Group 4: larger thigh muscle, moderate visceral fat and less subcutaneous fat, with better metabolic markers — higher HDL and lower insulin resistance.
  • Groups 2 and 3: leaner than the others, with less fat and muscle and a lower BMI; group 3 had the better metabolic markers, with higher HDL.

Amyloid: highest in those with the most visceral fat

The leanest group (group 2) had the lowest amyloid build-up. After the researchers adjusted for vascular risk factors — small-vessel disease, diabetes and high blood pressure — only one difference kept its statistical significance: amyloid in group 1 was about 6.7 points higher than in group 2 on the study’s scale.

Markers of brain inflammation on MRI were also lower in the leaner groups.

Tau: the lean, metabolically healthy group came first

For tau, group 3 — the leanest with the best metabolic markers — had the lowest build-up, and the other groups were 5–12% higher, with the largest gap between groups 3 and 4.

Why BMI alone is not enough

The most important message of this study is that BMI alone does not tell the whole story: two people of the same weight can differ completely in where their fat sits, how much muscle they have and how healthy their metabolism is. And thinness alone does not protect — group 4, despite its larger muscles, had the highest tau build-up compared with group 3. What matters, then, is the full picture: where the fat is, how much muscle there is, and how the metabolism is working.

Advice

These results do not mean that losing weight prevents Alzheimer’s — the study did not prove that. But the well-known steps for heart and metabolic health remain the wise choice for everyone: regular physical activity, a balanced diet, enough sleep, and keeping blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol checked with your doctor. There is no evidence that any particular diet or supplement prevents Alzheimer’s, so beware of ready-made promises. And if you are worried about your memory or your heart health, talk to your doctor.

The bottom line: an early association that needs confirmation

This study links where body fat sits and how healthy the metabolism is, on the one hand, with the build-up of Alzheimer’s proteins in the brain, on the other, in middle-aged people with no symptoms. But it must be read with care: it shows an association, not a cause, its sample is small (92 people), and its results were presented at a conference and have not yet been published as a peer-reviewed paper. It does not prove that improving metabolism lowers amyloid or tau or prevents Alzheimer’s, and it cannot predict whether any individual will develop the disease. Larger, more diverse studies with long-term follow-up are needed before any final conclusion.
