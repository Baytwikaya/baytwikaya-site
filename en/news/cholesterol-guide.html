Cholesterol: The Complete Guide

Share this article:

What is cholesterol and why do we hear about it so often?

Cholesterol is a fatty substance found in the blood and in every cell of the body, and it is necessary for building cell membranes and producing some hormones and vitamins. The liver makes most of the cholesterol the body needs, and part of it comes from food, especially animal products. The problem is not the presence of cholesterol itself, but a high level in the blood, especially one particular type, because it can build up in the walls of the arteries, narrow them, and increase the risk of heart disease and stroke. Because high cholesterol usually causes no symptoms, many people do not know their level is high until they have a blood test or develop complications. In this guide we explain the types of cholesterol, how it is measured, what raises it, what helps to lower it, and when medicine is necessary.

Types of cholesterol: the good and the bad

Cholesterol travels in the blood inside particles called lipoproteins. The best known are two types: low-density lipoprotein, often called bad cholesterol, because it carries cholesterol to the arteries, where it can build up when it is high. The second type is high-density lipoprotein, often called good cholesterol, because it helps carry excess cholesterol away from the arteries toward the liver. There are also triglycerides, another type of fat in the blood, whose level rises with excess sugar, alcohol, and excess weight. It is not enough to look at one number; the doctor must read the whole picture: total cholesterol, bad cholesterol, good cholesterol, and triglycerides, together with your age, family history, blood pressure, smoking, and diabetes if present. The real risk depends on all of these factors together, not on one number alone.

How is cholesterol measured and when should it be checked?

Cholesterol is measured with a simple blood test called a lipid profile. The doctor may ask you to fast for several hours before the test, especially to measure triglycerides accurately, so ask the laboratory or your doctor about the instructions before the appointment. Doctors usually recommend checking cholesterol in adults regularly, especially if you have a family history of early heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, excess weight, or if you smoke. Do not wait for symptoms, because high cholesterol is usually silent. When the result arrives, ask your doctor to explain your numbers and what they mean for you, and to say when to repeat the test. Keep your results in one file, because comparing results over the years helps the doctor see the trend, whether your level is improving or getting worse.

What raises cholesterol?

Several factors combine to raise cholesterol. The most important is a diet rich in saturated fat, found in fatty meat, butter, full-fat cheese, fried foods, and processed pastries, and trans fat found in some processed foods. Eating too much sugar, sweetened drinks, and alcohol also raises triglycerides. Other factors include lack of physical activity, excess weight, smoking, advancing age, family history, and some diseases such as an underactive thyroid, diabetes, and kidney disease, and some medicines. In Morocco, some dietary habits contribute to the problem, such as eating too much fatty meat, fried food, and sweets, and eating too little fish, vegetables, and legumes in some daily meals. But the good news is that many of these factors can be changed, and changing food and movement can make a real difference to the numbers.

What helps to lower cholesterol?

The first step is food. Reduce saturated fat: choose meat with visible fat removed, remove chicken skin, cut down on butter and full-fat cheese, avoid fried food as much as possible, and replace frying with grilling, steaming, or baking. Eat more fibre, because oats, barley, legumes such as lentils, chickpeas, and beans, vegetables, and fruit help lower bad cholesterol. Eat oily fish such as sardines, widely available in Morocco, because they are a good source of beneficial fatty acids, preferably grilled rather than fried. Use olive oil in moderation instead of animal fats. Cut down on sugar, sweetened drinks, and alcohol. The second step is regular movement, such as brisk walking on most days of the week, because it helps raise good cholesterol and lower triglycerides. The third step is losing excess weight gradually and quitting smoking. These changes do not help cholesterol alone; they help blood pressure, diabetes, and heart health as a whole.

When is medicine necessary?

For some people, lifestyle change alone is not enough, especially if bad cholesterol is very high, or there is a strong family history, or the person has heart disease, diabetes, or high blood pressure. In these cases the doctor prescribes a medicine, most commonly a group called statins, which studies have shown lower bad cholesterol and reduce the risk of heart disease and stroke. The medicine must be taken exactly as the doctor prescribed, and must not be stopped without consulting the doctor, even if the numbers improve, because the improvement is often thanks to the medicine itself. If side effects appear, such as severe muscle pain or unusual tiredness, tell your doctor, because there are alternatives and different doses, and do not stop treatment on your own. Ask your doctor about the regular tests needed during treatment. Remember that medicine does not replace healthy food and movement; it works with them, and combining them gives the best results.

Misconceptions about cholesterol

One misconception is that slim people do not develop high cholesterol; the truth is that heredity and diet matter regardless of weight. Another is that cholesterol in food is the only problem; the truth is that saturated fat in food affects blood cholesterol more than dietary cholesterol itself for most people. Another is that eggs are completely forbidden for people with high cholesterol; the truth is that it depends on the whole diet, and the amount should be discussed with the doctor or a dietitian instead of following strict general rules. Another is that supplements and herbs replace medicine; the truth is that there is no strong evidence that any single supplement lowers heart risk as proven medicines do, and some supplements may interact with medicines. Another is that high cholesterol causes symptoms such as headaches; the truth is that it is usually silent, and the only way to know it is a blood test.

Tip

Ask your doctor for a cholesterol test, keep your result, and discuss with your doctor what your numbers mean for your personal risk, instead of comparing yourself with others.

The bottom line: know your numbers and change what you can

Cholesterol is necessary for the body, but a high level silently increases the risk of heart disease. Check your level, know your numbers, reduce saturated fat and sugar, eat more fibre, fish, and vegetables, move regularly, quit smoking, and take your medicine if your doctor has prescribed it. These simple steps, if you keep them up, protect your heart and arteries for many years. If you have questions about your result, discuss them with your doctor, who will determine the treatment goal that suits you.
