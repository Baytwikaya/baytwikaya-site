Pregnancy Nutrition: The Complete Guide to Balanced Food and a Safe Pregnancy

Share this article:

Why is nutrition in pregnancy so important?

Nutrition in pregnancy is not nutrition for one person, but for two: the mother and the baby, who grows and develops very quickly over nine months. The food the mother eats provides the essential building blocks for the baby's brain, bones, muscles and organs, and it also affects her own health, her energy, her immunity, the strength of her blood and her ability to recover after birth. Research has shown that good nutrition in pregnancy is linked to better growth of the baby and a healthy birth weight, while a lack of some nutrients is linked to anaemia, tiredness and pregnancy complications.

The good news is that good nutrition in pregnancy does not mean eating large amounts or expensive foods. It means eating varied, balanced food regularly, paying attention to some important nutrients such as folic acid, iron, calcium and iodine, and avoiding some foods that may harm the baby. In Morocco, excellent local foods for pregnant women are available, such as vegetables, fruit, legumes, fish, olive oil, eggs and dairy products, and this guide explains how to benefit from them at every stage of pregnancy.

What does the pregnant body need? The essential nutrients

The pregnant body needs a few extra calories, especially in the second and third trimesters, but the increase does not mean eating for two people; it is a gradual, balanced increase. The most important nutrients to watch are folic acid, a vitamin that helps prevent neural tube defects in the baby, which is why health authorities recommend taking it before pregnancy and in the first weeks of it, and the doctor should be asked about the right dose. The body also needs iron to build blood and avoid anaemia, found in red meat, liver, legumes and leafy vegetables, and it is absorbed better with vitamin C, found in oranges, lemons and peppers.

The body also needs calcium and vitamin D to build the baby's bones and preserve the mother's bones. Calcium is found in milk, yoghurt, cheese, sardines and almonds, while moderate exposure to sunlight helps the skin produce vitamin D. The body also needs iodine for the health of the thyroid gland and the development of the baby's brain, found in iodised salt and sea fish, and omega-3 fatty acids, found in oily fish such as sardines, which support the development of the baby's brain and eyes. Protein is needed to build tissues, and it is found in meat, fish, eggs, legumes and dairy products.

What should a pregnant woman eat every day?

The ideal daily diet in pregnancy is varied and colourful: vegetables and fruit at every meal, whole grains such as whole bread, barley and whole couscous, legumes such as lentils, chickpeas and beans several times a week, a source of protein at every main meal, dairy products every day, and olive oil in moderation. In Morocco, a pregnant woman can rely on balanced home dishes such as tagines rich in vegetables and legumes, salads, grilled fish and soups, while reducing salt, sugar and fried fats. Water should be drunk regularly throughout the day, because dehydration increases tiredness, constipation and headaches.

If a pregnant woman suffers from nausea in the first months, it is advised to eat small, frequent meals instead of large meals, to avoid foods with strong or fatty smells, to eat dry biscuits or toast before getting out of bed, and to drink fluids between meals rather than with them. In the last months, when the baby presses on the stomach, small frequent meals help relieve heartburn and indigestion. If severe vomiting or weight loss continues, the doctor should be consulted, because severe vomiting needs assessment and treatment, and changing food alone is not enough.

Foods to avoid during pregnancy

Some foods may carry germs or substances harmful to the baby, so health authorities recommend avoiding them during pregnancy. The most important are raw or undercooked meat and fish, such as sushi and raw meat, soft cheeses made from unpasteurised milk, unpasteurised milk, raw or undercooked eggs, and liver in large amounts because it contains high amounts of vitamin A. Large fish that may contain high amounts of mercury, such as shark and swordfish, should also be avoided, and small fish such as sardines should be eaten in moderation.

A pregnant woman should also avoid alcohol completely, because there is no safe amount during pregnancy, and it can cause lasting harm to the baby's growth and brain. She should also stop smoking and avoid passive smoking, because it increases the risk of premature birth and low birth weight. Caffeine should be limited to one or two cups of coffee a day at most, as advised by the doctor, remembering that tea, soft drinks and chocolate also contain caffeine. If a pregnant woman takes any medicine or herbal supplement, she should tell the doctor, because some medicines and herbs are not safe in pregnancy.

Weight, movement and sleep during pregnancy

Weight gain during pregnancy is natural and necessary, but the right amount differs according to the mother's weight before pregnancy, so the doctor should be asked about the right gain for each case, and weight should be followed regularly without worry or obsession. Too little gain may be linked to less growth of the baby, and too much gain may increase the risk of gestational diabetes, high blood pressure and a difficult birth. The best gain is gradual, with balanced food and regular movement, not by following a strict diet during pregnancy, because a strict diet may deprive the baby of important nutrients.

Movement is useful for pregnant women in most cases: moderate daily walking, swimming and light stretching help reduce back pain, constipation and swelling, improve mood and sleep, and help control weight and blood sugar. Violent sports or those with a risk of falling or impact should be avoided, and the body should be listened to, stopping when pain, dizziness or shortness of breath is felt. Sleep should be regular, and sleeping on the left side in the last months helps improve blood flow, with pillows to support the belly and back, and the doctor should be consulted about loud snoring or pauses in breathing during sleep.

Tip

Make your plate colourful at every meal: vegetables, fruit, legumes and a source of protein; take iron with vitamin C, and avoid drinking tea with meals.

Common problems: anaemia, heartburn and constipation

Anaemia is one of the most common problems in pregnancy, appearing as tiredness, paleness, shortness of breath and a fast heartbeat, and it is detected by regular blood tests. To prevent it, eat foods rich in iron, such as meat, legumes and leafy vegetables, with vitamin C to improve absorption, and avoid drinking tea and coffee with meals, because they reduce iron absorption. If the doctor prescribes an iron supplement, it should be taken as directed, knowing that it may cause constipation, so water should be drunk and fibre eaten.

Heartburn is common in the last months because of the baby pressing on the stomach, and it is eased by small meals, avoiding spicy and fatty foods, and not lying down immediately after eating. Constipation is also common because of hormonal changes and iron supplements, and it is eased by drinking water, eating fibre from vegetables, fruit and whole grains, and walking daily. Mild swelling of the feet at the end of the day is also common, and it is eased by raising the feet and avoiding standing for long periods, but sudden swelling of the face and hands with headache or disturbed vision may be a sign of high blood pressure and deserves urgent medical advice.

When should you see a doctor immediately?

A pregnant woman should follow her pregnancy regularly with a doctor or midwife, and have the recommended tests, such as measuring blood pressure, blood and urine tests, and ultrasound scans, because this follow-up detects problems early. The doctor should also be consulted immediately when signs appear such as vaginal bleeding, leaking fluid from the vagina, severe abdominal pain, a severe persistent headache, disturbed vision, sudden swelling of the face and hands, reduced movement of the baby after it has been felt regularly, a high temperature, pain when passing urine, or severe persistent vomiting.

A pregnant woman should not hesitate to ask the doctor about any worry, however small it seems, because reassurance is part of care. If she lives with a chronic illness such as diabetes, high blood pressure or thyroid disease, the doctor should be told from the start of pregnancy, because some medicines need adjustment and some conditions need closer follow-up. The doctor should also be told about any supplement or herbal medicine, and about smoking or alcohol, without embarrassment, because the aim is to protect mother and baby together, and the doctor is there to help, not to judge.

The bottom line: balanced food and a safe pregnancy

Good nutrition in pregnancy is simple at heart: varied, balanced food, rich in vegetables, fruit, legumes, fish, protein and dairy products, with folic acid, iron, calcium and iodine, avoiding alcohol, smoking, raw and unpasteurised foods, with moderate movement, good sleep and regular medical follow-up. There is no need for expensive foods or many supplements without medical advice, only balanced home meals and steady healthy habits. Start today with a colourful, balanced plate, keep up your medical follow-up, and consult your doctor about any worrying symptom, because your health and your baby's health deserve care and attention.
