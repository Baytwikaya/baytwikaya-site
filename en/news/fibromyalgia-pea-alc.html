A supplement for fibromyalgia? What a 12-month study found — and what it cannot prove

Share this article:

Pain that never lets up: why fibromyalgia patients keep looking for new options

She wakes after enough hours of sleep, yet her whole body aches as if she had not slept, her muscles are tense, her thinking is foggy, and the simplest daily task drains her energy. That is how many people with fibromyalgia live: widespread pain, alongside exhaustion, disturbed sleep and difficulty concentrating, while blood tests and scans show no clear cause that explains all of this pain.

The treatments available today help some patients, but they are not enough for many, so attention turns to add-on options, including dietary supplements studied for a possible role in easing pain. Among them is a combination of two substances studied separately before: palmitoylethanolamide (PEA) and acetyl-L-carnitine (ALC). What did the newest study of this combination find after 12 months of follow-up?

What did the new study examine?

The study, published in the journal Nutrients on 7 November 2026, is a retrospective single-cohort study that followed 150 people with fibromyalgia for 15 months. Patients took a fixed formulation of palmitoylethanolamide 600 mg and acetyl-L-carnitine 500 mg plus Boswellia serrata 100 mg, with vitamin B6 and vitamin E, twice daily for 12 months, as an add-on to their stable background therapy, followed by a visit 3 months after stopping the formulation.

The design matters from the start: every patient took the formulation, and there was no comparison group taking a placebo or another treatment. That means the study can describe what happened to patients during follow-up, but on its own it cannot prove that the formulation caused what happened.

What did the numbers show?

Of the 150 patients, 64 completed the 12 months, while 45 stopped the formulation — 36 of them because they felt it was not helping — and 41 were lost to follow-up. Among those who completed the study, mean pain on the VAS scale fell from 76.3 to 52.3 after 12 months, and the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQR) score fell from 73.6 to 56.1.

But the picture changes when we look at all 150 patients, not only those who finished. Under the worst-case assumption — that everyone who did not complete returned to their baseline pain — the 12-month change in pain becomes −13.0 points (95% confidence interval: −15.9 to −10.2). After 6 months, 78.1% of completers reached the minimal clinically important difference in FIQR, and 59.4% reached the IMMPACT 30% pain-relief threshold — but those two figures fall to 37.3% and 28.7% of all 150 patients when non-completers are counted as non-responders.

Sleep, meanwhile, changed little: only 20.3% of completers improved by 3 points or more on the sleep quality index (PSQI). Adverse events appeared in 10.0% of patients. The study reports retention of 60.6%, falling to 42.7% if those lost to follow-up are counted as discontinuations. And 3 months after stopping the formulation, pain and FIQR remained within 1 point of their 12-month values.

A cautious reading: why these results alone are not enough

This is the most important point in the whole article, and it is the one the researchers themselves stressed: the study has no comparison group, so the changes cannot be attributed to the formulation or to any of its components. Pain in fibromyalgia fluctuates over time, and expectations, medical attention, regular follow-up and changes in daily life can improve results without the formulation playing any part.

More than half the patients did not finish the study, and about a third stopped because they felt no benefit, which weakens confidence in the figures. Even under the worst-case assumption there is still an average improvement in pain, but it is an improvement in a descriptive study without comparison — not proof of effectiveness. As for sleep, it barely improved, even though disturbed sleep is one of the most common complaints.

A smaller earlier study points the same way

This is not the first study to examine this combination. In 2025, the journal Pharmaceutics published a retrospective study by Bentivenga and colleagues, covering 100 people with primary fibromyalgia. Some took acetyl-L-carnitine 500 mg with PEA 600 mg twice daily for 8 weeks (once daily in the final week), as an add-on to standard care, and were compared with patients who received standard care alone, after matching the two groups (86 patients, 43 in each group).

In that study, the FIQR score fell by a median of −19.0 points (p < 0.001) in the LAC plus PEA group, with no significant change in the comparison group, and the improvement was clearer in a subgroup with suspected signs of small-fibre neuropathy. But the authors ended with the same call: further prospective controlled studies are needed before any conclusion.

Tip

This formulation is a dietary supplement, not an approved drug for fibromyalgia, and in the study it was used as an add-on to background therapy, not as a replacement. So do not stop your prescribed treatment and do not start any supplement on your own: discuss it with your doctor, ask about possible interactions with your medicines and about side effects, and about options with stronger evidence, such as graded exercise, cognitive behavioural therapy and better sleep. Any product that promises a cure for fibromyalgia is exaggerating.

The bottom line: a promising signal that needs a controlled trial

After 12 months on the formulation, pain and the impact of the disease improved among those who finished the study, while sleep stayed almost unchanged, and many stopped because they felt no benefit. These results are a signal worth following, not proof of effectiveness: the study has no comparison group, and it cannot credit the improvement to the formulation — as the researchers themselves said. The right next step, as they concluded, is a controlled trial lasting at least 12 months, comparing the formulation with a placebo, to finally answer the question that matters to patients: does it really help, or would the improvement have happened without it?
