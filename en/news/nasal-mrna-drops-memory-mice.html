Experimental nasal drops restore young memory in old mice — but they are not an Alzheimer's treatment yet

Share this article:

When the mind forgets what it once knew

He walks into a room and stops, wondering: why did I come in here? He searches for the name of a neighbour he has known for twenty years and cannot find it, and he re-reads the same page three times without remembering what it said. These small moments are the everyday face of memory decline with age, and for millions of families they are the start of a long road that sometimes ends in Alzheimer's disease. The World Health Organization estimates that more than 55 million people worldwide live with dementia, with around 10 million new cases each year, and Alzheimer's accounts for 60 to 70% of them.

Against this background comes striking news from Australia: a research team at the University of Sydney has developed experimental nasal drops that restored mental abilities close to those of young mice in aged mice, and prevented memory decline in mice engineered to carry features of Alzheimer's. The results were published in the journal Advanced Science on 18 November 2026. But before any excitement, the most important sentence in this whole article must be said: the experiment was done in mice only, no human has received this treatment yet, and it is not an available treatment, nor anywhere near pharmacies.

What exactly did the researchers do?

The research is led by Professor Greg Neely of the Charles Perkins Centre at the University of Sydney, and its first author is Dr Maria Bergamasco. The idea uses the same mRNA technology the world came to know through the COVID-19 vaccines, but with a completely different goal: instead of directing the mRNA-carrying lipid nanoparticles toward triggering an immune response, the researchers direct them to the cells of the nasal lining, prompting them to produce a natural protein known as brain-derived neurotrophic factor, BDNF for short.

This protein supports the survival of neurons and their ability to form new connections with each other — the very process that weakens gradually with age and in dementia, and whose levels fall with ageing and Alzheimer's. The old problem is that injecting the protein itself into the blood does not get it to the brain, because the blood-brain barrier blocks most compounds from crossing. Here, the nose itself becomes what the researchers call a local “drug factory”: nasal lining cells read the mRNA instructions, produce the protein, and release it toward the brain through nose-to-brain pathways. The mRNA used in the study was based on the same backbone as the BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine, modified to carry instructions for producing BDNF instead of immune instructions.

Aged mice recovered youthful memory

The team first tested the treatment on aged mice, 18 months old — elderly by laboratory standards — compared with young mice of 3 months. The aged mice received a dose of one microgram once a week for four weeks, and a memory test was carried out 24 hours after each dose. There were three tests: working memory, spatial memory, and the ability to tell new objects from familiar ones.

The result was that the treated aged mice outperformed untreated aged mice on all three tests, and scored close to the young mice — so close that the two groups were hard to tell apart. Professor Neely described this as returning animal cognition to its youthful prime. It is this direct comparison with young mice that makes the result striking: it does not mean a slight improvement only, but an almost complete return to young performance levels in those laboratory tests.

And in the Alzheimer's model: decline stopped before it started

The second group consisted of mice from a genetically modified strain known as 5xFAD, commonly used as an Alzheimer's model because they develop disease-like features early in life: brain pathology appears from two months of age, and cognitive decline usually begins at around four months. The researchers started treatment when these mice were two months old — when the first disease-related changes appear — and continued giving it twice a week. By four months, the treated mice showed no further decline in cognitive abilities, and they performed better than untreated mice on the same memory tests.

The team also observed increased generation of new neurons in the hippocampus, the brain region that plays a central role in learning and memory and is among the first damaged in Alzheimer's, with higher neuron counts in some hippocampal areas. And according to the researchers, no major side effects were recorded during the study period in the mice.

The study's surprise: the plaques stayed as they were

One of the most interesting findings is that the treatment did not reduce the amyloid plaques accumulated in the mice's brains — the protein deposits that are among the hallmark signs of Alzheimer's and disrupt communication between neurons. Memory improved, and the plaques did not change. The team takes this to mean that their approach does not work by removing plaques, but by supporting the health, survival and communication of the neurons themselves.

This feature could make the treatment a complement to other techniques that directly target plaque removal, rather than a replacement for them. It is a different philosophy for tackling the disease: instead of focusing only on clearing deposits, the brain itself is strengthened to resist their effects. But this idea itself remains a hypothesis built on mouse results, and it needs proof in human trials before any real therapeutic hope can be built on it.

Why the nose? A shortcut that bypasses the blood-brain barrier

Delivery through the nose offers an important advantage: it can bypass some of the obstacles of the blood-brain barrier, the selective layer of cells surrounding the blood vessels in the brain that prevents many therapeutic compounds from reaching it through the bloodstream. This is why many attempts to deliver the BDNF protein itself to the brain have failed: the protein does not cross this barrier efficiently, whereas the instructions to make it, wrapped in lipid nanoparticles, reach nasal cells that produce it on the spot.

Added to that, this approach is non-invasive, unlike some other experimental treatments that require direct injection into the brain or spinal cord. If its effectiveness and safety are ever proven in humans, it could be easier to apply on a wide scale, especially for older people, for whom surgical procedures carry extra risk. But the road from a mouse's nose to a human nose is not short — which brings us to the study's limits.

Strict limits: these are mice, not humans

This is where a good article differs from an exaggerated one. First, the results are limited to mice, and the anatomical difference between the human nose and the mouse nose is large: the structure of the nasal cavity, the area of the olfactory lining and the olfactory nerve pathways all differ, which makes it essential to test the treatment again in humans before any clinical use. Second, the behavioural test groups were small — 6 to 10 mice per group — and the follow-up was short: four weeks in the ageing experiment and two months in the Alzheimer's model, a duration that does not answer the question of whether the benefit lasts in the long term.

Third, the safety checks in the study were only a first pass, and long-term safety is unknown. The researchers themselves noted that the components of the treatment have previously been used separately in human trials, but the complete combination needs its own safety assessment, and a series of animal safety studies must come before any trial in patients. Professor Neely said he hopes human trials can begin within the next two years, once those studies are done. Fourth — a lesson the history of Alzheimer's research knows well — many treatments showed promising results in mice and then failed to achieve the same effectiveness in patients, because a mouse model, however precise, is not a human disease.

One point of transparency should also be added: the two lead researchers, Neely and Bergamasco, are listed as inventors on a provisional patent application related to this technology, and the study was supported by Australia's National Health and Medical Research Council and the New South Wales Ministry of Health, as well as third-party philanthropic support. That is common in scientific research, but it is worth mentioning because the team has a direct interest in the technology's success.

Tip

There are today no nasal drops and no mRNA treatment for memory or Alzheimer's, and do not look for one outside medical channels — it is a mouse experiment only, and any offer of it to humans outside an approved clinical trial is dangerous. What actually protects memory today is what human studies have proven: regular sleep of 7 to 8 hours, daily movement such as brisk walking, a Mediterranean-style diet rich in vegetables, fish and olive oil, controlling blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol, treating hearing loss if present, an active social life, and training the brain through reading and learning new things. And if you or a relative notice repeated forgetfulness that affects daily life, do not delay seeing a doctor — early diagnosis opens the door to approved treatments and care plans, and it is the only right step today.

The bottom line: real hope, and a long road

What the University of Sydney team achieved matters scientifically for two reasons: it proves that the nose can become a non-invasive route for delivering treatments to the brain through the mRNA platform — which proved it can be developed quickly and manufactured at scale during the COVID-19 pandemic — and that memory can be supported by strengthening the neurons themselves, without needing to remove plaques. But the fact that must stay front and centre is that all of this happened in mice only: aged mice recovered youthful memory in a laboratory, Alzheimer's mice stopped declining in a laboratory, and no human has received a single drop of this treatment yet. The hope is real, and the road to the patient is still long — it passes through animal safety studies, then human trials that could begin within two years if things go as the team hopes. As for today, the practical message does not change: memory is protected by sleep, movement, good food, controlling risk factors, and seeing a doctor at the first real worry.
