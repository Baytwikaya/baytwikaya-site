Before memory fades: sleep and appetite changes may reveal the beginnings of Alzheimer's

Share this article:

When sleep and appetite change before memory

Most people know Alzheimer's disease by memory loss, but many families notice something else years earlier: disturbed sleep, repeated waking at night, a changing appetite, and sleep and waking times falling out of step. These changes were long treated as side effects of the disease, but a new study published in Nature on 18 November 2026 suggests they may be part of the disease itself from its beginnings — because they arise from a small brain region that controls sleep, hunger and the body clock.

What is this region? What exactly did the researchers find in it? And does it mean that disturbed sleep alone can reveal Alzheimer's early? The short answer is no, not alone — but the new discovery gives scientists the first detailed map of this region in ageing and Alzheimer's, and a starting point for future research.

What is the hypothalamus? And why does it matter in Alzheimer's?

The hypothalamus is a small region at the base of the brain, roughly the size of an almond, yet it is the command centre for the body's internal balance: it regulates body temperature, sleep, hunger and thirst, hormone release, and the response to stress. It is made of specialised nuclei, each with its own job: the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) is the body's master clock that coordinates daily rhythms, the arcuate nucleus regulates appetite and energy balance, and the lateral hypothalamus helps drive appetite and wakefulness.

Most of what we know about Alzheimer's comes from the hippocampus and the cortex, where memory lives — as senior author Ashley Webb of the Buck Institute for Research on Aging puts it. But patients and their families often also report disrupted sleep, a change in appetite, and different responses to stress, all functions controlled by the hypothalamus. That is why her team decided to look directly at this region, with enough resolution to see which of its cells change with age and in the disease.

What did the researchers do? A new atlas called HypoAD

The team built a new atlas called HypoAD, combining two very different methods. First, they analysed MRI scans from 458 adults across the lifespan and measured the volume of 13 regions inside the hypothalamus, grouping participants by cognitive status and by levels of amyloid-beta, the protein that forms plaques in Alzheimer's.

Second, they analysed gene activity in about 614,403 cell nuclei from post-mortem hypothalamus tissue, from 17 donors — seven people with mostly early-stage Alzheimer's and seven age-matched healthy controls, plus three younger donors from previously published data. This method shows which genes are switched on in each cell, so changes can be traced to specific cell types and functions. The team also validated their findings against spinal fluid protein measurements, a prefrontal cortex dataset from about 800 donors, spatial gene-expression maps, and a mouse model of Alzheimer's.

What did they find? The body clock and inflammation lead the way

The first key finding concerns the body clock: the researchers found disrupted signalling involving vasopressin — a hormone and neuropeptide involved in circadian regulation — in a prominent group of neurons in the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the body's master clock itself, in people at early stages of the disease. This could help explain why Alzheimer's is linked to sleep disturbances and irregular activity and rest rhythms, including the evening worsening known as sundowning, which appears in later stages.

The second finding concerns inflammation: microglia — the brain's resident immune cells — showed the largest number of altered genes of any cell type examined. When the team ordered cells along a young-to-old-to-Alzheimer's path, these cells gradually shifted from a resting state toward the inflammatory, disease-associated state previously seen in the brain regions involved in memory. In Webb's words, the hypothalamus appears to transition to an inflammatory state early in Alzheimer's disease, just like the memory regions.

The researchers identified 254 neuron subtypes and found that inhibitory (GABAergic) neurons changed the most, mainly in neuropeptide and receptor signalling. Machine-learning models could also tell Alzheimer's cells from control cells using gene expression alone, and the most important genes centred on two themes: inflammation and circadian rhythm.

Some regions shrink and others grow: ageing reshapes the hypothalamus

The MRI scans produced a striking result: the hypothalamus as a whole looks relatively stable in volume with age, but its inner regions do not change in the same way. The lateral hypothalamus shrank, while the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the arcuate nucleus, the paraventricular and periventricular nuclei, the dorsomedial nucleus and other regions involved in metabolism and hormone regulation grew larger.

The crucial point is that this pattern held after accounting for Alzheimer's status, suggesting it mainly reflects normal ageing. In fact, the volume differences tied specifically to Alzheimer's were not statistically significant — imaging alone could not separate the effect of the disease from the effect of age on the size of these regions, and the molecular changes inside the cells were far clearer than the volume changes. As for why some regions grow, the researchers offer a hypothesis that still needs confirmation: larger cells through increased activity of the mTOR growth pathway, along with remodelling of the material between cells and inflammatory activation of glia, with no clear change in the total number of cells.

Read the results with care: association, not causation — and no treatment yet

This is where a good article differs from an exaggerated one. First, the study design is cross-sectional: it compares groups at a single point in time and does not follow people over years, so its results show association, not cause and effect. It does not prove that hypothalamic changes cause Alzheimer's, nor that disturbed sleep causes the disease, nor the reverse. Second, the human tissue sample is small: 14 older donors (seven with Alzheimer's and seven healthy) plus three younger ones — a usual number for this kind of study, but one that limits how far the findings can be generalised.

Third, the imaging data and the gene data came from different people, so the team compared them side by side without combining them statistically. And fourth, the study offers no new treatment and no diagnostic test: disturbed sleep and appetite changes are very common and have many causes — from stress and medicines to sleep apnoea, thyroid disease and depression — and neither of them alone means a person has Alzheimer's or will develop it. The next step, the researchers say, is to follow these changes over time and test whether protecting the regularity of the body clock and vasopressin signalling can help manage some symptoms or slow the course of the disease.

Advice

If you notice a new and lasting change in an older relative's sleep — repeated waking, excessive daytime sleepiness, or confusion between night and day — together with a change in appetite or weight or a decline in memory, do not interpret it on your own and do not wait for it to pass. See a doctor for a full assessment: these signs may have treatable causes, such as sleep apnoea, a vitamin deficiency, a thyroid disorder or depression, and only a doctor can decide whether a cognitive assessment is needed. In any case, keep regular sleep and waking times, get daylight exposure, stay physically active, and eat regular meals — these habits support the body clock and brain health in general, without being a treatment for Alzheimer's or proven prevention against it.

The bottom line: the body clock may speak before memory does

The new Nature study does not say that disturbed sleep diagnoses Alzheimer's, and it offers no treatment — but it provides the first detailed map of the hypothalamus in ageing and disease: a body clock whose signals are disrupted, brain immune cells shifting toward inflammation, and inhibitory neurons changing more than others — all in the early stages, before memory loss deepens. If circadian disruption is one of the first things to go wrong in the disease, it may also be one of the first places where we can intervene — and that is what future studies will test.
