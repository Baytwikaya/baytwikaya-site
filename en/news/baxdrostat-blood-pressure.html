Baxdrostat: the blood-pressure pill that keeps working two years after you stop? What the NEJM study found

Share this article:

A drug taken for months, then stopped — and blood pressure stayed lower for two years?

Millions of people take a blood-pressure pill every morning, year after year, because stopping it means their pressure climbs back within days. That is the rule every patient and every doctor knows. But a new study published in the New England Journal of Medicine in November 2026 broke that rule in a small group of patients: a drug taken for months, then stopped — and blood pressure stayed lower for up to two years afterwards.

So what is this drug? Who were these patients? And why do the researchers themselves insist it is far too early to talk about a cure?

What is aldosterone — and why does it raise blood pressure in some people?

Aldosterone is a hormone made by the adrenal glands, two small glands sitting above the kidneys. Its job is to regulate salt and water in the body: when it rises, the body holds on to salt and water, blood volume grows, and blood pressure rises with it.

In some people, an adrenal gland produces too much of this hormone for no clear reason, a condition called primary aldosteronism. Estimates put it behind 5 to 10 percent of all high blood pressure, and the pressure in these cases often resists standard medicines, while the risk of heart disease and stroke is higher than in ordinary hypertension. The problem is that many of these patients never learn that aldosterone is the cause of their pressure, because diagnosing it needs special hormone tests that are not ordered routinely.

What is baxdrostat, and how does it work?

Baxdrostat belongs to a new class of drugs called aldosterone synthase inhibitors. It does not block the hormone's receptors after it is made, as older drugs do — it stops the adrenal gland from producing the hormone in the first place, by switching off the enzyme that builds it.

The US Food and Drug Administration approved the drug in 2026 for uncontrolled hypertension, while a review is still under way in the United Kingdom. But the new study is not about lowering pressure while taking the drug — it is about what happened after the drug was stopped.

What did the study find? Pressure lower — and an aldosterone marker 99% lower

The study followed 14 patients with primary aldosteronism at eight centres in the United States. They took baxdrostat daily for 6 to 12 months, then stopped it, and the researchers kept following them for up to two years after stopping.

The result was surprising: two years after stopping, blood pressure was still lower by an average of 25 points compared with before treatment, and a marker of aldosterone production stayed 99% below its pre-treatment level. Most strikingly, just under a quarter of the patients came off all blood-pressure medicines and had completely normal blood pressure with no drug at all.

The study was led by Professor Morris Brown of Queen Mary University of London, with Mason Freeman of Harvard University, and was published in the New England Journal of Medicine on 6 November 2026.

Why did the effect last after stopping? A hypothesis that still needs proof

The explanation the researchers propose is still an unconfirmed hypothesis: that blocking aldosterone for months triggers a surge of reactive oxygen species that selectively destroys the overactive cells in the adrenal gland. In other words, the drug may do more than temporarily suppress the hormone — it may shrink the very source of the excess.

If that hypothesis holds, it would change how doctors think: from a pill taken for life to a treatment that resets the gland. But proving it needs a bigger trial, and a larger prospective trial called ELIXIR is already under way to test the idea in more patients.

Read the results carefully: only 14 patients

Here is where caution belongs. The study covered only 14 patients, and the follow-up after stopping was not a randomised controlled trial — it was an observational follow-up of a small group. The researchers themselves say it is far too early to call this a cure. The word they use is "remission": the disease quietened down, not that it vanished for good.

We do not yet know whether the effect lasts beyond two years, whether it applies to all patients with primary aldosteronism, or what the long-term side effects are. So these results do not mean any patient should stop their medicine on their own, or ask their doctor for baxdrostat tomorrow.

Tip

If your blood pressure stays high despite taking several medicines, ask your doctor about an aldosterone test — it may be the hidden cause of your resistant pressure. Do not stop any of your medicines on your own, and do not seek out baxdrostat outside medical channels: it is a new drug and is not available everywhere. While we wait for more evidence, the basics stay the same: measure your pressure regularly, cut down salt, move every day, take your prescribed treatment, and talk to your doctor before any change.

The bottom line: a long remission, not a cure yet

The baxdrostat study is among the strongest medical news of the year: a drug taken for months whose effect lasted two years after stopping, with some patients coming off every blood-pressure pill. But it is a small study, built on a hypothesis that is not yet proven, and its own authors are the first to warn against exaggeration. The promise is large, and the final verdict belongs to the wider ELIXIR trial. For today, the practical message does not change: if your pressure resists treatment, look for the cause with your doctor — aldosterone may be the answer.
