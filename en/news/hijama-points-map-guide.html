The Cupping Points Map: A Complete Guide

Share this article:

What is the cupping points map?

When practitioners talk about cupping, they often mention specific points on the body by name or number, such as points on the upper back, between the shoulders, the lower back, the head and the legs. The cupping points map is an illustration that gathers these points on a picture of the human body, so the practitioner knows where to place the cups for the complaint being treated.

This guide explains where these points came from, which are the best known and what is said about each, how to read the map correctly, and what the difference is between inherited tradition and proven science, so that the map does not become in your mind a ready-made medical prescription — which it is not.

Where did the cupping points come from?

Cupping goes back thousands of years, and was practised by ancient civilisations in Egypt, China, Greece and the Islamic world. In the Islamic tradition, reports mention particular points for cupping, such as the two sides of the neck and the upper back between the shoulders, and practitioners still use these points today and place them at the top of their maps.

Over time, practitioners added other points based on their practical experience and on the maps of traditional Chinese medicine and acupuncture points, so the maps grew until they contained dozens of numbered points on the back, head, abdomen and limbs. That is why maps differ from one school to another: there is no single map agreed on worldwide, and that is the first thing a reader should know.

The best-known points and what is said about them

Among the best-known points is the upper back between the shoulders, which practitioners use in many general cases and for back and shoulder pain. The two points on the sides of the neck are mentioned in the tradition in connection with headaches. Points on the lower back are used for back and leg pain, and points on the knee and leg are mentioned for joint pain.

There are also points on the head, chest and abdomen, which practitioners link to different complaints such as headache, chest tightness and digestive problems. It is important to understand that this link between a point and a complaint is mostly based on tradition and practical experience, not on strong clinical trials proving that a particular point treats a particular disease. A point alone does not diagnose an illness or determine a treatment.

Preventive points and the points of the tradition

Some practitioners distinguish between points used when there is a specific complaint and general preventive points used for people with no particular illness, such as the upper back points. They speak of the points mentioned in the tradition, a limited number of well-known points, in contrast to the much larger modern maps that contain many points for each complaint.

The preventive idea itself needs caution: cupping is not a routine check-up and not a substitute for a healthy lifestyle, and there is no strong evidence that regular cupping prevents disease in healthy people. Anyone seeking prevention should start with movement, a balanced diet, enough sleep and treating known risk factors, and consult a doctor before any preventive procedure.

How to read the map correctly

Read the map as an organisational tool for the practitioner, not as a precise treatment map. It helps the practitioner record the points used and communicate with other practitioners, but it does not mean that pain in a particular place is treated by placing a cup directly on it, or that every number on the map has a specific proven effect. The human body differs from person to person, and what suits one person may not suit another.

If a practitioner shows you a map full of points for a single session, ask why each point was chosen, how many cups will be used, and what risks are expected. A good session is not the one with the most cups, but the one in which the points are justified and understood, and in which the practitioner knows their limits and when to refer you to a doctor.

The limits of the evidence: what does science say?

Scientific reviews indicate that studies on cupping are few and small, many suffer from weak design and short follow-up, and there are no large well-controlled trials proving that a particular point on the map treats a particular disease. That does not mean people do not feel better after cupping — they may feel relaxed and get temporary pain relief — but that improvement does not prove that the chosen point caused the recovery.

Many studies also do not separate the effect of cupping itself from the effects of rest, care and expectation. Health bodies therefore remain cautious: cupping is a traditional practice in which some people may find relief, but it is not a proven treatment for diseases, and it must not delay diagnosis or medical treatment, especially in serious or chronic illnesses.

Tip

Do not rely on a points map alone to decide your treatment: medical diagnosis comes first, then the practitioner discusses the points suited to your case, and avoid any point with a wound or skin inflammation.

Safety rules before choosing any point

Before any session, tell the practitioner about your health and your medicines, and make sure there are no contraindications such as blood-thinning medicines, a bleeding disorder, severe anaemia, pregnancy or a skin condition at the point. Cups must never be placed on an open wound, a burn, inflamed skin, directly over a prominent bone, on the abdomen during pregnancy, or over prominent varicose veins.

Make sure sterile single-use cups and instruments are used, and that hands are washed and the skin cleaned before the session. After the session, watch the points: mild redness and round marks are expected and fade within days, but severe pain, swelling, discharge of pus or fever are signs that need medical advice without delay.

How does the practitioner choose the points for a complaint?

A good practitioner starts by listening to your complaint and asking about your health and medicines before thinking about any point, because choosing the points comes after understanding the case, not before it. Lower back pain usually draws attention to points on the lower back and the upper back between the shoulders, while a headache draws attention to the points on the sides of the neck, and a person who comes for general care without a specific complaint is usually offered only well-known general points such as the upper back. This general direction does not mean that the point itself treats the disease; it is a starting point for the discussion between you and the practitioner.

In a first session, the choice is usually limited and cautious: a small number of cups and points with a clear reason, an explanation of why each point was chosen and what is expected from it, and attention to how your body responds after the session. If you feel better or feel discomfort, tell the practitioner at the next session, because these observations are part of the decision. Choosing points is always individual: age, the condition of the skin, chronic illnesses and medicines, including blood-thinning medicines, all affect which points are safe for you, and the practitioner may leave out a point with a wound or skin inflammation even if it is the usual point for your complaint. There is no single points prescription that suits everyone, and any practitioner who offers the same map to every person without asking about their condition is ignoring the most important step of the session. Always ask the practitioner why this point was chosen rather than another, because a clear explanation is part of a safe session.

The bottom line: the map is a tool for the practitioner, not a treatment prescription

The cupping points map gathers a long tradition and practical experience, and it includes well-known points such as the upper back, the sides of the neck and the lower back, but it is not a single agreed map, and it is not proof that each point treats a particular disease. Read it as an organisational tool that helps the practitioner, consult your doctor before any session, and choose a qualified practitioner who explains their choices and follows safety rules.
