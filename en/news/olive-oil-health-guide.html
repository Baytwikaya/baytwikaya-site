Olive Oil and Health: The Complete Guide

Share this article:

Olive oil: Morocco's liquid gold

Olive oil is present in every Moroccan home, from morning bread to tagines and salads, and it is also one of the most studied fats in the world. Morocco produces olive oil in large quantities, with different varieties from different regions, from Meknes to Marrakech to Ouezzane. But not all olive oil is equal, and not every way of using it is equally healthy. In this guide we explain what extra virgin olive oil is, what makes it different from other oils, how it supports the health of the heart and blood vessels, and how to choose it, store it and use it in your kitchen without excess. The goal is to get the best from this Moroccan treasure, and to avoid the common mistakes that reduce its benefit or add calories without value.

What is extra virgin olive oil?

Extra virgin olive oil is oil extracted from olives by cold mechanical pressing, without chemical treatment and without high heat, and for this reason it keeps natural compounds called polyphenols, which are antioxidants that protect the oil itself and give it a distinctive taste with a slight bitterness and a peppery feeling in the throat. Ordinary or refined olive oil, by contrast, goes through processing that removes many of these compounds and much of the flavour, so it becomes less beneficial even though it remains a vegetable fat. Olive oil contains mainly monounsaturated fats, the type linked to a healthy dietary pattern when it replaces saturated fats such as butter and ghee. It also contains vitamin E, an antioxidant that helps protect cells. When buying oil, look for the words extra virgin, the production and expiry dates, and a dark bottle that protects it from light, and buy from a source you trust, because olive oil is one of the most adulterated products in the world.

Olive oil and heart health

Olive oil has been linked in large studies to the Mediterranean dietary pattern, a pattern centred on vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, fish and olive oil as the main source of fat. Research suggests that replacing saturated fats with monounsaturated fats, such as those in olive oil, helps improve blood cholesterol levels, an important factor in heart health. The polyphenols in extra virgin oil also have antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which scientists are studying with growing interest. But it is important to understand that olive oil is not a medicine, and it does not work alone: its benefit appears within a complete balanced dietary pattern, with plenty of vegetables, daily movement, and less salt, sugar and smoking. Someone who eats heavy fried meals and then adds olive oil on top will not get the same benefit. Moderation also matters, because olive oil, like all oils, is rich in calories, and a single tablespoon contains many calories, so excess raises body weight even when the type of fat is good.

Cooking with olive oil: what does science say?

A common question is: can you cook with olive oil over heat? The answer is that extra virgin olive oil is relatively stable at moderate heat, and it is suitable for everyday cooking such as tagines, cooked vegetables and light frying, especially because its antioxidants protect it from damage. But it should not be heated until it smokes heavily, frying oil should not be reused many times, and it should not be left on high heat for a long time, because prolonged intense heat damages its beneficial compounds and changes its taste. For repeated deep frying, it is better to reduce frying in general and replace it with grilling, steaming or oven cooking. For salads, bread and cold dishes, extra virgin olive oil is the best choice, because you enjoy its flavour and compounds fully without heat. In Moroccan cooking, you can add the oil at the end of cooking the tagine instead of at the start, to preserve its flavour, and use it with lemon, garlic and herbs as a dressing for Moroccan salad and grilled vegetables.

How to choose a good oil and store it

Choosing a good olive oil does not need great expertise, only a few clear signs. First, read the label: look for the words extra virgin, the country of origin and the harvest or production date, and avoid oil that gives no date. Second, notice the packaging: a dark bottle or a metal tin protects the oil from light better than a clear bottle. Third, taste it if you can: good oil has a fruity aroma and a taste with a slight bitterness and a peppery throat feeling, and these are signs of polyphenols, not a defect. Fourth, a very low price for oil that claims to be extra virgin should raise caution, because producing good oil is costly. As for storage, keep the oil in a cool dark place, away from the stove and oven, close the cap tightly after each use, and do not buy large quantities to last many months if you use only a little, because oil oxidises over time and loses its flavour and benefit. It is better to use an opened bottle within a few weeks.

Common mistakes in using olive oil

The most common mistake is treating olive oil as if it had no calories, pouring it generously over every dish, and then being surprised by weight gain. The truth is that all oils are roughly similar in calories, and the difference is in the type of fat, not in the amount allowed. The second mistake is using olive oil for repeated deep frying and then reusing it, which damages it and turns it into a source of unwanted compounds. The third mistake is storing oil in a clear bottle near the stove, where light and heat spoil it quickly. The fourth mistake is buying cheap oil of unknown origin and expecting the benefit of extra virgin oil from it. The fifth mistake is forgetting that olive oil is part of a complete meal: a spoon of oil on a fresh vegetable salad is completely different from a spoon of oil on a heavy fried dish. Avoiding these mistakes is simple: measure the oil with a spoon instead of pouring freely, store it well, use it in moderate cooking and salads, and make it a replacement for butter and ghee rather than an addition on top of them.

Olive oil in everyday Moroccan cooking

In Morocco, olive oil holds a special place beyond cooking: it is served with bread in the morning, added to harira, tagines and couscous, and used to dress salads and vegetables. To benefit from it daily without excess, spread your amount across the whole day: a small spoon with bread in the morning, a spoon on the salad at midday, and a spoon in the tagine in the evening, instead of pouring a large amount into one meal. You can mix it with lemon juice and cumin as a light dressing, or with thyme as a healthy dip, or with garlic and herbs as a marinade for grilled fish. In Moroccan souks, you will find oils from different regions with different flavours, so try small quantities first before buying a large amount, and ask the seller about the pressing date and the region. And remember that olive oil is not a replacement for visiting a doctor or for a prescribed treatment: if you have high cholesterol or heart disease, consult your doctor about your whole diet, and do not rely on a single ingredient, however good its reputation.

Tip

Use extra virgin olive oil for everyday cooking and salads, and keep it in a dark bottle away from light and heat so it keeps its quality.

The bottom line: good oil, in a moderate amount, in a balanced dish

Extra virgin olive oil is one of the best fats that can enter your kitchen, and it is a true Moroccan treasure that supports heart health within a balanced dietary pattern. Choose an extra virgin oil from a trusted source, keep it in a dark bottle away from light and heat, use it in a moderate amount in cooking and salads, and make it a replacement for saturated fats rather than an addition on top of them. With these simple steps, olive oil stays a friend to your health every day, without becoming a hidden source of excess calories.
