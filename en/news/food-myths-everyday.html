Food myths we believe every day: carbs, calories and fats — what is the truth?

Share this article:

Nutrition advice everywhere — and not all of it is true

Countless tips about nutrition and weight loss circulate on the internet and social media, and some misleading information may even come from health and fitness professionals. These beliefs include common ideas about calories, carbohydrates, fats and meal timing, as well as food supplements — and following some of them without understanding can lead to unsuitable food choices, or harm physical and mental health.

A report published by Al Arabiya on 22 November 2026, based on a report from the Healthline website, collected sixteen common food myths and what the evidence says about them. Here are the main ones, in our own words.

Calories and diets: the number is not everything

Myth one: calorie counting is all that matters. It is true that eating fewer calories than the body burns is a key factor in losing weight, but it is not the only one: hormones, genetic factors and some health conditions, such as an underactive thyroid, and medicines can make weight loss harder, and the quality of food and the sustainability of the diet matter no less than the number of calories. Indeed, an excessive focus on counting calories has been linked to a higher risk of eating disorders.

And myth two: crash diets are best. Very-low-calorie diets may produce rapid weight loss, but in the long term they can lower the metabolic rate and increase the risk of osteoporosis and gallstones — a moderate, sustainable calorie deficit is a better choice. As for myth three — losing weight is easy — the reality is that losing weight takes time, consistency and patience, and it may be harder for some people because of genetic or health factors.

Fats, cholesterol and carbohydrates

Myth four: foods rich in fat are unhealthy. The body needs fats to function, and diets very low in fat may be linked to health problems — what matters most is the type of fat and the quality of the diet, not avoiding fat altogether. Related to it is myth five: low-fat products are healthier — yet some of them may contain larger amounts of added sugar and salt, so food labels should be read before choosing.

And myth six: cholesterol-rich foods raise blood cholesterol directly — but experts explain that these foods do not necessarily raise cholesterol in most healthy adults, and eggs, for example, can have a place in a balanced diet. As for myth seven: carbohydrates cause weight gain — not all carbohydrates are alike; whole grains, legumes and fibre-rich vegetables differ from sweets and ultra-processed baked goods, and balanced diets that include healthy sources of carbohydrates are linked to lower risks of obesity, diabetes and heart disease.

Meal timing, juices and potatoes

Myth eight: breakfast is the most important meal of the day — not everyone needs to eat breakfast; some people can benefit from delaying it as part of intermittent fasting, but skipping meals does not suit children, teenagers, pregnant women or some people with health conditions. And myth nine: small, frequent meals are essential — there is no ideal number of meals that suits everyone; what matters is meeting the body’s needs for energy and nutrients, with exceptions such as people with diabetes who need a different meal pattern.

And myth ten: all juices and smoothies are healthy choices — drinks made from fresh vegetables and fruit can be nutritious, but some commercial products contain large amounts of added sugar and calories. As for myth eleven: potatoes are unhealthy — they contain potassium, vitamin C and fibre, and can be part of a balanced diet, preferably grilled or baked rather than fried.

Supplements and thinness: when can a myth be costly?

Myth twelve: non-nutritive sweeteners are always healthy — research is still studying their effects, and some studies have pointed to links with changes in gut bacteria and disturbed blood-sugar regulation, but these findings need more research. And myth thirteen: food supplements are a waste of money — some supplements may benefit people with specific nutritional needs, but their use should be based on need and on a specialist’s advice, choosing products that have undergone independent testing.

And myth fourteen: calcium supplements are necessary for everyone — not everyone needs them, and research does not show that they reduce the risk of fractures or osteoporosis in people who are not already at high risk, while calcium can be obtained from foods such as yogurt, sardines and legumes. And myth fifteen: fibre supplements replace fibre-rich foods — they may help improve bowel movement and blood-sugar control, but they do not replace the fibre, nutrients and plant compounds found in vegetables, fruit and legumes. And finally myth sixteen: you must be thin to be healthy — excess weight is linked to several health risks, but enjoying good health does not necessarily require reaching thinness; a balanced diet and regular physical activity are important factors for improving health.

Tip

Before following any dietary rule spreading on the internet, ask yourself: is it sustainable? And does it suit your health condition? Healthy eating is not built on strict rules or quick fixes, but on a balanced, sustainable pattern that fits each person’s needs. And before making radical changes to your diet or starting supplements, consult a doctor or dietitian, especially if you have a health condition that needs special follow-up.

The bottom line: healthy scepticism beats a quick rule

Sixteen myths in a single report, and they share one thing: an exaggerated simplification of a complex subject. Calories matter but they are not everything, fats and carbohydrates are not enemies in themselves, supplements are neither a magic solution nor an absolute waste, and thinness is not health itself. And the most important lesson from the report: treat circulating advice with caution, and do not adopt any diet or supplement just because it is promoted.
