“Forever chemicals” in water and cookware linked to more aggressive breast cancer? What a 2026 US study found

Share this article:

A glass of water every morning... and chemicals that never leave

Every morning she fills a glass from the tap, cooks in a pan that nothing sticks to, and wears a coat that keeps the rain out. Ordinary habits — but they share one thing: chemicals made to last. They resist water, grease and heat, and they resist breaking down too. Because they break down so slowly, they are called “forever chemicals”, and they accumulate in the environment and in our bodies.

A new US study, published in the Journal of Clinical Oncology on 5 November 2026, found a link between two of these chemicals and a type of breast cancer considered among the most aggressive. Which two chemicals? What did the numbers actually say? And what have they not said yet?

What are “forever chemicals”?

Forever chemicals, or PFAS, are a family of thousands of man-made compounds, used for decades in non-stick cookware, stain-resistant furniture and carpets, waterproof clothing, some types of food packaging, personal care products and firefighting foam. They enter the body mainly through drinking water and food, and they stay in it for years — they are measurable in the blood of most people in industrialised countries.

Two of the best-known are older chemicals: perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS). They have been gradually phased out in many countries, but they are still present in the environment and in people’s bodies, because they break down so slowly — and that is exactly what makes their “forever” nature a health concern.

What exactly did the study do?

The researchers drew on the “Sister Study”, a large US project led by the National Institutes of Health, which enrolled 50,884 women aged 35 to 74 in the United States and Puerto Rico between 2003 and 2009. Each participant had a sister who had been diagnosed with breast cancer at enrolment, but the participants themselves did not have it.

The researchers measured 17 PFAS in blood samples collected years before diagnosis, from 1,609 women who later developed breast cancer, and from a comparison sample of 2,269 women who did not. Because estrogen-receptor-negative cancer is less common, the researchers oversampled it — 324 estrogen-receptor-negative cases versus 1,120 estrogen-receptor-positive cases — to sharpen the analysis. They then compared the risk of disease between the women with the highest blood levels (the top quarter) and the lowest (the bottom quarter) for each chemical.

The numbers: 67% and 93% — what do they really mean?

Among women in the top quarter for PFOA, the rate of estrogen-receptor-negative breast cancer was 67% higher than among women in the bottom quarter (hazard ratio 1.67, 95% confidence interval 1.10 to 2.53). For PFOS the gap was larger: 93% higher, almost twice as high (hazard ratio 1.93, confidence interval 1.15 to 3.22).

And here we must pause on the words “higher by”: these are relative differences between two groups, not the chance of any one woman developing the disease. If the underlying risk is low, raising it by 67% still leaves it relatively low. The study gave no figure for individual risk, and no woman can conclude from a chemical level in her blood that she will or will not develop cancer.

The study found no clear link between the two chemicals and breast cancer overall — the link appeared only with the estrogen-receptor-negative type. A meta-analysis of seven earlier studies, which had also measured PFAS in blood before diagnosis, supported the same finding but more weakly: a pooled relative risk of 1.30 for PFOA and 1.20 for PFOS.

Why do scientists worry about this type in particular?

Estrogen-receptor-negative breast cancer is a tumour whose cells do not respond to the hormone estrogen, so the hormone treatments that help with other types do not work for it. It tends to grow faster, spread earlier and come back after treatment, and it is linked to less favourable health outcomes. Known risk factors for it are also fewer than for other types — which is why any possible environmental factor draws special attention.

The study’s lead investigator at the National Institutes of Health, Alexandra White, said the findings, together with the meta-analysis of earlier studies, provide evidence that the two chemicals are linked to a higher risk of this aggressive type of cancer, which has few known risk factors. But she stressed at the same time that more research is needed, especially on the newer replacement chemicals.

Read the numbers carefully: what did the study not prove?

This is where a good article differs from an exaggerated one. First, this is an observational study: it found an association, and it did not prove that the two chemicals cause cancer. Blood PFAS levels may be linked to other factors — living near a source of pollution, dietary habits, or genetic factors — that were not all separated out.

Second, exposure was measured once, from a blood sample collected years before diagnosis, and levels change over time and as sources of exposure change. The study also does not identify the source of exposure — water, food or household products. So no one can tell from this study alone which source lay behind a high level.

Third, some of the figures themselves sit on the edge: the statistical trend for PFOA did not reach the usual threshold for significance (P for trend 0.09), and in the meta-analysis the confidence interval for PFOS included the value 1 (0.91 to 1.58), meaning the result is weaker than the 93% figure suggests. Indeed, a third chemical from the same family (PFTrDA) showed an inverse association — lower, not higher, risk — a reminder that the picture is not uniform.

Fourth, the participants were not ordinary women: each had a sister with breast cancer, so their underlying risk is higher than that of women in general, and the figures cannot be generalised to everyone as they stand. And fifth, the study focused on the two older chemicals that have been gradually phased out, while experts know very little about the shorter-chain replacement chemicals that have taken their place — a limitation the lead researcher herself acknowledged.

Tip

There is no need to panic over a glass of water, no routine blood test is recommended to measure these chemicals, and no treatment flushes them from the body. But reducing exposure is possible with simple steps: follow the drinking-water quality reports for your area, consider a filter certified to remove PFAS if your local water is known to be contaminated, use scratched or ageing non-stick pans less, avoid heating food in grease-resistant packaging, and cut back on stain-resistant products and waterproofing sprays where you can do without them. And most important of all: do not skip regular breast checks and mammography as your doctor recommends, especially if you have a family history — early detection is what truly saves lives, not worrying about a chemical you cannot see. If you have a strong family history of breast cancer, talk to your doctor about the follow-up plan that suits you.

The bottom line: an association worth attention, not a final verdict

This is the largest study of its kind to measure PFAS in blood before diagnosis, and its results — backed by a meta-analysis of earlier studies — point to two older “forever chemicals” being linked to a higher risk of an aggressive type of breast cancer. But it is a signal of association, not proof of causation, with relative rather than individual figures, in a selected group of women, and about chemicals that have been gradually phased out while their replacements remain of unknown effect. So the practical message is not to fear every glass of water, but to pay attention to the quality of the water and the products around us, to keep up with regular screening — and to wait for more research to determine whether this link is a real cause or merely a signal worth following up.
