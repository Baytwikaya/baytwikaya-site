First mRNA flu vaccine enters commercial use: what did the trial reveal about its effectiveness?

Share this article:

Flu changes every year — and vaccines try to keep up

The flu virus does not stay the same: it changes from one season to the next, which is why scientists must update the vaccine formula every year to match the strains in circulation. The big news this season is that the first flu vaccine built on messenger RNA (mRNA) technology — the same technology the world came to know through the COVID vaccines — is entering commercial use for the first time, in the 2026–2027 season.

The vaccine is called mFlusiva, scientific name mRNA-1010, from Moderna, and the US Food and Drug Administration has approved it for adults aged 50 and over. So how does this vaccine work? What did the large trial published in the New England Journal of Medicine find? And is it really better than the traditional flu vaccines?

How does an mRNA flu vaccine work?

The idea is simple at its core: the vaccine contains messenger RNA (mRNA) molecules carried inside tiny lipid nanoparticles, with instructions for the body’s cells to produce the hemagglutinin (HA) protein — the protein found on the surface of the flu virus. Once the cells produce this protein, the immune system recognises it and makes antibodies against it, so it is ready to fight the real virus if it enters the body later.

Three points are important to make clear: the vaccine contains no live flu virus, the mRNA molecules break down after doing their job, and they do not change human DNA. The 2026–2027 formula is trivalent, covering three strains: A/H1N1, A/H3N2 and B/Victoria. The vaccine is given as a single intramuscular dose each season.

What did the trial find? And how should we read the 26.6% figure?

The key study was published in the New England Journal of Medicine (NEJM) on 6 May 2026, and it included more than 40,000 participants: 20,179 received the mRNA vaccine, while 20,124 received a standard-dose inactivated vaccine, and researchers followed them for about 6 months. The result: lab-confirmed symptomatic flu cases numbered 411 with the mRNA vaccine versus 557 with the traditional vaccine — 2% versus 2.8% — a relative risk reduction of 26.6%.

An important clarification, so the figure is not misunderstood: 26.6% does not mean the vaccine protects only 26.6% of recipients. It means the risk of symptomatic flu was 26.6% lower than with the traditional vaccine. Both vaccines offer protection — the mRNA vaccine simply reduced the risk further in this trial.

Who is it approved for? And why is age 65 different?

The US Food and Drug Administration approved the vaccine for adults aged 50 and over, but with two different types of approval: standard approval for ages 50–64, and accelerated approval for people aged 65 and over, with a post-marketing study required that compares the vaccine with a high-dose vaccine. The reason is that older adults are the most at risk of severe flu complications, and high-dose vaccines are the established standard for them — so the open question is whether the mRNA vaccine also beats those high-dose vaccines.

For people under 50, the vaccine is not licensed yet, and the available data on pregnancy and breastfeeding are limited. This clarification matters: not being licensed does not mean it has been proven unsafe — it means the available data are not yet enough to judge.

Side effects: more common, but mostly mild

Side effects were more common with the mRNA vaccine than with the traditional vaccine, but most were mild to moderate and temporary: injection-site pain, fatigue, headache, muscle and joint pain, chills, and swelling or pain in the armpit lymph nodes.

The vaccine is contraindicated in case of a severe allergy to one of its components, or a previous severe allergic reaction to a dose of it. As with any vaccine, anyone with a history of severe allergy should talk to their doctor before being vaccinated.

The race against virus mutations: why does mRNA technology matter?

The biggest advantage scientists see in mRNA technology is the speed of updating: when the virus changes, the vaccine formula can be adjusted far more quickly than traditional manufacturing, which takes months. But this point should not be overstated: a faster formula update does not mean a new vaccine can be distributed immediately — quality, safety and efficacy checks, and regulatory approvals, are still needed before any updated vaccine reaches people.

There are also important caveats to state plainly. First, the main comparison in the trial was against a standard-dose vaccine, not the high-dose vaccines intended for older adults. Second, questions remain open about cost, availability in different countries, storage and transport, and whether the advantage holds across different seasons. And third — most importantly — the traditional approved flu vaccines remain effective options, and nobody should skip their annual flu shot while waiting for a new vaccine.

Advice

If you are in an age group covered by the flu vaccine, ask your doctor or pharmacist which vaccine suits you best this season, and do not delay vaccination while waiting for a new vaccine that may not yet be available in your country. The approved traditional vaccine still offers effective protection, and the annual flu shot matters most for older adults, people with chronic conditions, and those who live with them.

Conclusion

The first mRNA flu vaccine entering commercial use is an important step in the race between vaccines and a constantly changing virus: a large trial of more than 40,000 participants showed a 26.6% lower risk of symptomatic flu compared with a standard-dose traditional vaccine. But the comparison was not against the high-dose vaccines for older adults, and questions about cost, availability and performance across seasons remain open. It is real progress — not the end of the race.
