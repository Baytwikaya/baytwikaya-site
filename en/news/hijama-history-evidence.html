Cupping Through History and Modern Evidence

Share this article:

An ancient practice that crossed civilisations

Cupping is one of the oldest health practices in human history. Traces of it are found in ancient Egypt thousands of years ago, it was practised in China within traditional medicine, and it was known to the Greeks and Romans and spread in the Islamic world, where it is mentioned in the prophetic tradition with its known points. In each civilisation it took a slightly different form, but the basic idea remained the same: placing cups on the skin and creating suction in them.

This guide follows cupping's journey through history, explains how ancient civilisations understood it, how it moved into modern medicine, and what scientific studies today say about its effectiveness and safety, so we can distinguish between a beautiful history, strong scientific evidence, and a mere claim that still needs proof.

Cupping in Egypt, China and Greece

In ancient Egypt, pictures and tools resembling cups appear in old medical papyri, and practitioners used animal horns and wooden and ceramic cups. In China, cupping became part of the traditional medicine system linked to the flow of energy in the body, using bamboo and glass cups, and it is still practised there today in clinics and hospitals.

In Greece, Hippocrates, the father of medicine, spoke about it and linked it to the theory of the four humours that prevailed at the time — the idea that health depends on the balance of the body's fluids. This theory passed into Arab and Islamic medicine, where physicians developed it and wrote about the points and timings of cupping, including Ibn Sina in his book The Canon of Medicine.

Cupping in the Islamic tradition

In the Islamic tradition, cupping holds a special place: reports encourage it and mention particular points for it, such as the sides of the neck and the upper back, and mention preferred days for it in the lunar month — the seventeenth, nineteenth and twenty-first days. Muslims practised it through the centuries as part of prophetic and traditional medicine, and these days and points are still present in the practice of many practitioners in Morocco and the Islamic world today.

It is important to understand this tradition in its context: it is a religious and cultural heritage that people respect and trust, but it is not a substitute for modern scientific assessment of effectiveness and safety. Respecting the tradition does not mean accepting every therapeutic claim without evidence, just as weak scientific evidence does not mean denying the cultural and religious value of the tradition for those who believe in it.

From wooden cups to modern clinics

Cupping tools have developed greatly: from animal horns and wooden and ceramic cups to glass cups and then single-use plastic cups, and from suction by fire to manual and electric suction pumps. Hygiene and sterilisation rules have also developed, so sterile single-use cups, gloves and skin disinfection are now basics of safe practice in modern clinics.

In recent decades, cupping has entered complementary medicine programmes in some countries, associations and training centres have been created for it, and scientific studies have begun testing it with modern research methods. In Morocco, cupping is practised in specialised centres, and people seek it for back and joint pain, headaches and other complaints, which is why the need for accurate information about it is greater than ever.

What do modern studies say?

When we review modern scientific studies on cupping, we find a mixed picture. There are small studies suggesting improvement in pain in conditions such as back, neck and knee pain and headache, and there are systematic reviews that gather these studies and conclude that the results are promising but not conclusive. The reason is that most studies are small, short, and differ in cupping method, number of sessions and points, and many do not compare cupping with a placebo or another treatment.

Many studies were also conducted in particular countries and published in small journals, and their results have not been repeated in large independent trials. Health bodies therefore remain cautious: cupping may help some people feel temporary improvement in pain, but it is not a proven treatment for any specific disease, and there is no strong evidence that it treats or prevents chronic diseases.

Why is it hard to prove cupping's effectiveness scientifically?

It is hard to design a perfect trial for cupping: the participant knows they are receiving cupping because the cups leave clear marks, so it is hard to hide the treatment from them, and this makes the expectation effect strong. Cupping is also usually linked to rest, care and conversation with the practitioner, all factors that can improve the feeling of pain without cupping itself being the cause.

There is also difficulty in standardising the method: the number of cups, the points, the duration of suction and the number of sessions differ from one practitioner to another, making results hard to compare. Researchers therefore need larger and longer trials, with standardised methods and good control groups, before it can be said with confidence that cupping is effective for a particular condition. Until then, we must speak about it honestly: promising but unproven.

Tip

When you read about a cupping study, ask three questions: how many participants were there? Was it compared with a control group? And how long was the follow-up? The answers reveal whether the evidence is strong or weak.

How to read news or a study about cupping

When you read news saying a study has proven cupping effective, ask: how many participants were there? Was there a control group? How long was the follow-up? Was the study published in a peer-reviewed scientific journal? Have other researchers repeated the result? If the study was small and short and had no control group, it is an interesting start, but not proof.

Avoid exaggerated headlines that promise a cure for serious diseases, and remember that a single study does not change medicine; it is the long accumulation of good studies that changes it. If you are ill, consult your doctor before basing your decision on a single news story.

Cupping in Morocco today

In Morocco today, cupping is practised mainly in specialised centres, and people seek it for back and joint pain, headaches and other complaints, as mentioned earlier. The form of this practice has changed a great deal from the past, when it was usually carried out at home or in a place that was not equipped, with instruments that were reused and suction by fire, without a systematic question about health and medicines and without clear hygiene rules. Modern centres, by contrast, work in a clean and suitable place, use sterile single-use plastic cups and gloves, disinfect the skin before the session, ask about your health and medicines before starting, and explain to you the type of cupping, the points, the number of cups and the expected risks.

This development is not limited to the tools; it also covers the way the practice is organised: associations and training centres for cupping have been created in some countries, and a good practitioner now knows their limits and refers to a doctor when a case goes beyond their expertise, instead of promising a cure for every illness. In Morocco, the reader's role remains important: choose a clean centre that asks about your health, explains its choices and uses single-use instruments, ask about training and experience before the session, and consult your doctor when your condition is chronic or serious. Modern practice does not erase the tradition, but it adds to it what was not available in the past: sterilisation, record-keeping and medical caution, and it is these additions that make a session safer.

The bottom line: a rich history, and evidence still in its early stages

Cupping is an ancient practice that crossed civilisations from Egypt, China and Greece to the Islamic world, and its tools and rules developed until they reached modern clinics. Modern scientific evidence says it may relieve pain temporarily in some people, but it has not yet been proven as a treatment for any specific disease, and the available studies are small and short. Respect the history, ask for the evidence, consult your doctor, and do not let cupping delay a diagnosis or medical treatment.
