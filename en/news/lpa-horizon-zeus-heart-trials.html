Two heart drugs cut the numbers but not the heart attacks: what the HORIZON and ZEUS trials found

Share this article:

When the lab numbers improve but the patient does not

Imagine a patient who has already had a heart attack, takes his medicines faithfully, and then hears from his doctor that an important blood test has improved a great deal thanks to a new drug. He leaves reassured, believing his risk of a second heart attack has fallen by the same amount. But the truth revealed by two large trials presented at the American Heart Association Scientific Sessions in Chicago on 7 November 2026, and published in the New England Journal of Medicine the same day, was sobering: the numbers improved dramatically — while heart attacks and strokes did not fall at all.

The two trials are HORIZON, which tested the drug pelacarsen to lower lipoprotein(a), and ZEUS, which tested the anti-inflammatory drug ziltivekimab. What did each drug do? And why did beautiful numbers not translate into real protection?

What is lipoprotein(a) — and why do doctors worry about it?

Lipoprotein(a), written Lp(a), is a fatty particle in the blood that resembles LDL cholesterol, but is stickier and clings more to artery walls. Its level in the blood is mostly set by genes: it rises or falls according to inheritance, is barely affected by diet or exercise, and is lowered only slightly by standard cholesterol drugs such as statins.

Doctors worry about it because studies have linked high levels to a higher risk of heart attacks, strokes and narrowing of the heart valves. So it seemed logical that lowering it with a powerful drug might protect the heart. That very hypothesis is what the HORIZON trial tested.

The HORIZON trial: a big drop in the marker, no result for patients

In the HORIZON trial, about 8,300 adults with established cardiovascular disease and a high lipoprotein(a) level — 70 mg/dL or more — received either a monthly injection of pelacarsen (from Novartis) or a placebo, and were followed for a median of 4.7 years.

The drug did what it was designed to do: lipoprotein(a) fell by 78.4% after one year. But the outcome that matters to patients did not move: a heart attack, stroke, cardiovascular death or urgent coronary procedure occurred in 12.3% of those on the drug, versus 12.1% of those on placebo — effectively no difference, with a hazard ratio of 1.02 (95% confidence interval 0.90 to 1.15).

There was another worrying point: about 21% of participants stopped the drug because of side effects, compared with only 7.5% on placebo. After these results, Novartis ended the pelacarsen heart programme.

The ZEUS trial: inflammation fell, clots did not

The ZEUS trial started from a different idea: that chronic inflammation is what drives heart disease. Researchers tested ziltivekimab, an antibody that targets the inflammatory protein interleukin-6, from Novo Nordisk, in more than 6,300 adults with artery disease and chronic kidney disease with raised inflammation (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein of 2 mg/L or more), followed for a median of 33 months.

Here too, the drug hit its stated target: the inflammation marker fell by 64 percentage points more than with placebo. But the clinical result was almost identical: cardiovascular death, heart attack or stroke occurred in 17.6% of those on the drug, versus 17.7% on placebo, a hazard ratio of 0.99 (95% confidence interval 0.88 to 1.11).

Serious infections, on the other hand, were more common with the drug: 20.4% versus 17.9% with placebo — an expected price for a drug that dampens the immune system, but a real price paid by patients.

Why the gap between a marker and real risk matters

These two trials are a reminder of a golden rule in medicine: improving a number in a blood test is not the same as lowering a patient's risk. Doctors call these numbers "surrogate markers" — such as cholesterol level or an inflammation marker — and they are useful when they translate into real benefit, but they can mislead when they do not. The reason may be that the target itself is not the main driver of the disease, or that the intervention came too late after the arteries were already damaged, or that side effects cancelled out the benefit. Researchers are still studying these questions, and they are not yet settled.

None of this means lipoprotein(a) is innocent, or that inflammation does not matter. Lipoprotein(a) remains a recognised inherited risk factor, and other drugs working through different mechanisms are still in trials. But the lesson is clear: a heart drug should not be adopted merely because it improved a lab number, but only if it proves that it prevents heart attacks and strokes.

Tip

If you have heart or artery disease, do not stop any of your current medicines because of these results — they do not mean your treatment is not working, only that two new drugs added no extra protection. Ask your doctor whether measuring lipoprotein(a) would be useful in your case, as knowing it helps to understand your inherited risk. What protects your heart today is what trials have proven: controlling blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar, quitting smoking, moving every day, and sticking to your prescribed treatment — and talk to your doctor before any change to your medicines.

The bottom line: a better number does not mean a safer heart

At the American Heart Association meeting in 2026, two promising drugs achieved a large laboratory success and a clear clinical failure at the same time: pelacarsen cut lipoprotein(a) by 78.4%, and ziltivekimab cut inflammation by 64 percentage points, yet the rates of heart attacks, strokes and cardiovascular deaths stayed almost exactly the same in both trials. It is a real disappointment for researchers and patients — but also a protection for patients: thanks to these large trials, expensive drugs will not be prescribed merely because they improve a lab number, and the decisive question will remain the same as ever — does the drug actually prevent a heart attack?
