Sciatica: the complete guide to causes, symptoms and treatment

Share this article:

What is sciatica?

Sciatica is not a disease in itself, but the name for a set of symptoms that occur when the sciatic nerve is irritated or compressed by something. The sciatic nerve is the longest and thickest nerve in the body: it starts in the lower back, passes through the hip and buttocks, and runs down the leg to the foot. When this nerve is compressed or inflamed, the person feels pain spreading from the lower back or buttocks into the leg, usually in one leg only. The pain may be accompanied by tingling, numbness, or weakness in the leg or foot muscles. Sciatica differs from ordinary lower back pain because the pain spreads along the path of the nerve and may reach below the knee and sometimes to the foot. Sciatica affects many people at some point in their lives, and it often improves within weeks with self-care and appropriate treatment, without the need for surgery.

In this guide we explain the causes of sciatica, its symptoms and the warning signs that require urgent care, how a doctor diagnoses it, the treatments that genuinely help, the exercises and positions that ease the pain, and steps to prevent it from coming back.

What causes sciatica?

The most common cause is a herniated disc, where part of the disc between the vertebrae slips out of place and presses on a nerve root. Other causes include lumbar spinal stenosis, a narrowing of the channel through which the nerves pass, which is common in older people. Sciatica can also result from piriformis syndrome, in which a deep muscle in the buttocks presses on the sciatic nerve. Less common causes include an injury or fracture of the spine, a tumour pressing on the nerve, or arthritis. Risk factors include increasing age, as discs and vertebrae wear with time; work that involves heavy lifting, prolonged sitting or driving long distances; excess weight, which increases pressure on the spine; diabetes, which can damage nerves; and lack of physical activity and weak back and abdominal muscles. In Morocco, sciatica is common among people doing hard manual work or sitting for long hours in transport or in front of a computer without enough movement.

Symptoms and warning signs

The main symptom is pain that starts in the lower back or buttocks and spreads into the leg, usually on one side. The pain may be sharp like an electric shock, burning or tingling, and it worsens with coughing, sneezing, prolonged sitting or bending. It may be accompanied by numbness or tingling in the leg or foot, and weakness in the leg muscles that makes lifting the foot difficult. The pain varies from day to day and may ease and then return. There are warning signs that require urgent medical care without delay: loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness in the area between the legs, sudden severe weakness in the leg, pain after an accident or a fall, pain accompanied by fever, or pain that does not improve over time. These signs may indicate severe pressure on the nerves needing immediate assessment. You should also see a doctor if the pain lasts more than a few weeks despite self-care, or if it prevents you from sleeping and working.

How does a doctor diagnose sciatica?

Diagnosis begins by asking about the pain, where it spreads and how severe it is, followed by a physical examination: the doctor tests muscle strength, reflexes and sensation in the legs, and may ask you to lie down and raise the straight leg, a test that triggers sciatic pain when the nerve is irritated. In most cases no imaging is needed at first, because the condition usually improves within weeks. But if the pain persists, if muscle weakness appears, or if the doctor suspects another cause, an MRI may be ordered — the best way to see discs and nerves — or a CT scan, or a nerve and muscle study. It is important for the patient to know that imaging results alone are not enough: scans can show a herniated disc in people who feel no pain at all, so the doctor always connects the images with the symptoms and the clinical examination before making any treatment decision.

Treatment: what genuinely helps?

Most cases of sciatica improve within weeks without surgery. In the first days, painkillers and anti-inflammatory medicines prescribed by a doctor or pharmacist help, along with cold or warm compresses on the lower back. In some cases the doctor may prescribe muscle relaxants or medicines for nerve pain, and no medicine should be taken at a dose or for a duration decided on one's own. As for bed rest, modern medical guidance advises against it: staying as active as possible while avoiding movements that worsen the pain is best. Physiotherapy plays a major role: stretching and strengthening exercises for the back, abdominal and hip muscles, explained by a specialist and followed up, help relieve pressure on the nerve and prevent the pain from returning. A corticosteroid injection around the nerve may help in some cases that do not improve, reducing inflammation and pain for a time. Surgery is usually suggested only if severe pain persists despite conservative treatment, or if increasing weakness or warning signs appear, and the doctor decides it with the patient after discussing benefits and risks. Be cautious of unproven treatments that promise rapid healing: there is no single treatment that removes sciatica instantly.

Exercises and positions that ease the pain

Gentle exercises help relieve pressure on the sciatic nerve. One of the best known is the knee-to-chest stretch: lie on your back, slowly pull one knee towards your chest and hold it for a few seconds, then switch legs. The piriformis stretch: lie on your back, place the ankle of the affected leg on the knee of the other leg, and pull the thigh gently towards you until you feel a stretch in the buttocks. And the cat-cow exercise: on hands and knees, arch your back upwards and then downwards slowly. Practise these exercises daily, calmly, and stop if the pain increases sharply. In daily life, take short, frequent walks during the day, avoid prolonged sitting, and when you sit, keep your back straight and your feet on the floor; avoid lifting heavy weights or bending suddenly, and when lifting something, bend your knees, not your back. Sleeping on your side with a pillow between the knees brings relief to many people, as does sleeping on your back with a pillow under the knees. Consult your doctor or physiotherapist before starting exercises, especially if the pain is severe or accompanied by weakness.

Tip

During a sciatica flare-up, keep moving lightly and avoid complete bed rest: short, frequent walks during the day are better than lying down all day, unless your doctor advises otherwise.

Preventing sciatica from coming back

After a flare-up improves, prevention remains the best treatment. Keep up regular physical activity: daily walking, swimming, and exercises that strengthen the abdominal and back muscles all support the spine and reduce pressure on the discs. Maintain a healthy weight, because excess weight increases the load on the lower back. Pay attention to your posture: sit with a straight back, take movement breaks every hour if your work is at a desk, and adjust your car seat so it supports your lower back. Avoid smoking, as smoking weakens the nourishment of the discs and slows their healing, according to medical sources. In manual work, learn the correct lifting technique and ask for help with heavy loads. If the pain returns, start self-care early and see a doctor if it does not improve within a few weeks: early intervention shortens the flare-up and prevents it from becoming a chronic problem.

The bottom line: sciatica is painful but usually treatable

Sciatica is a troublesome pain that follows the path of the sciatic nerve, but most cases improve within weeks without surgery, with light movement, physiotherapy and proper care. Remember the warning signs that require urgent care, and do not hesitate to seek medical attention if the pain persists or weakness increases. After recovery, stay active and keep your back muscles strong — they are the best protection against the pain returning.
