When Is Cupping Unsafe? Contraindications and Warnings

Share this article:

Cupping is not suitable for everyone

Cupping is an ancient practice, widespread in Morocco and in many countries, and people seek it to relieve pain and improve their general sense of well-being. But its popularity does not mean it is safe for everyone and in all circumstances. It is a procedure that affects the skin and may involve drawing blood, so it has clear contraindications and real risks that everyone considering it should know.

This guide explains frankly when cupping is unsafe, who should avoid it, what the common and rare side effects are, how to choose a safe practitioner, and what questions to ask before a session. A safe decision starts with correct information, not with enthusiasm or fear.

What is the difference between dry and wet cupping?

Dry cupping involves placing cups on the skin and creating suction without any cut or bloodletting, and it is the less invasive of the two types. Wet cupping involves, after suction, lightly scratching the surface of the skin and then replacing the cups to draw a small amount of blood, so it carries greater risks, such as bleeding, infection and slow wound healing.

This difference matters when assessing safety: some contraindications apply specifically to wet cupping, such as taking blood-thinning medicines or having a bleeding disorder, while hygiene and sterilisation rules remain essential for both types. If the practitioner does not explain which type will be used and why, that in itself is a reason to stop and ask.

Who should avoid cupping entirely?

There are situations in which cupping, especially wet cupping, must be avoided without debate. These include bleeding disorders such as haemophilia, taking blood-thinning medicines such as warfarin or newer anticoagulants, severe anaemia, and a low platelet count. Pregnant women should also avoid it, especially on the abdomen and lower back, as should young children, frail older people, and people with an active skin disease where the cups would be placed.

Great caution is also needed for people with uncontrolled diabetes or slow wound healing, advanced heart or kidney disease, people taking medicines that suppress the immune system, and people with a history of repeated fainting or low blood pressure. In all these cases, the practitioner's approval alone is not enough; the treating doctor's opinion must be obtained first.

Common side effects and rare risks

Common effects after cupping include round red or purple marks on the skin that fade within days, mild pain or muscle soreness where the cups were placed, and some people feel brief dizziness, tiredness or mild nausea after the session, especially if they were fasting or had not drunk enough water. These effects usually disappear on their own within a day or two.

The less common but more serious risks include skin infection and abscess if the instruments are not sterile, prolonged bleeding in people with a bleeding disorder, burns if fire is used incorrectly to create suction, and worsening of some skin conditions such as eczema and psoriasis where the cups are placed. The medical literature has also reported rare cases of severe anaemia after repeated sessions, so the number of sessions and the interval between them deserve attention.

Warning signs after a session: when to seek help

After any session, watch yourself over the following days. Seek medical help without delay if bleeding does not stop, or if there is increasing swelling, severe redness and warmth at the site, discharge of pus, fever, severe pain that does not ease, strong dizziness and fainting, or shortness of breath. These signs may indicate infection, bleeding or a serious reaction.

Also contact a doctor without hesitation if you feel general weakness or severe paleness after wet cupping, or if the small cuts take more than a few days to heal. Note the date of the session, the practitioner's name and what was done, as this information helps the doctor assess your condition quickly if you need care.

How to choose a safe practitioner

A safe practitioner first asks about your health, your medicines and your condition before touching any instrument, explains the type of cupping, the points, the number of cups and the expected risks, and tells you when you should see a doctor instead of having cupping. They should use gloves and single-use or properly sterilised cups and instruments, wash their hands and clean your skin before the session, and work in a clean and suitable place.

Beware of a practitioner who promises to cure a serious illness, asks you to stop your medicines, refuses to answer your questions, uses unsterile instruments or reuses cups between people, or performs the session in an unclean place. These are clear danger signs, and staying away from them protects you. Remember that a good practitioner knows their limits and refers you to a doctor when your condition goes beyond their expertise.

Tip

Before any cupping session, write a list of your medicines and illnesses and show it to both your doctor and the practitioner, and never hide any blood-thinning medicine or chronic illness, however minor it seems.

A checklist before the session

Before you book an appointment, ask yourself these questions: have I consulted my doctor? Have I told the practitioner about all my medicines and illnesses? Is the place clean and are the instruments sterile and single-use? Has the practitioner explained the type of cupping, the points and the risks? Do I know what to do if I feel unwell after the session? If the answer to any of these questions is no, postpone the session until you have a clear answer.

After the session, drink water and eat a light meal, avoid heavy exertion on the same day, keep the treated areas clean, and do not apply any ointment or substance your doctor has not recommended. If you feel any concern, call your doctor: an early question is better than late treatment.

What happens after the session? Normal effects, warning signs and aftercare

After a session, most people experience familiar short-lived effects: round red or purple marks on the skin that fade within days, mild pain or muscle soreness where the cups were placed, and some feel brief dizziness, tiredness or mild nausea, especially if they were fasting or had not drunk enough water. These effects usually disappear on their own within a day or two, and they do not mean that the session succeeded or failed; they are an expected reaction of the skin and the body after suction and light scratching.

Warning signs are quite different, and you should seek medical help without delay if they appear: bleeding that does not stop, increasing swelling with severe redness and warmth at the site, discharge of pus, fever, severe pain that does not ease, strong dizziness and fainting, shortness of breath, general weakness and severe paleness after wet cupping, or small cuts that take more than a few days to heal. A good centre does not leave you after the session without instructions: it explains what is normal and what is not, asks you to drink water and eat a light meal, to avoid heavy exertion on the same day, to keep the treated areas clean, not to apply any ointment or substance your doctor has not recommended, and to contact a doctor if you have any concern. Note the date of the session, the practitioner's name and what was done, as this information helps the doctor assess your condition quickly if you need care.

The bottom line: safety before everything

Cupping is not suitable for everyone, and its contraindications are real: bleeding disorders, blood-thinning medicines, severe anaemia, pregnancy, active skin diseases, and others. Its risks range from temporary skin marks to infection and bleeding when sterilisation and experience are lacking. Consult your doctor first, choose a qualified, clean and honest practitioner who is clear about the limits of what they offer, and stop at once if any danger sign appears after a session.
