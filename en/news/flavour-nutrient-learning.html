Why coffee and chocolate start to taste better: your brain learns flavours from your gut

Share this article:

Why does your favourite coffee start to taste better over time?

The first time you tasted coffee, you may have found it bitter and unpleasant, and weeks later its smell alone is enough to make you feel comforted. The same happens with dark chocolate and foods rich in fat and sugar: the flavour itself has not changed, but your experience of it has. Now a new study published in Nature on 4 November 2026 has revealed the neural mechanism behind this shift: the brain does not learn flavours from the tongue alone, but from the stomach too — through a slow dopamine pathway that links what reaches the stomach to what you taste.

How does this pathway work? What did the experiments actually prove? And what have they not proved yet?

A slow message from the stomach to the brain

The study was carried out by a team at the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF), led by researcher Zachary Knight, and published under the title “The neural basis of flavour–nutrient learning”. The team found that nutrients reaching the stomach trigger sustained dopamine release — a neurotransmitter linked to reward and learning — in a brain area called the anterior basolateral amygdala (aBLA), starting about 48 seconds after nutrients reach the stomach.

That timing matters: taste signals reach the brain in fractions of a second, while this signal is slow and sustained, and comes from the gut after the body starts receiving nutrients. So the brain has two different channels: a fast one that says “this is the taste of coffee”, and a slow one that says “this food gave me energy” — and when they coincide, the brain learns to love the flavour.

The mouse experiment: preference jumps from 35% to 81%

To test the idea, the researchers gave mice two drinks with different flavours: one paired with glucose delivered directly into the stomach, the other paired with water. After six days of training, the mice’s preference for the glucose-paired flavour rose from about 35% to 81% of licks — the mice were no longer choosing the flavour for its taste alone, but because their brains had learned that it meant nutrients were coming.

The result is striking because the two drinks were almost identical in their outward flavour, and the only difference was what reached the stomach after drinking. The learning here was not about taste, but about the nutritional outcome that followed it.

Switching learning off and on: the strongest evidence

The team then tested causality directly. When the researchers silenced dopamine neurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) during training, the mice did not learn the preference at all — without the dopamine signal, the link between flavour and nutrients never formed. And conversely, when the researchers artificially stimulated dopamine fibres in the aBLA, the mice began to prefer a calorie-free flavour that had never been paired with any real nutrients.

These two tests together — blocking and stimulating — are what make the study strong: the first proves dopamine is necessary for the learning, the second proves it is sufficient on its own to create the preference, even without real energy.

And what about humans? Early signals from brain imaging

The study did not stop at mice. Brain imaging in 13 men, and then in a separate experiment with 38 adults, showed nutrient-related activity in the left amygdala — the human counterpart of the mouse aBLA. These human results are early and small in scale, but they suggest the same pathway may exist in us.

Even so, the gap remains wide between an activity seen in a brain scan and an everyday behaviour like loving coffee: the human study proves a signal exists, not that it alone explains all our eating habits.

An important clarification: the study did not test coffee or GLP-1 drugs

Here is the point that must be stated in complete honesty. The study did not test coffee or caffeine at all — it tested glucose and milkshake-like nutrients. Coffee is only an illustration given by senior author Zachary Knight, who said: “once your brain has learned to associate the flavour of your favourite brew with the uplift from caffeine, that coffee starts to taste a lot better”. Saying that “science has proved why you love coffee” goes beyond what the study actually did.

The same applies to GLP-1 obesity drugs: they were not tested in this study at all, and Knight’s suggestion that they might alter this learning is only a hypothesis that has not yet been tested. Anyone reading that these drugs “reprogram your taste” is reading a guess, not a result.

Tip

Use this learning to your advantage: taste is built by repetition, not by the first bite. If you want to love a new healthy food — vegetables or whole grains — pair it with a flavour you already like, and try it several times instead of judging it on the first taste. And watch the other side of the coin: the same mechanism is what makes foods rich in fat and sugar more attractive every time you eat them, because your brain links their flavour to fast energy. Repetition is a friend when you aim it at good food, and an opponent when you leave it to ultra-processed foods.

The bottom line: the brain learns to love what nourishes it

The 2026 Nature study reveals an important piece of the puzzle of taste: the stomach sends the brain, through dopamine, a slow message saying “this food gave me energy”, and the brain learns to love the flavour that came before it. The mouse experiments are strong — blocking learning and triggering it — and the early human results are encouraging. But the study’s limits are clear: it was done mainly in mice with simple nutrients, and it did not test coffee, caffeine or GLP-1 drugs. Coffee is a beautiful illustration of the idea, not proof of it — and the difference between an illustration and proof is the difference between a good article and an exaggerated one.
