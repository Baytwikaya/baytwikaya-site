Semaglutide extends mouse lifespan by 12%: are diabetes drugs nearing a way to slow ageing?

Share this article:

A diabetes drug that makes mice live longer… what is the story?

Semaglutide is a well-known drug for type 2 diabetes and obesity, sold under brand names many people recognise. But a new study from the University of California, Berkeley, places it in a very different context: researchers found that giving it daily to aged mice extended their lifespan by more than 12%, and improved their movement, memory and blood sugar control — even beating calorie restriction, the only intervention proven so far to extend lifespan in animals, on some measures.

The study was published in Nature on 2 September 2026, led by Danica Chen, professor of the biology of ageing at Berkeley, together with researcher Yufan Feng. The question the news raises today is not “will people live longer if they take the drug?”, but “what did the study actually find, and what are its limits?” — because the gap between mouse and human is large here, and the details are what matter.

What did the Berkeley researchers do?

The team used female C57BL/6 mice aged 20 months — roughly equivalent to 60 to 65 years in humans. They began giving semaglutide at a dose of 10 nanomoles per kilogram each day, and continued until each mouse died naturally, alongside a control group that received a placebo. Each group numbered around 40 mice.

The researchers did not only measure lifespan: they tracked movement, exploration, spatial memory and glucose control, and examined brain, muscle and liver tissue for signs of inflammation, cellular senescence and mitochondrial function — the cell’s power plants. They also compared the results with another group put on calorie restriction, to learn whether the drug merely mimics the diet or goes beyond it.

The key number: a 12.4% longer life

The median lifespan of the mice given semaglutide was 834 days, compared with 742 days in the control group — an increase of 92 days, or 12.4%. Food intake fell by about 24%, a known effect of the drug, but the improvements in memory, movement and glucose control were not simply the result of eating less, as we shall see.

The treated mice moved more and explored their surroundings more actively, performed better on spatial memory tests, and kept better control of their blood sugar. In their tissues, inflammation and markers of cellular senescence fell, mitochondrial function improved, levels of NAD+ — a molecule linked to energy production and cell repair — rose, and the formation of new neurons in the hippocampus, the brain’s memory region, improved.

It beat calorie restriction on memory and blood sugar

The comparison with calorie restriction is the most striking part of the study. Calorie restriction extends lifespan in animals, but it is hard to sustain in humans and sometimes comes with hunger and muscle loss. The researchers found that semaglutide beat calorie restriction at improving memory and glucose control, even though both groups lost a similar amount of weight.

That suggests the drug does not work only by reducing eating, but through broader biological pathways: lowering the chronic inflammation linked to ageing, improving mitochondrial function, and supporting the renewal of neurons. That finding is what made the study a talking point in scientific circles, because it opens the door to a drug that already exists and is used by millions of people, which may carry an effect beyond diabetes and obesity.

Read the result carefully: three caveats not to miss

First, the trial included females only, from a single strain, with around 40 mice per group. We do not know whether the same effect would appear in males, in other strains, or in humans. Second, critics — including ageing researcher Matt Kaeberlein — noted that the control group’s median lifespan was shorter than usual for this strain in other laboratories, which could mean the drug corrected an unusually short lifespan rather than slowing ageing itself. Third, there is no evidence to date that semaglutide extends human lifespan, and no clinical trial has been designed to test that.

Semaglutide is a prescription drug, with known side effects, especially on the digestive system, and it is not to be taken in order to live longer. The study is an important scientific step — but a step in mice, not a prescription.

Tip

Do not take a prescription drug for diabetes or obesity in order to live longer: there is no evidence for that in humans, and the drug has side effects. What studies have actually shown to slow ageing is what you already know: regular physical activity, enough sleep, a balanced diet, a healthy weight and medical follow-up. If you use semaglutide on prescription, follow your doctor’s instructions and do not change the dose on your own.

The bottom line: a real scientific promise, still in mice

The 2026 Nature study showed that daily semaglutide extended the lifespan of aged mice by 12.4%, improved their memory, movement and blood sugar, and beat calorie restriction on some measures, with improvements in inflammation, mitochondria and neuron renewal. But the trial used females of a single strain only, the control group lived shorter than usual, and there is no evidence of the effect in humans. The news is promising — but it is not an invitation to take the drug; it is an invitation to wait for the science.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the studies

  • Publication: Nature, 2 September 2026, vol. 657, pp. 469–476; DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-10940-7; first author Yufan Feng, team leader Danica Chen, University of California, Berkeley.
  • Animals: female C57BL/6 mice aged 20 months (roughly 60–65 years in humans), around 40 mice per group, semaglutide at 10 nanomoles per kilogram daily until natural death.
  • Lifespan: median lifespan 834 days versus 742 days in the control group — an increase of 92 days, or 12.4%.
  • Food: food intake fell by about 24%.
  • Improvements: better movement and exploration, better spatial memory and better glucose control; less inflammation and fewer markers of cellular senescence, better mitochondrial function, higher NAD+, and new neuron formation in the hippocampus.
  • Comparison with calorie restriction: semaglutide beat calorie restriction at improving memory and glucose control, despite similar weight loss.
  • Limitations: females only, a single strain, around 40 mice per group; the control group’s median lifespan was shorter than usual for this strain (critique by Kaeberlein and others); and no evidence that human lifespan is extended.
