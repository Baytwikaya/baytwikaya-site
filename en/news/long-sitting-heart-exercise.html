Long sitting harms the heart even if you exercise: what the Colorado twin study found

Share this article:

When the chair becomes a daily habit that ages the heart faster

His day starts sitting at a computer at work, continues sitting in the car on the way home, and ends sitting in front of a screen in the evening — and by bedtime he realises he spent most of his waking hours in a chair. This is no longer the exception; it has become the lifestyle of a whole generation. According to a study from the University of Colorado Boulder and the University of California Riverside, millennials now spend more than 60 hours a week sitting, between video meetings, streaming evenings and scrolling social media.

What surprised even the researchers is that this long sitting leaves its mark on the heart and metabolism even in young adults who exercise. Many people assume an evening workout “erases” a whole day of sitting, but the study — published in the journal PLOS ONE in September 2024 and back in the spotlight as it circulates in the Arabic press — says it is not that simple.

What did the researchers do? A twin study following participants since childhood

The researchers analysed data from more than 1,000 current or former Colorado residents, including 730 twins, aged 28 to 49, with an average age of about 33. The participants belong to the Colorado Adoption/Twin Study of Lifespan behavioral development and cognitive aging, known as CATSLife, which has followed twins and adopted individuals since childhood — allowing the researchers to look at sitting and movement habits in young adulthood, a critical period that predicts the risk of heart and metabolic disease later in life.

The strength of this study lies in the twins: identical twins share 100% of their genes, so when two twins differ in their sitting and exercise habits and their health markers differ too, it becomes easier to rule out genetics and focus on lifestyle. The study was led by Ryan Bruellman, a doctoral candidate in the Department of Genetics, Genomics and Bioinformatics at the University of California Riverside, under the supervision of Professor Chandra Reynolds, professor of psychology and neuroscience at the University of Colorado Boulder.

The researchers focused on two early markers of heart and metabolic aging: the ratio of total cholesterol to “good” cholesterol (HDL), and body mass index (BMI). The higher these two values, the more signs of early aging in the body.

Nine hours of sitting a day: what did the study find?

Participants reported sitting for almost 9 hours a day on average, with some sitting as much as 16 hours a day. In return, they did between 80 and 160 minutes of moderate physical activity a week, and less than 135 minutes of vigorous exercise a week. The researchers note that these figures are probably better than the national average in the United States, because Colorado residents are known for a more active lifestyle than most.

The main finding is clear: the more time people spent sitting, the “older” their heart and metabolic markers looked. Young adults who sat 8.5 hours a day and exercised at or below the current recommendations could fall into a “moderate to high risk” category for cardiovascular and metabolic disease, according to the researchers. In other words, the problem is not only for older people — it starts in the thirties, silently, without symptoms.

Exercise does not cancel the harm: twenty minutes a day is not enough

This is the point that overturns the common idea: meeting the minimum recommended amount of physical activity — about 20 minutes a day of moderate exercise, or about 150 minutes a week — was not enough to offset the risks of spending most waking hours seated. Adding a little moderate activity on top of a long day of sitting did little to buffer these effects.

The twin comparison even showed that replacing sitting time with exercise was better for cholesterol levels than simply adding exercise to a day spent sitting all the time. In other words, an evening workout alone does not make up for 9 hours of uninterrupted sitting; what matters is reducing the sitting itself, not only adding movement on top of it.

The good news: thirty minutes of vigorous exercise turns back the clock

But the study does not leave the reader without hope. Vigorous exercise — the kind that raises the heart rate and makes you breathe hard, such as running, cycling, fast swimming or walking uphill — had a real effect: people who did 30 minutes a day of vigorous exercise had cholesterol levels similar to those of less sedentary people five years younger, and a body mass index similar to that of people ten years younger.

The researchers still set a clear limit to this optimism: the benefit of vigorous exercise was partial and showed diminishing returns, and it did not fully cancel the harms of long sitting. The study’s conclusion, as written by its authors, is that vigorous activity compensates for part of the damage from sitting, but reducing sitting time remains paramount. As Professor Reynolds put it: “Our research suggests that sitting less throughout the day, getting more vigorous exercise, or a combination of both may be necessary to reduce the risk of premature aging in early adulthood.”

Bruellman sums up the mindset that makes young people ignore the risk: “Young adults tend to think they’re immune to aging. They think, ‘I don’t have to worry about this until I’m 50 or 60,’ but what they do in this critical period of their lives matters.”

Advice

Do not settle for a single evening workout while you sit for the rest of the day. Break up your sitting: stand and move for a few minutes every 30 to 60 minutes, walk during phone calls, take the stairs instead of the lift, and work standing for part of the day if you can. Add to that 30 minutes a day of vigorous exercise that raises your pulse — brisk uphill walking, running, cycling or swimming — and build up gradually if you are a beginner or have any health problem. Remember that prevention starts in your thirties, not in your sixties, and that asking your doctor to check your blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar is a simple step that reveals the risk before any symptoms appear.

The bottom line: sit less first, then make your exercise harder

The Colorado twin study says that long sitting — about 9 hours a day — speeds up the aging of the heart and metabolism even in thirty-somethings who exercise, and that the minimum amount of moderate exercise is not enough to offset it. The practical message is twofold: sit less throughout the day, and make part of your movement vigorous. The chair you sit on for eight hours is not neutral; according to this study, it is a silent risk factor, and preventing its harm needs no gym membership — just a simple habit: getting up and moving, again and again, every day.

Key facts: the essential numbers

  • Study size: more than 1,000 Colorado residents, including 730 twins; ages 28 to 49, average age about 33 (CATSLife study).
  • Sitting time: more than 60 hours a week for millennials; an average of about 9 hours a day, reaching 16 hours a day for some participants.
  • Participants’ activity: between 80 and 160 minutes of moderate exercise a week, and less than 135 minutes of vigorous exercise a week.
  • Risk threshold: sitting 8.5 hours a day while exercising at or below current recommendations could place a young adult in a “moderate to high risk” category for heart and metabolic disease.
  • The minimum is not enough: about 20 minutes a day of moderate exercise (about 150 minutes a week) does not offset the risks of long sitting.
  • Benefit of vigorous exercise: 30 minutes a day makes cholesterol levels similar to those of a person five years younger, and body mass index similar to that of a person ten years younger.
  • Measures used: total cholesterol to HDL cholesterol ratio (TC/HDL) and body mass index (BMI).
