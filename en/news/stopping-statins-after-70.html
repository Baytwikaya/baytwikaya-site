Stopping Statins After 70: Safe at Two Years, but Events Ran Slightly Higher

Share this article:

What did the STREAM trial find?

A large international clinical trial found that stopping statins in adults aged 70 and older who have never had cardiovascular disease was «noninferior» to continuing them for major cardiovascular events or death over two years, meaning the difference stayed within a prespecified acceptable margin. The results were presented on 7 November 2026 at the American Heart Association's Scientific Sessions in Chicago and published the same day in the New England Journal of Medicine, led by Professor Nicolas Rodondi of the University of Bern in Switzerland. The trial included 1,880 participants with a mean age of 77, 53% men, all living with at least two chronic conditions and taking a statin for at least a year for primary prevention, that is, with no previous heart disease. Participants were randomly assigned to two groups: 938 stopped their statin and 942 continued. The primary measure was the average event-free survival time over two years: 96.5 weeks in the stopping group versus 98.5 weeks in the continuing group, a gap of only two weeks, far inside the prespecified noninferiority margin of 5.2 weeks, so stopping was judged noninferior to continuing.

But the picture is more nuanced than that: in absolute numbers, events were slightly higher in the stopping group. Over two years, there were 100 cardiovascular events or deaths in the stopping group versus 77 in the continuing group, 10.7% versus 8.2%. Heart attacks occurred in 1.4% versus 0.6%, and ischaemic strokes in 1.9% versus 0.8%. Over a median follow-up of 103 weeks, the primary outcome occurred in 119 participants (12.7%) in the stopping group versus 87 (9.2%) in the continuing group, a hazard ratio of 1.38. The researchers estimated that stopping statins leads to one additional cardiovascular event or death over two years for every 40 people who stop treatment.

How was the trial done?

STREAM, whose full name is «Statin Discontinuation as Prevention among the Elderly and Multimorbid», ran at 24 centres in Switzerland, France and the Netherlands, enrolling participants between November 2021 and April 2025, funded by the Swiss National Science Foundation. All participants were 70 or older, had at least two chronic conditions, had no known cardiovascular disease, and 32.1% had diabetes. They were randomly assigned one-to-one to stop or continue, in an open-label design, meaning participants and doctors knew which group each person was in, with no placebo, although outcome assessment was blinded. The primary outcome was nonfatal heart attack, nonfatal ischaemic stroke or death from any cause, whichever came first. Importantly, about 14% of participants switched groups during the trial, but sensitivity analyses did not change the results. The researchers found no improvement in quality of life, muscle pain, falls or sarcopenia among those who stopped, while at least one serious adverse event occurred in 43.0% of the stopping group versus 39.8% of the continuing group.

What about the longer term?

The researchers also presented longer-term data, and that is where the greatest caution lies. Over four years, event rates rose in both arms but rose more in the stopping group. Nonfatal heart attacks at four years reached 1.6% in the stopping group versus 0.6% in the continuing group, after being under 1% in both groups at two years. This means the gap between the groups may widen over time, and two years of follow-up may not be enough for a final verdict on the safety of stopping. The data safety monitoring board also recommended that participants assigned to stop discuss restarting statin therapy with their care team, a recommendation that reflects the researchers' own caution. On the other hand, the trial is a reminder that statins are not free of cost: many older adults take numerous medicines, and each extra drug means potential side effects, interactions and a daily burden, especially with advancing age and multiple illnesses.

What do doctors say?

The researchers did not issue a general recommendation to stop statins for everyone after 70. Professor Rodondi presented the findings, while Dr Rita Redberg of the University of California, San Francisco, a senior co-author of the study, said that «discontinuation is safe for most people who are in this age group and without a previous CVD event», but stressed shared decision-making between patient and doctor. Rodondi added that the data support discussing stopping with suitable patients, not stopping treatment for everyone. This comes in the context of existing clinical practice: according to the study's discussion, 80% of general practitioners in 30 countries already advise stopping statins in people aged 80 and older without cardiovascular disease, especially when there are side effects or frailty. About a quarter of US adults have elevated LDL cholesterol, so the continue-or-stop decision concerns millions of people. It is worth noting that these results apply to primary prevention only, that is, people who have never had heart disease. For those who have had a heart attack or stroke, or a procedure on their heart arteries, statins remain essential, and the STREAM trial does not change that at all.

Tip

Never stop your statin on your own, even after 70. Discuss it with your doctor: stopping may suit some older adults who have never had heart disease, but the decision should be shared and take your full health and medicines into account.

What does this mean for you or your parents?

If you are over 70, take a statin and have never had heart disease, this study opens a conversation with your doctor, not a ready-made decision to stop. Ask your doctor about your personal risks: do you have diabetes or high blood pressure? Have you had statin side effects such as muscle pain? How many medicines do you take each day? Would you prefer to reduce the number of pills? And remember that stopping did not improve quality of life in the trial, that events were slightly higher in the stopping group, and that the gap may widen after two years. If you decide together to stop, make sure to have your cholesterol and blood pressure checked regularly, and agree with your doctor on a clear plan to restart the statin if new risk signals appear. But if you have had a heart attack or a stroke, or a procedure on your heart arteries, keep taking your treatment and never stop it without medical advice: this study does not apply to you.
