Swimming and Health: The Complete Guide

Share this article:

Why does this matter?

Swimming is one of the oldest physical activities humans have practiced, and one of the few that moves the whole body at once: the arms, legs, back, and abdomen, without the joints carrying the body's weight. The water carries you and resists your movement at the same time, gently strengthening your muscles and improving your fitness without impact.

Despite these advantages, many people hesitate to swim: some do not know how to swim at all, some are afraid of the water, and some think it is a sport for children or athletes only. The truth is that swimming suits most ages and conditions, it can be learned at any age, and it can be done at a very gentle pace. In this guide we explain the benefits of swimming, its types, how to start safely, and what mistakes to avoid.

Why is swimming different? The benefits of water

What makes swimming special is buoyancy: the water supports about ninety percent of the body's weight, relieving pressure on the knees, hips, and spine. That is why swimming is often recommended for people with joint pain or inflammation, for those recovering from an injury, or for people whose excess weight makes running and jumping painful.

At the same time, the water resists every movement you make, so your body works against natural resistance in every direction, and muscles grow stronger without weights and without impact. Swimming is also an excellent aerobic exercise: it raises the heart rate, strengthens the lungs, and improves breathing, because it teaches you to control inhaling and exhaling in a steady rhythm. In addition, water has a calming effect on many people, so swimming relieves stress and improves mood and sleep.

The proven benefits of swimming

Regular swimming is linked to wide-ranging benefits for the whole body:

  • Heart and lung health: it strengthens the heart muscle and improves lung capacity and breathing efficiency.
  • Muscle strength: it moves the muscles of the arms, legs, back, and abdomen in one complete workout.
  • Joint health: it is a low-impact exercise, suitable for people with knee or back pain.
  • Flexibility: the wide movements of swimming preserve the flexibility of the shoulders, hips, and spine.
  • Weight: it burns calories and helps maintain a healthy weight together with a balanced diet.
  • Balance and coordination: it improves balance and coordination between the limbs, which matters especially for older adults.
  • Mental health: it relieves stress and anxiety, and improves mood and sleep quality.

Expectations should stay realistic: swimming alone does not strengthen bones the way weight-bearing exercise does, such as walking and running, because the water reduces the load on the bones. It is therefore recommended to combine swimming with a weight-bearing activity, such as walking, for full bone benefit.

Types of swimming: which one suits you?

Each type of swimming has its own characteristics and benefits:

  • Breaststroke: the easiest to learn for most beginners, calm and good for relaxation.
  • Backstroke: excellent for people with neck or back pain, because the face stays out of the water and breathing is easier.
  • Front crawl: the fastest and most efficient, strengthening the arm and shoulder muscles.
  • Butterfly: the hardest and most demanding, needing good fitness and technique, and not suitable for beginners.
  • Walking in water and water exercises: an excellent alternative for people who cannot swim or who are recovering from an injury.

No stroke is best for everyone; the best is the one you enjoy and can practice regularly. You can vary between strokes to spread the effort across different muscles and avoid boredom.

How do you start swimming? A practical plan for beginners

If you cannot swim, start with lessons from a qualified instructor; it is the fastest and safest way. If you know the basics, start gradually:

  • First two weeks: two sessions a week, twenty minutes each, with rest breaks between laps.
  • Next two weeks: increase to thirty minutes, and gradually reduce the rest breaks.
  • After the first month: three sessions a week, thirty to forty-five minutes, at a pace that makes you breathe faster but still lets you talk.

Always start with a light warm-up outside the water or a slow walk in the water, and finish with slow swimming and light stretching. Use swimming goggles to protect your eyes from chlorine, and a swim cap if your hair is long. And remember that tiredness in the water comes silently: if you feel short of breath or very tired, stop and rest at the edge of the pool, and do not try to finish the lap by force.

Water safety: rules that allow no compromise

Swimming is safe when its rules are respected, and dangerous when they are ignored:

  • Never swim alone, especially in the sea or in a pool without a lifeguard.
  • Do not swim right after a heavy meal, or after drinking alcohol.
  • Learn the basics of safety and floating before learning speed.
  • In the sea, watch out for currents and waves, do not go far from the shore, and respect warning flags.
  • Drink water before and after swimming, because the body loses fluids in the water too, without noticing.
  • Shower before entering the pool and after, to protect your skin and eyes from chlorine.

If you have heart disease, epilepsy, or severe asthma, consult your doctor before starting swimming, always swim where there is a lifeguard, and tell the people with you about your health condition.

Common swimming mistakes

Some common mistakes reduce the benefit or cause pain:

  • Wrong breathing: holding your breath or lifting your head high strains the neck and breaks the rhythm; learn to breathe out under water and breathe in quickly above it.
  • Starting too hard: swimming many laps in the first session causes exhaustion and shoulder pain, so progress slowly.
  • Skipping the warm-up: entering cold water and swimming fast at once can cause muscle cramps.
  • Persisting with poor technique: repeating a wrong movement for weeks causes shoulder pain, so ask an instructor to correct your technique.
  • Swimming alone for your bones: as mentioned, swimming does not strengthen bones enough on its own, so add walking or resistance exercises.

Repeated shoulder pain in swimmers, especially in front crawl, is a sign worth attention: ease off, check your technique, and consult a specialist if the pain continues.

Tip

Start with two short sessions a week, twenty minutes each, with rest breaks between laps. Do not race anyone in the first month: the goal is to learn steady breathing and enjoy the water, and fitness will follow on its own.

Swimming in Morocco: the sea and the pool

Morocco is fortunate for swimming: it has long coastlines on the Atlantic Ocean and the Mediterranean Sea, and many cities have public and private pools. Sea swimming is a lovely experience, but it is harder than pool swimming: waves, currents, and cold make the effort greater, so start in the pool to learn technique, then move to the sea gradually, in safe and familiar places.

In summer, many Moroccans swim in the sea with their families, a healthy and social habit. But watch out for peak sun hours, use sunscreen, drink water regularly, and watch children at every moment, because drowning happens silently and quickly. If you swim in a pool, choose less crowded times if possible, so you can swim freely and with focus.

When should you see a doctor?

See a doctor before starting swimming if you have heart disease, shortness of breath on exertion, or chest pain, or if you are recovering from surgery or a shoulder or back injury, or if you have epilepsy or severe asthma. You should also stop swimming and seek help if you feel chest pain, dizziness, or severe shortness of breath while swimming. Apart from these situations, swimming remains a safe and beneficial activity for most people.

The bottom line: the water carries you, and you build your health

Swimming is a complete sport: it strengthens the heart, lungs, and muscles, protects the joints, calms the mind, and suits most ages. You do not need to be a skilled swimmer to start; you need a simple decision: two short sessions a week, gradual learning, and respect for the safety rules.
