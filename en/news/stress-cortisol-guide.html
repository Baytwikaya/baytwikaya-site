Stress and Cortisol: The Complete Guide to Understanding and Managing Stress Every Day

Share this article:

What is stress? And what is cortisol?

Stress is the body's natural response when we face a challenge, pressure or threat, whether it is an exam, a problem at work, an illness in the family or worrying news. In those moments the body releases hormones that help us focus and act quickly, the most important of which is cortisol, sometimes called the stress hormone. Cortisol is released by the adrenal glands, which sit above the kidneys. Its level rises in the morning to help us wake up and be active, then falls gradually during the day to its lowest level at night, which helps us sleep.

Cortisol itself is not an enemy of the body; it is an essential part of healthy balance. It helps regulate blood sugar, blood pressure, inflammation and the sleep-wake cycle, and it helps the body use energy from food. The problem is not the presence of cortisol, but its remaining high for a long time because of stress that never ends. Then it changes from a temporary helper into a daily burden that affects sleep, mood, immunity, weight, the heart and memory, and this guide explains that step by step.

How does stress work in the body?

When the brain senses a threat, it sends a fast signal to the adrenal glands to release adrenaline and cortisol. The heart beats faster, blood pressure rises, muscles tense and attention sharpens. This response is useful when it is short, because it helps a person escape danger, finish an urgent task or concentrate in a difficult situation. After the situation ends, the body gradually returns to its normal state, cortisol falls, muscles relax and breathing calms down.

Chronic stress happens when pressures continue for weeks or months without enough rest, such as constant pressure at work, financial problems, caring for a sick person or permanent worry about the future. In this case the body's alarm system stays switched on all the time, and the body never gets a chance to recover. Over time, constant tiredness, disturbed sleep, headaches, muscle pain, digestive problems and poor concentration may appear, and the desire for food rich in sugar and fat may increase, because the body is looking for quick energy and temporary comfort.

Signs of high stress to watch for

The signs of stress differ from person to person, but there are common signs worth noticing when they repeat or continue. Physical signs include frequent headaches, tension in the muscles of the neck, shoulders and back, disturbed sleep or repeated waking at night, tiredness despite sleeping, stomach or bowel upset, a faster heartbeat or a feeling of tightness in the chest, repeated colds, and changes in appetite and weight. Psychological signs include constant worry, quick irritability, difficulty concentrating, frequent forgetfulness, a feeling of exhaustion and loss of enthusiasm for things that used to be enjoyable.

It is important to note that these signs can have causes other than stress, so no single symptom should be interpreted as stress alone. If symptoms continue, get worse or affect work, relationships and sleep, it is wise to consult a doctor to rule out other health causes, such as a thyroid disorder, anaemia, high blood pressure or sleep problems. Severe stress can also be linked to anxiety or depression, both of which are treatable conditions, and asking for help with them is a step of strength, not weakness.

What raises cortisol? What lowers it?

Cortisol rises because of continuous psychological pressure, lack of sleep, chronic pain, illness, smoking, too much caffeine, lack of movement, irregular meals and some medicines. Waking up late and irregular sleep also disturb the natural rhythm of cortisol, so a person feels tired in the morning and tense in the evening. Repeated bad news, constant arguments and constant comparison with others on social media also increase the brain's sense of threat, even when there is no real danger.

On the other hand, cortisol gradually falls when the body gets what it needs in sleep, rest, movement, balanced food and supportive relationships. Regular sleep at night, a daily walk outdoors, slow deep breathing, time with family and friends, prayer or meditation or any quiet practice are all safety signals for the brain, telling it that the danger has ended. Eating regular meals rich in vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes and fish, and reducing sugary foods, soft drinks and processed foods, also helps the body restore its balance.

Practical steps to lower stress every day

The best way to lower stress is to build small, regular habits, not to look for one magic solution. Start with sleep: set a fixed time for sleeping and waking, stay away from screens for an hour before bed, and make the bedroom dark, quiet and fairly cool. Then add movement: a brisk walk for half an hour on most days of the week, climbing stairs or light stretching exercises, because movement helps the body use up stress hormones and improves mood and sleep. Do not forget breathing: take several slow, deep breaths several times a day, breathing in through the nose and out slowly through the mouth, because this simple method calms the heartbeat quickly.

Also organise your day in a realistic way: write tasks in order of priority, break a large task into small steps, say no to extra commitments when you are exhausted, and set aside time for rest just as you set aside time for work. Reduce caffeine in the evening and avoid smoking and alcohol, because they do not relieve stress but increase it in the long term. In Morocco, you can benefit from a walk in the early morning, from home meals rich in vegetables, legumes, olive oil and fish, and from sitting with family, all simple habits that support daily balance without cost.

Tip

Set aside ten minutes every day for a slow walk and deep breathing away from your phone, and you will notice an improvement in your sleep and mood within a few weeks.

What does not help? Common mistakes about cortisol

Much is claimed on the internet about cortisol, such as supplements, drinks or diets said to lower cortisol quickly, or home tests said to measure stress accurately. The truth is that cortisol levels change a lot during the day and are affected by sleep, food, illness and medicines, so the health of a person cannot be judged from a single number in a single test without a full medical assessment. Most supplements marketed to lower cortisol are not supported by strong evidence, and they may interact with other medicines or cause side effects, so they should not be taken without consulting a doctor or pharmacist.

Another common mistake is believing that all stress is bad, or that getting rid of it completely is possible. Stress is a natural part of life, and the goal is not to erase it but to learn to deal with it so that it does not control our health. Another mistake is relying on food, the phone or extra work to escape stress, because these methods bring temporary relief and then make the problem worse. Cupping, massage and relaxation may help some people feel comfortable and ease muscle tension, but they are not a treatment for chronic high cortisol, nor a substitute for sleep, movement and psychological care when needed, and the evidence for them is limited, so they should be seen as support for rest, not as a treatment for stress.

When should you see a doctor?

See a doctor if stress and anxiety continue for more than two weeks, or if they affect your sleep, work and relationships, or if you feel constant sadness or lose interest in things you used to love, or if persistent physical symptoms appear such as chest pain, shortness of breath, severe headache or an irregular heartbeat. These symptoms may be linked to stress, but they may also be a sign of another health problem that needs urgent assessment, especially chest pain and shortness of breath, so do not delay seeking medical care when they appear.

Also consult a doctor if you live with a chronic illness such as diabetes, high blood pressure or heart disease, because chronic stress can make these conditions harder to control. If your doctor prescribes a medicine or refers you to a mental health specialist, follow the plan and keep your appointments. If you take a medicine that contains cortisone or its derivatives, never stop it suddenly without consulting your doctor, because stopping it suddenly can be dangerous. Remember that seeking professional help is part of caring for yourself, and many people improve greatly when they get the right support.

The bottom line: stress can be managed

Cortisol is an essential hormone that helps us face challenges, but it becomes a problem when it stays high because of stress that never calms down. The good news is that stress can be managed with simple daily habits: regular sleep, daily movement, balanced food, deep breathing, realistic time management, supportive relationships and less caffeine in the evening. There is no need for quick fixes or expensive supplements, only small, steady steps whose effect a person notices within weeks. Start today with one step, such as a walk after dinner or switching off the phone an hour before bed, keep it up, and consult your doctor if symptoms continue or worsen, because your health deserves care and follow-up.
