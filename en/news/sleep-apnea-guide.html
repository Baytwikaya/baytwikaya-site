Sleep Apnea: The Complete Guide

Share this article:

Why does this matter?

You wake up in the morning feeling as if you never slept at all, even though you spent eight hours in bed. Your partner tells you that your snoring fills the room, and that they have sometimes noticed your breathing stop for a few seconds and then return with a sudden gasp. These may be signs of sleep apnea, a common disorder in which breathing stops repeatedly during the night without the person noticing, robbing them of deep sleep and affecting their health in the long term.

Many people with sleep apnea do not know they have it, because the symptoms happen while they are asleep. In this guide we explain what this disorder is, what types exist, what causes it and who is at risk, what its symptoms are, how it is diagnosed and treated, what practical steps ease it, and when you should see a doctor.

What is sleep apnea?

Sleep apnea is a disorder in which breathing stops and starts repeatedly during sleep. The pauses happen when the airway becomes blocked or when the brain temporarily stops sending breathing signals to the muscles. Each pause may last only a few seconds, but it can repeat dozens or even hundreds of times in a single night, so blood oxygen levels fall and the brain wakes up briefly to restart breathing, without the person remembering these awakenings in the morning.

There are two main types: obstructive sleep apnea, the most common, which happens when the throat muscles relax during sleep and narrow or close the airway; and central sleep apnea, which is less common and happens when the brain does not send the right signals to the breathing muscles. Some people have a combination of both types.

Who is most at risk, and what causes it?

The risk of sleep apnea rises with several factors:

  • Excess weight: fat deposits around the neck and the upper airway narrow the airway during sleep.
  • Age and sex: risk rises with age, and it is more common in men, but women's risk rises after menopause.
  • A narrow airway: large tonsils, a small jaw, a thick neck, or chronic nasal congestion.
  • Sleeping on the back: gravity makes the airway more likely to close.
  • Alcohol and sedatives: they relax the throat muscles and worsen the blockage.
  • Smoking: it increases inflammation and congestion of the airway.
  • Family history: having relatives with the condition raises the risk.

Some diseases also raise the risk, such as high blood pressure, heart failure, and type 2 diabetes, which are linked to sleep apnea in both directions: the disorder may contribute to their development, and they in turn may worsen sleep disturbance.

Symptoms: what you notice, and what others notice

The nighttime symptoms are the clearest, but the person usually does not see them; the person sharing the room does:

  • Loud and repeated snoring.
  • Breathing stopping during sleep, then returning with a gasp or choking sound.
  • Waking up repeatedly to urinate at night.
  • A dry mouth or sore throat on waking.
  • Night sweats.

The daytime symptoms are the ones the person feels directly: severe daytime sleepiness despite enough sleep, morning headaches, difficulty concentrating and remembering, irritability, and lower productivity at work. The person may doze off in inappropriate situations, such as while driving, which is one of the most dangerous consequences of the condition.

Why should sleep apnea not be ignored?

Untreated sleep apnea is not just an annoying snoring problem; it is a disorder with real health effects. Repeated drops in oxygen and partial awakenings during the night strain the heart and blood vessels, and are linked to a higher risk of high blood pressure, heart disease, stroke, and type 2 diabetes. Severe daytime sleepiness also raises the risk of road accidents and workplace accidents, and affects quality of life and family relationships.

The good news is that treatment makes a big difference: when sleep returns to normal, concentration, mood, and blood pressure often improve, and the person feels energy they have not known for years. That is why the condition deserves diagnosis and treatment, not just getting used to the tiredness.

How is sleep apnea diagnosed?

Diagnosis starts with the doctor asking about symptoms and about the partner's observations, followed by a physical examination that includes weight, neck circumference, blood pressure, and an examination of the nose and throat. The decisive test is a sleep study, which records breathing, oxygen levels, heart rate, and brain activity during the night. This study can be done in a sleep laboratory, or at home with a portable device the patient sets up themselves.

A sleep study measures how many times breathing stops or becomes shallow per hour, which determines the severity of the disorder, mild, moderate, or severe, and guides the treatment plan. Do not rely on phone apps alone for diagnosis, as they do not measure oxygen and breathing accurately enough.

Treatment: what really helps?

Treatment depends on the severity of the disorder and its causes. The most effective treatments are:

  • Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP): a machine that blows air at gentle pressure through a mask during sleep to keep the airway open. It is the first-line treatment for moderate and severe cases, and it takes some getting used to, but its benefits are great for those who stick with it.
  • Weight loss: it eases the blockage in many people, and may be enough on its own in mild cases.
  • Changing sleep position: sleeping on the side instead of the back reduces the blockage in some people.
  • Oral devices: a piece made by a dentist that keeps the lower jaw slightly forward during sleep.
  • Surgery: considered in specific cases, such as enlarged tonsils or airway abnormalities.

Alcohol and sedatives should be avoided before bed, and nasal congestion should be treated if present. There are no medicines that treat obstructive sleep apnea itself; treatment depends on opening the airway or removing the cause of the blockage.

Practical steps to improve your sleep

Alongside medical treatment, simple daily habits help ease the symptoms:

  • Keep a healthy weight, even with a small gradual loss; every kilogram makes a difference.
  • Sleep on your side; a pillow behind your back can stop you rolling onto your back.
  • Keep regular sleep and wake times, and make your bedroom dark, quiet, and relatively cool.
  • Stop smoking, and avoid alcohol and sedatives in the evening.
  • Be physically active regularly; it improves sleep quality and helps with weight loss.
  • If your doctor has prescribed a CPAP machine, use it every night, and ask about adjusting the mask if it feels uncomfortable.

These steps do not replace diagnosis and treatment in moderate and severe cases, but they are an essential part of the plan, and they increase the effectiveness of medical treatment.

Tip

If your partner notices that your breathing stops during sleep, or loud snoring, do not ignore it: write down what they observe and show it to your doctor, as it is one of the most important clues for diagnosing sleep apnea early.

When should you see a doctor?

See a doctor if you snore loudly and constantly, or if your partner has noticed your breathing stopping during sleep, or if you wake up tired despite enough sleep, or if you feel severely sleepy during the day, or if you doze off while driving or working. You should also see a doctor promptly if you have high blood pressure that is hard to control with medicines, or heart disease together with poor sleep. Do not postpone the appointment: early diagnosis protects your heart and your brain and restores restful sleep.

The bottom line: your sleep deserves free breathing

Sleep apnea is a common and treatable disorder, but it often goes undiagnosed because its symptoms happen at night. Pay attention to what the person who shares your room notices, and do not consider loud snoring and morning tiredness normal. A sleep study is simple and painless, and treatment brings back deep sleep and clear daytime energy.
