Intermittent fasting 16:8: does it really cut weight after a year? What the trials found

Share this article:

When the clock decides what you eat — not just how much

He wakes up in the morning, drinks his coffee, then waits until nine for his first meal and stops eating at five in the afternoon. He does not count calories precisely, and he follows no complicated diet — he simply keeps to one time window every day. That is intermittent fasting in its simplest form, an idea that has spread so fast it has become one of the most searched weight-loss methods online. But the question that matters to anyone who has tried it is not what happens in the first weeks — it is what is left after a full year: does the weight stay off, and is the price worth it?

Two new studies from 2026 answer that question directly, together with two large reviews that put the results in context. So what did the trials actually find?

What is intermittent fasting?

Intermittent fasting — or time-restricted eating — means taking all of the day’s meals inside a set time window and fasting outside it. Its best-known form is 16:8: fasting for 16 hours and eating during 8 hours, for example eating between 9am and 5pm, or between 1pm and 9pm. During the fasting hours, water and calorie-free drinks are usually allowed, while the idea itself sets no rule about what kind of food you eat inside the window — a point we will come back to, because what you eat inside the window matters as much as when you eat it.

The Granada study: a year later, the weight was still lower

The first study, from the University of Granada in Spain, was published in the journal Clinical Nutrition in 2026. It enrolled 99 adults with overweight or obesity for a 12-week intervention: three groups fasted with an 8-hour eating window — early (9am to 5pm), late (1pm to 9pm), and a third that chose its own window — against a usual-care group whose eating window was 12 hours or more. Importantly, all groups, including usual care, received Mediterranean diet education, so the difference being measured is the effect of meal timing alone, not the effect of learning to eat healthily.

Twelve months after the intervention ended, the early and late groups had kept significantly greater weight loss than usual care (early p=0.01, late p=0.02). The early window alone had also kept greater fat-mass loss (p=0.04). The group that chose its own window did not reach statistical significance. During the 12 weeks themselves, the fasting groups lost about 3 to 4 kg more than diet advice alone.

But the results carried a less bright side: the loss of fat-free mass — muscle, mostly — persisted alongside the weight loss even after a year. That is the central trade-off of intermittent fasting, as we will see. One striking observation was that one in three participants kept fasting on their own during the follow-up year, without being asked to — a sign that the pattern is sustainable for many people, not just a passing experiment.

The TREAD trial: a 10-hour window and 4.6% weight loss

The second study, the randomised TREAD trial published in the journal Obesity in 2026, was smaller and shorter: 61 adults with obesity, a body mass index between 30 and 50, and no diabetes, for 12 weeks. It compared a 10-hour eating window with standard care, and the fasting group lost 4.59 kg on average (4.6% of body weight) against 1.68 kg (1.7%) in the standard-care group — a difference of 2.91 kg, most of it body fat rather than muscle. The researchers also found lower expression of pro-inflammatory genes in the fat tissue of the fasting group, an interesting biological signal, though on its own it does not prove a long-term clinical benefit.

The wider picture: the real difference is modest

This is where the two studies need their context, because reading one study alone misleads. A network meta-analysis published in the BMJ in 2025, pooling 99 trials with 6,582 adults, found that time-restricted eating cut weight by only 1.72 kg compared with unrestricted eating — a real difference, but a modest one. More importantly, intermittent fasting has not beaten ordinary daily calorie restriction in the trials that compared them head to head. And a 2026 Cochrane review confirmed the same picture: little to no difference between intermittent fasting and regular dietary advice.

What does this apparent contradiction mean? The two new studies compared fasting with general advice or usual care, not with an organised calorie-restricted diet. When the alternative is “eat as usual”, fasting wins; when the alternative is a diet with counted calories, its advantage mostly disappears. That does not mean fasting does not work — it means its real strength may be that it is an easier way to stick to eating less, not magic in the timing itself.

Who should not fast without medical advice?

Intermittent fasting is not right for everyone, and some groups should not start it without medical advice:

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding women.
  • Children and adolescents.
  • People with type 1 diabetes, or anyone taking diabetes medicines, because of the risk of low blood sugar.
  • People with a history of eating disorders.
  • People who are underweight.
  • Older adults at risk of muscle loss.

Tip

If you decide to try intermittent fasting, pick a window you can keep every day — consistency matters more than the window’s timing, and the early window is slightly better for fat loss if it fits your day. Pay attention to what you eat inside the window: real, balanced food, enough protein at every meal, and resistance exercise to protect your muscles — because muscle loss is the hidden price of fasting. And if you take any medicine, or belong to one of the groups listed above, talk to your doctor before starting, and never stop a treatment on your own.

The bottom line: the window matters less than sticking with it

After a full year, intermittent fasting remains a practical approach that many people stick to, and it keeps off more weight than general advice alone — but it is not superior to organised calorie restriction, and it is not magic. The early window is slightly better for fat loss, consistency matters more than timing, and what you eat inside the window matters more than the window itself. And the price to budget for is muscle: enough protein and resistance exercise, or you may lose with the weight something you did not want to lose.
