Can weight-loss drugs cause blindness? The truth about the new US warning and the limits of the risk

Share this article:

A story that frightened millions of users worldwide

On 9 November 2026, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) ordered all medicines in the popular GLP-1 class — Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro and Zepbound — to add a new warning to their labels: a possible increased risk of a rare condition affecting the optic nerve, which can cause sudden vision loss. Within days the news spread everywhere, and millions of users began asking the same question: should I stop the drug?

The decision does not mean banning these medicines or withdrawing them from the market, but it raises a legitimate question for people who rely on them to control diabetes or lose weight. What is this condition? What does the evidence actually say? And how big is the risk in real numbers, not in headlines?

What is the condition behind the warning?

The condition is called non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy (NAION). It happens when blood flow drops to the front part of the optic nerve, the nerve that carries visual signals from the eye to the brain. The reduced blood supply damages nerve fibres, causing sudden vision loss, usually in one eye and without pain.

Some people wake up to find part of their visual field darkened, or a hazy spot blocking some details, while others notice a drop in visual sharpness or a change in colour perception. It does not necessarily mean total blindness, as severity varies and some patients improve partly. But there is still no proven treatment that restores vision lost to this condition, and the damage can be permanent.

Where did the story start? The 2024 study that sparked the debate

Attention to the possible link began in July 2024, after a study in JAMA Ophthalmology reported a higher risk of optic nerve damage among users of semaglutide, the active ingredient in Ozempic and Wegovy. Doctors at Massachusetts Eye and Ear had noticed three cases in a single week, so they examined the records of 16,827 patients.

The results were striking in relative terms: among patients with type 2 diabetes, 17 of 194 semaglutide users (8.9%) developed the condition, compared with 6 of 516 non-users (1.8%) — a risk about 4.3 times higher. Among people taking it for obesity, 20 of 361 users were affected versus 3 of 618, a risk about 7.6 times higher. But the study relied on records from patients treated at a single specialist centre, which limits how far its findings can be generalised, and the case numbers were small with wide confidence intervals.

What did the FDA review find? 126 trials and only 15 cases

In its later review, the FDA analysed data from 126 randomised clinical trials covering 134,253 participants, including 78,217 who used GLP-1 drugs and 56,036 who received a placebo or a comparison drug. Despite the huge number of participants, only 15 possible cases of optic nerve damage were found — 10 among drug users and 5 in the comparison groups.

The incidence rate was 6.4 cases per 100,000 person-years among drug users, versus 3.8 in the other group. The difference between the groups amounts to about 2.6 extra cases per 100,000 person-years — a small difference in absolute terms, but one worth attention because losing vision is so serious. Even so, the small number of cases does not allow a firm conclusion that the drugs directly caused them, and a review of animal data found no confirmed biological mechanism explaining how these treatments could damage the optic nerve.

The large studies: relative risk rises, absolute risk stays small

The studies do not agree on the size of the risk. A population study published in 2025, based on data from more than 61,000 diabetes patients in Denmark and Norway, found that the risk among semaglutide users was about 2.8 times that of users of SGLT2 inhibitors, another diabetes drug class. But the large relative increase corresponds to a small absolute increase of about 1.4 extra cases per 10,000 person-years, out of only 32 cases in the whole study.

By contrast, an FDA review using the Sentinel database found no statistically significant increase when GLP-1 drugs were compared with SGLT2 inhibitors, though it did find an increase when compared with another class known as DPP-4 inhibitors. These mixed results show that the risk estimate depends on the patients studied and the drugs used for comparison, as well as other health factors that may raise the chances of the condition.

The European Medicines Agency, for its part, classified the condition in June 2025 as a very rare side effect of semaglutide — meaning it may affect up to 1 in 10,000 users — and recommended stopping the drug if the diagnosis is confirmed.

Why does the warning cover Mounjaro too?

Most studies focus on semaglutide, but the US warning extends to all GLP-1 drugs, including Mounjaro and Zepbound, which contain tirzepatide, as well as older diabetes medicines. The FDA explained that reports of optic nerve damage had been received for the various products covered by the warning. But that does not mean the risk is equal across all drugs, or that the evidence on Mounjaro matches what has been published on Ozempic and Wegovy, and the current data do not allow a reliable ranking of these treatments by their risk to the optic nerve.

Who is most at risk?

Optic nerve damage of this kind is not a new disease, and it is not limited to users of weight-loss drugs. It is more common after the age of 50 and is linked to several factors, notably diabetes, high blood pressure, smoking and obstructive sleep apnoea, along with some lipid disorders and blood vessel diseases. The difficulty in interpreting the studies is that many patients taking GLP-1 drugs already have one or more of these risk factors.

A previous case of optic nerve damage in one eye also deserves special attention, because the chances of the other eye being affected are higher. That is why people with such a medical history should discuss the matter with their doctor when assessing whether the treatment suits them.

Advice

Do not stop your medicine on your own because of this warning, as long as you have no new visual symptoms. Some of these drugs do more than lower weight or control blood sugar: their benefits in reducing heart and kidney complications have been proven in certain groups, and stopping them without a suitable alternative can worsen diabetes control or lose important therapeutic benefits. On 11 November 2026, the American Academy of Ophthalmology and the North American Neuro-Ophthalmology Society stressed that any decision to stop treatment should be discussed with a doctor, noting that the possible risk remains small in absolute terms, without recommending extra eye tests for all users — while keeping the usual routine eye checks for diabetes patients. But if optic nerve damage is confirmed, the European agency recommends stopping semaglutide, and the US FDA asks doctors to consider stopping GLP-1 drugs unless the benefits of continuing clearly outweigh the risks.

When does it become an emergency?

Any sudden loss of vision calls for urgent medical assessment, especially if it happens in one eye without pain. Warning signs that should never be ignored include a dark spot or a missing area in the visual field, severe sudden blurring, or a rapid decline in the ability to distinguish details and colours. Do not wait for the next routine appointment or the next dose, because these symptoms can also point to other serious conditions, such as a blocked retinal blood vessel or a stroke. The patient needs an urgent examination to identify the cause, assess the optic nerve, and rule out conditions that need rapid intervention.

The bottom line: a real but rare risk — and panic is more dangerous

The US warning is real, and the studies agree there is a risk signal, but they differ on its size: the relative risk may double or triple, while the absolute risk stays tiny — a handful of extra cases per 10,000 or 100,000 years of treatment. Most importantly, causation has not been proven, and the risk factors themselves — diabetes, obesity and high blood pressure — are already present in most users of these drugs. Neither dismissal nor panic is right: people who use the drug on prescription and benefit from it should discuss the warning with their doctor, keep up routine eye checks, and seek emergency care at once if their vision changes suddenly. And for anyone considering the drug for weight loss alone, without a prescription, the message is clearer still: these are medical treatments with benefits and risks, not a cosmetic solution to be taken without supervision.
