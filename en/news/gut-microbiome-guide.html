The Gut Microbiome: The Complete Guide

Share this article:

The gut microbiome: a small world inside your body

Inside your gut lives a huge number of microorganisms, mostly bacteria, which together form what is called the gut microbiome. These organisms are not passive guests, but active partners in your health: they help digest fibre that your body cannot digest alone, produce vitamins and useful compounds, train the immune system, and protect the gut wall. In recent years, the microbiome has become one of the most studied topics in medicine, and links have appeared between its diversity and health and digestion, immunity, weight and even mood. But much of what is published about it is exaggerated, and products are sold that claim to repair it without sufficient evidence. In this guide we explain what the microbiome is in simple terms, what we really know and what is still unconfirmed, how to support it through food and lifestyle, and when you should consult your doctor instead of relying on supplements.

What is the gut microbiome?

The microbiome is the collection of microorganisms that live in the gut, most of them bacteria of hundreds of different species, plus other organisms in smaller numbers. Its formation begins at birth, and it is influenced by birth, breastfeeding, food, medicines and the environment, stabilises relatively in childhood, and then changes constantly with food, age, illness and treatment. Every person has a microbiome different from everyone else, like a fingerprint, but healthy people generally share greater diversity, meaning a larger number of different species, and the presence of known beneficial species. These bacteria feed mainly on dietary fibre that reaches the colon undigested, and turn it into compounds called short-chain fatty acids, which feed the cells of the colon wall and support its health. The microbiome also helps produce some vitamins, such as vitamin K and some B vitamins, and interacts with the immune system, a large part of which is in the gut, so scientists consider the gut an important organ in immunity and not only in digestion.

The microbiome and digestion: when is the balance disturbed?

When the microbiome is balanced and diverse, digestion usually works smoothly: less bloating, regularity, and a feeling of comfort after eating. When diversity falls or non-beneficial species increase, symptoms may appear such as bloating, gas, constipation or diarrhoea and abdominal pain, but these symptoms have many causes, and alone they do not mean that the microbiome is the cause. Factors that harm diversity include a diet poor in fibre and rich in processed foods, sugars and saturated fats, lack of movement, lack of sleep, chronic stress, and repeated use of antibiotics without need. Antibiotics are sometimes essential to treat infection, but they kill beneficial bacteria along with harmful ones, so they should be used only on a medical prescription and for the set duration, and should not be taken without advice. If you have persistent digestive symptoms, such as blood in the stool, unexplained weight loss, severe pain or persistent diarrhoea, these are signs that call for a visit to the doctor at once, and changing food or taking a supplement is not enough.

Food first: fibre and fermented foods

The strongest way to support the microbiome is food, not supplements. Fibre is the preferred food of beneficial bacteria, and it is found in vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains and nuts. Moroccan cooking is rich in it: lentils, chickpeas, broad beans and white beans, seasonal vegetables, whole-wheat bread, and fruit such as figs, grapes and oranges. The practical rule is variety: the more varied the plant foods in your week, the more varied the beneficial bacteria in your gut. Fermented foods are foods that contain live beneficial bacteria, such as laban and yoghurt, which have been present in Moroccan cooking for a long time. Eating natural laban without added sugar regularly supports the microbiome, especially types whose label says they contain live cultures. There are also other fermented foods in the world such as kimchi and pickles, but very salty pickles should be eaten in moderation because of the salt. Avoid excess sugars, fizzy drinks and processed foods, because they favour less useful bacterial species, and reduce the diversity of the microbiome in the long term.

Probiotics and supplements: what does the scientific evidence say?

Probiotics are supplements that contain live bacteria, sold widely in pharmacies and online, and they claim many benefits for digestion, immunity, weight and mood. The truth is that the scientific evidence is mixed: some probiotic strains help in specific situations, such as reducing diarrhoea linked to antibiotics or easing some symptoms of irritable bowel syndrome, but the benefit depends on the strain, the dose and the condition, and there is no single strain that helps everyone in every situation. Most studies are also short, the long-term benefits are unconfirmed, and supplements are not regulated with the same strictness as medicines in many countries. For this reason probiotics should not be taken as a replacement for a varied diet, nor as a replacement for medical treatment, especially if you have a weak immune system, a serious illness or take medicines that suppress immunity, in which case you must consult a doctor before any supplement. The best choice for most people is to invest in food: varied fibre, natural laban, home cooking, movement and sufficient sleep, before thinking about supplements. If you decide to try a supplement, ask your doctor or pharmacist about the strain suitable for your condition and the duration, and do not expect magical results.

The microbiome, immunity, weight and mood

Scientists link the microbiome to topics wider than digestion, but many of these links are still at the research stage. The immune system learns in the gut to distinguish friend from foe, and a diverse microbiome helps train it, so scientists study its relationship with allergies and autoimmune diseases. In weight, studies have noted that people living with obesity have on average a less diverse microbiome, but we do not yet know whether low diversity is a cause of obesity or a result of it, so we cannot say that changing the microbiome alone reduces weight. In mood, there is a connection between the gut and the brain through nerves and hormones, called the gut-brain axis, and scientists study how bacteria affect anxiety and mood, but the human evidence is still early, and there is no proven probiotic to treat depression or anxiety. The important message is: the microbiome matters, but it is part of a larger picture that includes food, movement, sleep, stress and genes, and health cannot be repaired from the gut alone. Anyone who promises you that is selling you an illusion, not science.

A practical plan for a healthier gut

To support your microbiome in the coming weeks, try these simple steps. First, increase fibre gradually: add a vegetable dish to every meal, eat legumes at least three times a week, and choose whole bread instead of white. Second, vary your food: try different vegetables and fruit every week, and do not eat the same items every day. Third, eat natural laban without sugar regularly, or natural yoghurt. Fourth, reduce processed foods, sugars and fizzy drinks, and cook at home as much as possible. Fifth, move every day: walking and movement support gut health. Sixth, sleep seven or eight hours at night, because lack of sleep affects the microbiome and appetite together. Seventh, reduce stress through movement, breathing and social relationships, because chronic stress affects digestion. Eighth, take antibiotics only on prescription, and complete the set duration. If you have persistent digestive symptoms, record your food and symptoms for two weeks, and take the record to your doctor, as this helps them diagnose more than any commercial microbiome test.

Tip

Eat a varied diet rich in fibre every day: vegetables, legumes, whole grains and yoghurt, and reduce processed foods and sugars.

The bottom line: feed your gut bacteria well, and they will repay you

The gut microbiome is a small world that affects your digestion, your immunity and your general health, and the best way to support it is a varied diet rich in fibre, natural laban, movement and sufficient sleep, and avoiding unnecessary antibiotics. Supplements, by contrast, have a limited benefit tied to specific situations, and do not replace food. If you have persistent or worrying symptoms, consult your doctor, and do not rely on products that promise to repair the microbiome overnight.
