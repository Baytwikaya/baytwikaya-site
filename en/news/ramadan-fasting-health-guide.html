Ramadan Fasting and Health: The Complete Guide to Healthy and Safe Fasting

Share this article:

Fasting in Ramadan: worship and health together

The month of Ramadan is a month of worship and drawing closer to God, and it is also an opportunity to reconsider our food and health habits. Fasting means abstaining from food and drink from dawn to sunset, and this change in the timing of eating, drinking and sleeping affects the body in different ways: digestion may improve and eating may become more regular, a person may feel energetic and clear-headed, but he may also face headaches, tiredness, dehydration and disturbed sleep if he does not pay attention to his food, hydration and sleep. Healthy fasting therefore needs simple planning, not overeating at night and not neglecting water.

The effect of fasting differs from person to person according to age, health condition, type of work, the length of the day and the weather. A healthy adult usually tolerates fasting without problems, while a sick person, especially with diabetes, heart or kidney disease, or a pregnant or breastfeeding woman, needs to consult a doctor before Ramadan to assess whether fasting is possible and to adjust medicines if necessary. This guide explains healthy fasting step by step: from iftar and suhoor to water, sleep and exercise, from chronic diseases to danger signs that require breaking the fast and consulting a doctor.

What happens in the body during fasting?

During the hours of fasting the body uses its stored energy, starting with the sugar stored in the liver, then gradually moving to stored fat, and this is a natural part of fasting. The digestive system also rests during the day, and some people feel an improvement in bloating and indigestion, especially when iftar is balanced. The body gets used to a new rhythm after the first days, so headaches and tiredness gradually fall, and concentration improves for many people, especially when they sleep well and drink enough water at night.

But fasting can also cause temporary problems in the first days, such as headaches because of lack of caffeine or lack of sleep, tiredness because of lack of water, constipation because of lack of fibre and water, and heartburn because of overeating at iftar. These problems often improve with regular sleep, gradually reducing caffeine before Ramadan, drinking water regularly between iftar and suhoor, and eating fibre from vegetables, fruit and whole grains. If symptoms continue or get worse, the doctor should be consulted, because they may be a sign of another health problem.

A healthy iftar: how to break the fast?

The best way to break the fast is to start with dates and water, as is the tradition, then eat soup and salad, then wait a little before the main meal, because this helps the stomach prepare gradually and prevents fullness and indigestion. The main meal should be balanced: vegetables and protein such as meat, fish, chicken or legumes, and grains such as bread, rice or couscous in moderation, with fewer fried and fatty foods and sweets. Overeating at iftar is one of the most common mistakes in Ramadan, causing fullness, heartburn, sluggishness and weight gain, while moderation preserves energy and comfort.

In Morocco, iftar tables vary with harira, dates, chebakia and briouates, beloved traditional foods, but they should be eaten in moderation: harira, rich in legumes and vegetables, is a good choice, while fried sweets rich in sugar should be eaten in small amounts and not every day. Soft drinks and sweetened juices should be avoided and replaced with water and green tea without too much sugar. Food should also be divided into two small meals between iftar and suhoor, instead of one large meal, because this helps digestion and preserves energy.

Suhoor and water: the fuel for the whole day

Suhoor is a very important meal, because it supplies the body with energy and water for the long hours of fasting, and skipping it increases tiredness, headaches, hunger and thirst. Suhoor should be balanced and contain protein and fibre, such as eggs, cheese, yoghurt, whole bread, oats and fruit, with less salt, because salt increases thirst. Spicy and fatty foods should be avoided at suhoor, because they increase heartburn and thirst, and sweets should be avoided, because they give quick energy followed by a quick fall.

As for water, it should be drunk regularly between iftar and suhoor, not all at once, because the body does not store extra water; it removes it quickly. Water and unsweetened fluids should be drunk throughout the night, and foods rich in water should be eaten, such as vegetables, fruit, soup and yoghurt, while coffee and tea should not be taken in excess, because they increase water loss. On hot days or when working under the sun, more attention should be paid to signs of dehydration, such as a dry mouth, dizziness, headaches, little urine or dark urine, and the doctor should be consulted when they appear.

Sleep and exercise in Ramadan

Sleep changes in Ramadan because of suhoor, taraweeh prayers and work times, and night sleep may become shorter and disturbed, which increases tiredness, headaches and poor concentration. A fixed time for sleep should therefore be kept as far as possible, a short nap should be taken during the day if possible, long nights in front of screens should be avoided, and the bedroom should be kept dark and quiet. Caffeine should be reduced gradually a week or two before Ramadan, so that the body does not suffer caffeine withdrawal symptoms in the first days, such as headaches and tiredness.

As for exercise, it can be practised in Ramadan, but at a suitable time and with moderate intensity. A light walk after iftar or before suhoor is a good choice for many people, while intense exercise is better delayed until after iftar, when the body is hydrated and fed. Intense exercise in the hot daytime should be avoided, and the body should be listened to, stopping when dizziness, nausea, chest pain or shortness of breath is felt. Older people and sick people should ask the doctor about the type of exercise and the right time for them in Ramadan.

Tip

Break your fast with dates, water and soup, divide your food into two small meals, drink water regularly between iftar and suhoor, and never skip suhoor.

Fasting and chronic diseases: diabetes, heart and kidneys

Fasting may be possible for some people with chronic diseases, but it may be dangerous for others, so the decision must be made with the doctor before Ramadan, not by personal judgement. A person with diabetes, especially one who takes insulin or medicines that can cause low blood sugar, is exposed to the risk of severe low or high blood sugar or dehydration during fasting, and needs a plan to monitor blood sugar, adjust medicines and their timing, and know when to break the fast immediately, such as with severe low blood sugar, severe high blood sugar or signs of dehydration. God has granted the sick person permission to break the fast, and a person's health comes before fasting if fasting harms him.

As for a person with heart disease or high blood pressure, the doctor should be asked about fasting and about adjusting the timing of medicines, especially diuretics, which increase water loss, and medicines should not be stopped without medical advice. A person with kidney disease, especially kidney failure or repeated stones, is exposed to the risk of dehydration and worsening kidney function during fasting, and needs a careful assessment before Ramadan. As for pregnant and breastfeeding women, the law grants them permission to break the fast when they fear for their health or the health of the baby, and they should consult a doctor to assess the situation. Remember that breaking the fast when sick is a legal and health permission, and the missed day can be made up later or compensated, according to the case, after consulting people of knowledge and the doctor.

When should you break the fast immediately and consult a doctor?

The fast should be broken immediately and a doctor consulted when signs appear such as severe low blood sugar in a person with diabetes, or severe high blood sugar with symptoms such as intense thirst, frequent urination and nausea, or chest pain or shortness of breath, or severe dizziness or fainting, or severe dehydration with little or no urine, or persistent vomiting, or a severe persistent headache, or any symptom that threatens health. Fasting should not be continued when these signs appear in the name of endurance, because preserving life comes before completing the fast, and the day can be made up later when able.

The doctor should also be consulted before Ramadan when taking any medicine regularly, because some medicines need a change in dose or timing, such as medicines for diabetes, blood pressure, heart disease and epilepsy. No medicine should be stopped suddenly without medical advice, and its dose should not be changed alone. If you doubt your ability to fast, consult your doctor early before Ramadan, and ask him for a clear written plan, because this is better than deciding on the first day of Ramadan. Remember that healthy fasting is fasting that preserves your health and your worship together.

The bottom line: a healthy Ramadan through moderation and planning

Fasting in Ramadan is a great opportunity for health and worship together, when it is done with moderation and planning: a balanced iftar starting with dates, water and soup, a suhoor rich in protein and fibre, enough water between iftar and suhoor, regular sleep, moderate movement after iftar, and fewer sweets, fried foods and sweetened drinks. Do not forget to consult a doctor before Ramadan if you are sick, pregnant or taking medicines regularly, and break the fast immediately at any danger sign, because your health is a trust, and the permission to break the fast when sick is a mercy. May God accept your fasting and prayers, and make Ramadan a month of health and goodness for you and your families.
