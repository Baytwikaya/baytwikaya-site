Air pollution speeds up dementia: 25% higher risk in the most polluted areas — what a 2026 study found

Share this article:

The air you breathe may reach your brain

We know that air pollution harms the lungs and the heart, but what about the brain? A large new study says polluted air may not stop at the chest: its effects may reach the immune cells inside the brain, reprogramme how they work, and raise the risk of Alzheimer's disease and related forms of dementia. So what did the study actually find? And does it mean that people in polluted cities are doomed to dementia? The answer is far more nuanced than that.

The study, published in the Journal of Neuroinflammation on 11 November 2026, combined two parts: a health analysis of nearly half a million people in Britain, and laboratory experiments in mice. It is the combination that makes the findings striking — the human part tracks the risk on a large scale, while the animal part explains the possible mechanism behind it.

The headline figure: 25% higher risk in the most polluted areas

The researchers followed 475,325 participants from the UK Biobank for up to 14 years, comparing people according to their long-term exposure to fine particulate matter, PM2.5. They found that people living in the most polluted third of areas were 25% more likely to develop Alzheimer's disease or a related dementia than those living in the least polluted third — after accounting for age, smoking, diabetes and other factors.

That 25% is a relative risk, not an absolute one: it means the risk rises by a quarter compared with the least polluted group, not that a quarter of residents will develop dementia. The absolute increase in risk for any one person remains small, but across a whole city or country it amounts to thousands of extra cases.

What is PM2.5?

PM2.5 is very fine particulate matter in the air, less than 2.5 micrometres across — about 30 times smaller than the width of a human hair — released by vehicle exhaust, industrial chimneys, fuel burning and wildfires. Its tiny size is what makes it dangerous: it penetrates deep into the lungs, and the smallest particles can pass into the bloodstream, and from there to the body's organs, including the brain.

Its levels are usually measured in micrograms per cubic metre of air, and they vary greatly from city to city and day to day. In the human part of the study, each participant's long-term exposure was estimated from their home address, not measured directly from what each person actually breathed — an important point we will come back to.

Inside the brain: immune cells reprogrammed

In the laboratory part, the researchers exposed mice — some healthy, and some genetically modified to be prone to Alzheimer's disease (the 5xFAD model) — to real outdoor air from the city of Cleveland in the United States, with its fine particles concentrated to levels similar to those in the world's most polluted cities, for 20 weeks (about five months), while control mice breathed filtered clean air.

The result was that the pollution-exposed mice — even the healthy ones, with no genetic predisposition to the disease — developed memory problems, a leaky blood-brain barrier that is supposed to protect the brain, brain inflammation, a depleted cellular energy reserve (the NAD+/NADH balance) that the brain needs to withstand injury and repair itself, and increased death of newborn neurons in the hippocampus, the brain's centre for learning and memory.

Most importantly, molecular examination of the microglia — the brain's resident immune cells, which clear away debris and fight infection — showed that pollution changed which genes were active in them, and also changed the structure of the chromatin that controls genes being switched on and off. In other words, pollution did not merely disturb these cells temporarily; it “reprogrammed” their working instructions, so they stopped doing their normal jobs properly. And in the mice genetically prone to Alzheimer's, pollution also sped up the build-up of amyloid plaques, a hallmark of the disease.

Genes and pollution: does the risk double when they combine?

The researchers also studied the interaction between pollution and genetic susceptibility: in the human part, both long-term PM2.5 exposure and genetic predisposition to Alzheimer's, measured with a polygenic risk score, were each independently linked to a higher risk of dementia. But the interaction between them was smaller than simple arithmetic would predict (interaction hazard ratio 0.981) — the two factors together did not multiply the risk by the product of their separate effects, but came closer to adding up. Put simply: pollution raises the risk even in people without a strong genetic predisposition, and genes raise the risk even in clean air, and their combination does not explode the risk as might have been feared.

In the mice too, the size and direction of the microglial response to pollution depended on the genotype, and the changes converged on inflammatory, lysosomal, oxidative-stress, synaptic and amyloid-related pathways. This helps explain why the same pollution may affect different people to different degrees.

Read the results carefully: what did the study not prove?

This is where a good article differs from an exaggerated one. First, the human part of the study is observational: it records an association between pollution and dementia, and on its own it does not prove that pollution is the cause — people in polluted areas may differ from others in income, diet, health care and other factors, and although the researchers accounted for many of these, observation alone cannot rule out every hidden factor. The mouse experiments support the idea of causation, but they are experiments on animals, not on humans.

Second, the pollution levels in the mouse experiments were concentrated to resemble the world's most polluted cities, far above the levels usually found in Britain, where the human data were collected. So the mouse results cannot be transferred literally to someone living in a city with moderate pollution levels.

Third, “most polluted” in the human study means the highest third among areas in Britain, where pollution is moderate by world standards — the comparison is relative within Britain, not a comparison between a clean city and a severely polluted city elsewhere in the world.

Fourth, the microglial “reprogramming” mechanism was observed in mice, not in human brains, and there is no treatment built on this mechanism today — the study opens a door to research on ways to make the brain more resilient, but it offers no therapy and no preventive drug.

Tip

No one can choose their city's air alone, but there is still something to do: follow the air quality index in your city, and on heavily polluted days reduce strenuous outdoor exercise, especially near busy roads, open windows for ventilation at the least polluted times of day, and, if you can, use an air purifier in the bedroom. At home and at work, avoid smoking and second-hand smoke, and keep up what protects the brain anyway: enough sleep, regular movement, a balanced diet, blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol under control, and mental and social activity. Dementia is not made by a single factor, and preventing it does not come from a single step either. And if you notice repeated forgetfulness in yourself or a relative that affects daily life, see a doctor — an early diagnosis opens doors to treatment and follow-up that waiting does not.

The bottom line: cleaner air means a healthier brain

The 2026 study in the Journal of Neuroinflammation combines large-scale human observation — 475,325 people over 14 years — with controlled laboratory experiments, to say that fine air pollution is not only a lung problem, but a neurological risk factor: in the most polluted areas, the risk of Alzheimer's and related dementia rose by 25%, and in mice, pollution reprogrammed the brain's immune cells and weakened its protective barrier, its energy and its memory. But the message is not a sealed fate: the risk is relative, the mechanism was observed in animals, and prevention remains within reach — cleaner air, less polluted cities, and daily habits that protect the brain.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the study

  • The human part: 475,325 participants from the UK Biobank, followed for up to 14 years.
  • The risk: people in the most polluted third of areas were 25% more likely to develop Alzheimer's disease or a related dementia than those in the least polluted third (relative risk, after adjusting for age, smoking, diabetes and other factors).
  • The particles: PM2.5, fine particles less than 2.5 micrometres across, from vehicle exhaust, industry and wildfires.
  • The mouse experiments: 20 weeks of exposure to concentrated outdoor Cleveland air with fine particles, at levels similar to the world's most polluted cities, versus filtered air; healthy mice and 5xFAD Alzheimer's-model mice.
  • What happened in the mice's brains: microglial activation, a leaky blood-brain barrier, a depleted NAD+/NADH energy pool, impaired formation of new neurons in the hippocampus, and memory problems; increased amyloid plaques in 5xFAD mice.
  • The mechanism: remodelling of gene expression and chromatin accessibility in microglia (RNA-seq and ATAC-seq analysis), in inflammatory, lysosomal, oxidative-stress, synaptic and amyloid-related pathways.
  • The interaction with genes: interaction hazard ratio 0.981 (95% confidence interval 0.965–0.998; p=0.026), a sub-multiplicative interaction — the combined effect of pollution and genetic predisposition was smaller than the product of the two.
  • Publication: Journal of Neuroinflammation, 11 November 2026; senior authors Sanjay Rajagopalan and Andrew Pieper, Case Western Reserve University; DOI: 10.1186/s12974-026-04118-1.
