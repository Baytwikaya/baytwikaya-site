A new blood test measures how far Alzheimer’s has spread — what the JAMA study found

Share this article:

When the question becomes: how far has the disease progressed?

Alzheimer’s begins quietly: a forgotten appointment, a lost word, difficulty arranging a simple step. Over time, the question that worries families and doctors is no longer only “is it Alzheimer’s?” but “how far has the disease progressed?” Knowing the stage changes decisions: which treatment fits, when to start it, how to follow it, and whether an experimental treatment could still help or whether it is too late.

Until today, measuring the stage of the disease accurately has required complex and costly tests: brain imaging or a spinal fluid draw. The blood tests available so far detect the disease early, but say little about how far it has advanced. A new study published in JAMA on 16 November 2026 suggests a way to fill this gap: a blood test that measures a marker called eMTBR-tau, which reflects how far tau tangles have spread in the brain.

What is new about this test?

The new test is the first scalable blood test measuring eMTBR-tau, a fragment of the tau protein that reflects how far tau tangles have spread inside brain cells. Tau tangles are fibrous clumps that build up inside nerve cells and damage them, and their spread is closely linked to the progression of the disease and the decline of memory and abilities.

The study was led by Dr Niklas Mattsson-Carlgren of Lund University in Sweden, with researchers from Banner Health, Lund University, McGill University in Montreal and others, and the test was developed by Alamar Biosciences. The results were presented the same day at the Clinical Trials on Alzheimer’s Disease (CTAD) conference in Boston.

How was the study done?

The study included 728 participants from two groups: 521 brain donors, from the Banner Arizona Study of Aging and Neurodegenerative Disorders and the Brain and Body Donation Program in Sun City, Arizona, and from the Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center at the University of California, Irvine; and 207 participants from imaging cohorts, from the BioFINDER-2 study in Lund and the TRIAD study at McGill.

This combination matters: brain donors allow the test result to be compared with what the actual examination of the brain found — the gold standard — while imaging cohorts allow the result to be linked to what brain scans show in living people.

eMTBR-tau versus p-tau217: what is the difference?

The blood marker most widely used today is p-tau217. It rises early and reflects amyloid plaques, the first sign of the disease, but it gives limited information about tau tangles in later stages. eMTBR-tau, by contrast, tracks how far the tangles themselves have spread, which is why it showed clearer differences between early and late stages of the disease and accurately detected advanced tau pathology.

That does not mean the new marker replaces the old one; the two may work together: one detects the disease early, the other measures how far it has progressed. Together, they could draw a fuller picture of each patient’s disease course.

What does this mean for patients today?

First of all, the test is not yet available for clinical use, and it is not meant to diagnose Alzheimer’s disease on its own. Its potential value lies elsewhere: staging the disease more accurately, choosing the right patients for clinical trials, and following the progression of the disease or its response to treatment over time. But all of that needs further validation before it reaches clinics.

Tip

If you are worried about your memory or that of a relative, do not wait for a new blood test and do not look for it outside medical channels — it is not yet available in clinics, and it does not diagnose the disease on its own. The right step today: see your doctor, describe the symptoms precisely, ask for a memory assessment, and do not hesitate to seek a second opinion. While waiting for a diagnosis, keep regular sleep, physical activity, a balanced diet and social contact — habits that support brain health at every age.

The bottom line: a step toward measuring Alzheimer’s from blood

The new eMTBR-tau test does not diagnose Alzheimer’s, and it is not yet available in clinics, but it fills a real gap: measuring how far the disease has progressed from a simple blood sample, where that knowledge previously required complex tests. If the results are confirmed in larger studies, this test — together with p-tau217 — could become a tool that helps doctors stage the disease, choose the right treatment and follow its course, a step that brings Alzheimer’s medicine closer to precision medicine.
