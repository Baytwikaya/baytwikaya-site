HPV viral load: can its trajectory over time predict cervical cancer risk?

Share this article:

A new study tracks viral load over time

A new study published online on 9 November 2026 in CANCER, a peer-reviewed journal of the American Cancer Society, could change how doctors follow women with persistent human papillomavirus (HPV) infection. Researchers at Changzhou Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital, part of Nanjing Medical University in China, prospectively followed 624 women with persistent HPV infection and found that the trajectory of viral load over time — whether it rises, falls or stays stable — strongly predicts the risk of developing precancerous cervical lesions. The differences between groups were striking: lesions appeared in only 10.9% of women in the low stable trajectory group, compared with 90.4% in the persistently high group.

What is persistent HPV infection?

HPV is one of the most common sexually transmitted infections in the world, and most infections are transient: nearly 90% clear on their own within one to two years, without causing any harm, as the immune system eliminates the virus. A small proportion of infections persist, meaning the virus remains detectable on repeated tests, and this persistent infection is the well-established prerequisite for precancerous cervical lesions, which can, over years, develop into cervical cancer. Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer among women worldwide, with an age-standardised incidence of 14.1 cases per 100,000 women in 2022. The greatest challenge for doctors is therefore not diagnosing the infection itself, but knowing which women with persistent infection are truly most at risk, and which can be followed with greater reassurance.

What is viral load, and why does its trajectory matter?

Viral load is the amount of virus present in a cervical sample, usually measured relative to the number of human cells in the sample. Earlier studies had already shown that a high viral load is linked to less efficient clearance of the infection and a greater risk of lesions. Most of those studies, however, relied on a single measurement at one point in time. In the new study, the research team measured viral load in each woman at repeated visits about six months apart, and used a statistical method known as latent class growth analysis to sort women into groups that shared a similar viral-load pattern over time. As co-corresponding author Lina Zhang, MD, explained: “Two women with similar HPV viral loads at one visit may have very different outcomes depending on how those levels change over time.” That idea is the heart of the study: the trend matters more than a single number.

Four trajectories, dramatically different outcomes

The researchers identified four distinct viral-load trajectories among the 624 women with persistent infection: low stable, moderate declining, moderate increasing, and persistently high. The outcomes differed dramatically: precancerous cervical lesions occurred in 10.9% of women in the first group, 17.8% in the second, 74.2% in the third, and 90.4% in the fourth. After adjusting for factors such as age, HPV type and infection with multiple types, the risk of lesions was 1.95 times higher in the second group, 29.66 times higher in the third, and 124.29 times higher in the fourth, compared with the low stable group. For comparison, lesions occurred in 40.4% of women with persistent infection overall, versus only 8.3% of those whose bodies cleared the virus. All 21 cases of high-grade lesions recorded in the study occurred among women with persistent infection. Importantly, adding trajectory information over time improved clinicians' ability to predict risk beyond baseline clinical characteristics and a single baseline viral-load measurement.

What could this change in medical follow-up?

If these results are confirmed in other studies, they could open the door to more personalised follow-up for women with persistent HPV infection. Instead of treating all women the same way, doctors could intensify surveillance — with more frequent tests and colposcopy when needed — for women whose viral load is rising or stays high, while reassuring women whose viral load stays low or declines, sparing them unnecessary repeated examinations. As co-corresponding author Zhonghua Shi, MD, put it: “A major challenge in managing persistent HPV infection is identifying women who need closer surveillance while avoiding unnecessary examinations. We hope these findings will contribute to more personalised follow-up strategies and ultimately improve cervical disease prevention.”

Tip

Do not panic if you test positive for HPV — most infections clear on their own within one to two years. Get vaccinated against HPV where it is available, keep up with regular cervical screening (Pap smear and HPV test) as recommended in your country, follow up any abnormal result with your doctor promptly, and ask them what viral load and its follow-up mean in your case.

Study limits and the bottom line

The findings should still be read with caution. The study was conducted at a single centre in China, enrolled women aged 25 to 65 who had not received the HPV vaccine, and is observational by design, meaning it shows a strong association rather than proving that viral load causes the lesions. Repeated viral-load measurement is also not yet a routine standard for triaging women in screening programmes, and results may differ in other countries or with other HPV types. Even so, the core message is both clear and reassuring: most HPV infections clear on their own, and even among women with persistent infection, the way viral load changes over time seems to distinguish those who need closer follow-up from those who can be reassured. That is a promising step towards more precise, personalised cervical health screening and follow-up.
