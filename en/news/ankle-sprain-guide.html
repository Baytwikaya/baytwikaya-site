Ankle sprain: the complete guide to causes, symptoms and treatment

Share this article:

What is an ankle sprain?

An ankle sprain is one of the most common joint injuries. It happens when the foot suddenly bends or twists in an unnatural direction, so the ligaments that connect the ankle bones are stretched or partly torn. Ligaments are strong bands of fibrous tissue that stabilise the joint and give it stability, and the most commonly injured are the outer ligaments of the ankle, which are stretched when the foot rolls inwards, as happens when walking on uneven ground, coming down stairs, or landing badly after a jump in sport. A sprain may be mild, with a slight stretch of the ligament, moderate, with a partial tear, or severe, with a complete tear and instability of the joint. A sprain is usually accompanied by pain, swelling and bruising, and difficulty walking or putting weight on the foot. Although most sprains improve within weeks with proper care, neglecting treatment and rehabilitation can lead to chronic ankle weakness and repeated sprains.

In this guide we explain how a sprain happens and its grades, its symptoms and warning signs, how a doctor distinguishes it from a fracture, the correct treatment step by step, rehabilitation exercises, and how to protect your ankle from another injury.

How does a sprain happen? And who is most at risk?

A sprain usually happens in a single moment: a wrong step on the edge of a pavement, uneven ground, a landing after a jump in football or basketball, or a sudden twist while running. In that moment the foot goes beyond its normal range of movement, and the ligament is stretched beyond its capacity or torn. Several factors increase the risk: weak leg and ankle muscles, poor balance, unsuitable shoes or high heels, walking or running on uneven ground, and returning to sport before full recovery from a previous sprain. People who have sprained an ankle before are also more likely to sprain it again, because the ligament may remain weaker, and because balance and the sense of the foot's position can be affected after the injury. In Morocco, sprains are common among football players on uneven pitches, among people walking in irregular lanes and stairways, and among children and young people in sporting activities. It is important for the reader to know that a sprain is not always a trivial injury: a severe sprain may be accompanied by a small fracture or cartilage injury, so it must be assessed seriously.

Symptoms and warning signs

The first symptom is pain in the ankle, which increases when putting weight on it or moving the foot. Swelling appears quickly around the ankle, and blue or purple bruises may appear within a day or two, and the injured person may hear a popping sound at the moment of injury. Walking may become difficult or impossible, and the person may feel instability in the ankle, as if the foot is about to give way. There are signs that require urgent medical care: being completely unable to put weight on the foot, severe pain in the bone when touched, an obvious deformity in the shape of the ankle, numbness or coldness in the foot, swelling that does not ease after days, or persistent pain that does not improve. These signs may indicate a fracture or a severe injury needing immediate assessment. See a doctor also if the same ankle sprains repeatedly, if instability persists after weeks of treatment, or if the pain prevents you from working and walking normally.

How does a doctor tell a sprain from a fracture?

Diagnosis begins by asking how the injury happened, followed by a physical examination: the doctor examines the swelling and bruising, gently feels the ankle bones for points of bone pain, and tests the range of movement and stability of the joint. Doctors rely on known clinical rules to decide who needs an X-ray: if the injured person can walk four steps immediately after the injury and there is no severe pain at specific bony points, an X-ray is usually not necessary. But if the person cannot bear weight, or the bone pain is severe, the doctor orders an X-ray to rule out a fracture. In some cases an MRI may be ordered to examine the ligaments and cartilage in detail. It is important for the patient to know that severe pain does not always mean a fracture, that a severe sprain can be more painful than some fractures, and that an accurate diagnosis in the first days determines the correct treatment plan and shortens recovery time.

Treatment: step by step in the first days

In the first days after a sprain, follow the rule of protection, relative rest, ice, compression and elevation. Protect the ankle from further injury and avoid putting weight on it if the pain is severe, using crutches if you need them. Applying ice to the ankle for a short time several times a day, with a cloth between the ice and the skin, helps reduce swelling and pain. Wrapping the ankle with an elastic compression bandage reduces swelling and supports the joint, without wrapping so tightly that it cuts off circulation. Raise the foot above heart level as much as possible, especially while sitting and lying down, as elevation reduces swelling. A doctor or pharmacist may prescribe painkillers and anti-inflammatory medicines for a short time. A splint or brace may be recommended by the doctor for moderate and severe sprains for several days or weeks, but prolonged immobilisation without movement weakens the muscles, so gentle ankle movement begins at the earliest safe time, following the doctor's instructions. Be cautious of unproven treatments that promise instant healing, and of forceful massage on the swollen ankle in the first days, as it increases bleeding and swelling.

Rehabilitation: exercises that restore strength and balance

After the pain and swelling ease, the rehabilitation stage begins, and it is the most important step in preventing another sprain. Start with range-of-motion exercises: slowly move your foot in circles, and pull your toes towards you and then away from you. Then strengthening exercises: press your foot against light resistance, such as an elastic band, in all four directions, and rise onto your heels while standing to strengthen the calf muscles. Then balance exercises, which are the most important: stand on the injured foot alone for a few seconds and increase the time gradually, then try standing on it with your eyes closed, then on an uneven surface such as a cushion. Practise walking gradually, then light running, then jumping and turning, only when you can walk without pain or limping. A physiotherapist explains these exercises and follows your progress, especially in moderate and severe sprains. Do not rush back to sport: returning before strength and balance are fully restored is the main cause of a repeat sprain. Consult your doctor or physiotherapist before starting exercises if the pain is severe or a fracture is suspected.

Tip

After an ankle sprain, raise your foot above heart level and apply ice in the first days, and do not rush back to sport before you have regained strength and balance — returning too early is the main cause of a repeat sprain.

Preventing another ankle sprain

After recovery, prevention remains the best protection. Continue balance exercises and strengthening of the ankle and leg muscles regularly, as they protect the joint at any wrong step. Wear suitable, stable shoes that support the ankle, avoid high heels on uneven ground, and choose sports shoes suited to your sport. In sport, warm up before playing and avoid playing when tired, as tiredness weakens balance and increases the risk of spraining. Pay attention to the ground you walk or run on, and avoid holes and uneven surfaces as much as possible. If your ankle is weak or keeps spraining, the doctor may advise a brace or supportive bandage during sport, or a longer physiotherapy programme. If the sprain returns, start proper care early and see a doctor if it does not improve within days: early intervention prevents the injury from becoming a chronic problem.

The bottom line: an ankle sprain is common, but neglecting it is costly

An ankle sprain is a common and painful injury, but most cases improve within weeks with proper care in the first days followed by good rehabilitation. Remember the warning signs that require urgent care, and do not rush back to sport before regaining strength and balance. After recovery, keep up balance and strength exercises — they are the best protection for your ankle against another injury.
