Asundexian: a new drug cuts the risk of a second stroke by 26% — who can take it and who cannot?

Share this article:

The second stroke: the danger that comes back after the first

Anyone who has had a stroke lives with a question that never leaves: will it come back? A second stroke, when it comes, is often worse than the first, and preventing it is the main concern for the patient and their doctor after leaving hospital. Today's standard prevention is aspirin or clopidogrel, two drugs that reduce blood clotting — yet the risk remains.

Now comes news of a new drug: adding asundexian to the standard treatment cut the risk of a recurrent stroke by 26%, without increasing major bleeding. So what is this drug? And how does it succeed where older blood thinners struggled?

What is asundexian, and how is it different from older blood thinners?

Asundexian belongs to a new class of drugs called Factor XI inhibitors — Factor XI is one of the clotting factors in the blood. The idea behind it is clever: to block the pathological clots that close off arteries, while preserving the body's normal ability to stop bleeding when it is injured.

Traditional blood thinners face an old dilemma: the stronger they are at preventing clots, the greater the bleeding risk, forcing doctors into a fine balance between two dangers. Targeting Factor XI aims to separate the two: preventing clots without disturbing the natural bleeding-control mechanism much. That is why the researchers watched bleeding in the trial just as closely as they watched strokes.

What did the trial find? A 26% lower risk, with no extra bleeding

The global trial covered more than 12,000 patients. Asundexian was added to the standard treatment — aspirin or clopidogrel — and the results were compared with standard treatment alone. The risk of a recurrent stroke fell by 26% in those who took the new drug.

More important than the percentage itself is that this reduction came with no increase in major bleeding — the first fear with any new drug that blocks clotting. The drug did not buy stroke prevention at the price of bleeding; it achieved both together, and that is what makes the result striking.

Who can take it — and who cannot?

The drug is not for everyone, and that distinction matters. It does not suit patients whose stroke came from the heart — for example those with atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter — because their clots form by a different mechanism and need a different treatment. It also does not suit patients with moderate or severe liver disease.

There is another point: some medicines can reduce asundexian's effect, so the doctor must review every medicine the patient takes before prescribing it. The decision here is an individual medical one, built on the type of stroke, the state of the liver and the other medicines — not a decision the patient takes alone.

Where is the drug available — and what should you not do?

The US Food and Drug Administration has approved the drug, China approved it in October 2026, and it is still under review in the United Kingdom and Europe. It is a new drug, not yet available in every country, and it may take time before it reaches many patients.

What must be stressed: do not stop your current treatment, do not buy the drug online, and do not take it without a prescription. The aspirin or clopidogrel you take today is your existing line of defence, and stopping it without medical advice exposes you to the risk of a new stroke.

Tip

If you have had a stroke or a transient ischaemic attack, take your prescribed treatment without missing a dose, and ask your doctor whether asundexian suits your case when it becomes available in your country. At the same time, remember that preventing a second stroke does not depend on one drug alone: control your blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol, stop smoking, move every day, cut down salt and saturated fat, and see your doctor regularly.

The bottom line: an important step — but not for everyone

Asundexian is an important step in preventing a second stroke: a 26% lower risk with no increase in major bleeding, in a trial of more than 12,000 patients. But it is a new drug, not available everywhere, it does not suit patients with heart-caused strokes or with moderate or severe liver disease, and some medicines interfere with it. It is a promising addition to standard treatment, not a replacement for it — and the decision to use it belongs to the doctor alone.
