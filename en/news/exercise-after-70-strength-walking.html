After 70: a year of exercise builds strength and speeds up walking — even without bigger muscles

Share this article:

After 70, can the body still get stronger?

Many people assume that growing older inevitably means losing strength and walking more slowly, and that exercise after 70 can only help a little. But a new Finnish study challenges that common belief, saying that a full year of regular exercise can strengthen muscles and speed up walking in people aged 70 to 85 — even if their muscle mass does not increase at all.

The study, led by researcher Onni Hämäläinen at the University of Jyväskylä in Finland and published in the European Journal of Applied Physiology on 24 October 2026, included 314 community-dwelling adults with a mean age of 74.5 years, about 60% of them women, all of whom were not meeting physical activity recommendations before the study began. A total of 288 of them completed both assessments, before and after the programme.

What did the programme involve?

The programme was not a single exercise, but a multicomponent programme lasting 12 months. Participants attended one supervised strength-and-balance session per week and one supervised walking-and-balance session per week, plus a home programme of strength, balance and flexibility work two to three times a week, for 20 to 30 minutes each time, often with resistance bands. The goal was also to reach at least 150 minutes of moderate activity per week, mostly walking.

The study is a secondary analysis of a randomised trial known as the PASSWORD trial, registered as ISRCTN52388040, whose participants were recruited between 2017 and 2019. A secondary analysis means the researchers returned to data from an existing trial to ask a new question: who benefits most from this kind of programme?

The results: stronger and faster on foot

After 12 months, grip strength, leg strength — that is, knee extension strength — maximal walking speed and six-minute walking distance all improved significantly, with a p value of less than 0.01 for all of these measures. For the group as a whole, six-minute walking distance rose by an average of 36.7 metres, about 37 metres, and knee extension strength rose by a mean of 40.1 newtons.

But group averages do not tell the whole story. When the researchers looked at meaningful individual improvement, they found that 46% of participants achieved a meaningful improvement in knee extension strength, 44% in six-minute walking distance, 30% in maximal walking speed, 27% in grip strength, and only 17% in appendicular lean mass. In other words, almost half of the participants clearly improved in leg strength or walking distance, while fewer than a third improved in grip strength.

The surprise: the muscles did not get bigger

Here comes the most surprising result. Despite all these gains in strength and speed, appendicular lean mass did not change significantly on average. Put differently, the participants became stronger and faster without their muscles getting bigger.

How is that possible? The researchers suggest the gains came mainly from better nervous system function and better coordination between muscles, and from improved muscle quality and efficiency, rather than from an increase in size. This is an important point for anyone who judges progress by the scales or by appearance alone: strength and the ability to walk can improve a great deal while muscle size stays the same. That is also why individual improvement in muscle mass was the lowest of the five measures, achieved by only 17% of participants.

Who benefited most? And who benefited least?

Not everyone benefited to the same degree. Nearly half of the participants — 46% — achieved a meaningful improvement in at least two of the five measures, while only about 8% improved in almost all of them. As researcher Onni Hämäläinen put it: “Some participants experienced considerable benefits, while others showed little change or even declines.” Individual responses varied widely, including some declines.

When the researchers looked for factors linked to benefiting in several areas, they found four: younger age, a lower body mass index, closer adherence to strength training, and having multiple chronic conditions. That last point deserves attention: people with several chronic conditions were more likely to improve in several areas, not less. In other words, chronic disease did not prevent benefit from the programme — it was linked to broader improvement, perhaps because those people had more room to improve from the start.

Tip

If you are over 70 and want to start strength or walking exercise, begin gradually and under supervision, and discuss the programme with your doctor first, especially if you have chronic conditions. Real progress is measured by strength, walking speed and balance — not by muscle size on the scales.

The bottom line: strength after 70 is possible, but not guaranteed for everyone

This study says that a year of supervised, progressive multicomponent exercise can strengthen muscles and speed up walking in people aged 70 to 85, even without an increase in muscle mass. Progress after 70 is not impossible, and it is not measured by muscle size, but by what the body can do: a stronger grip, stronger legs, and a faster, longer walk.

But the message also needs caution. This study is a secondary analysis, and the factors linked to benefiting are associations, not proven causes, while individual responses varied widely, including some declines. The programme was also supervised and progressive, not a do-it-yourself routine. So before starting any new exercise programme after 70, discuss it with your doctor or an exercise professional, especially if you have chronic conditions or balance problems.
