Whole-Wheat Bread and Blood Sugar: Could Your Gut Bacteria Make the Difference?

Share this article:

What did the study do?

Researchers at The Ohio State University ran a tightly controlled clinical trial to find out whether whole-wheat bread alone could improve glucose tolerance in people with prediabetes, a stage in which blood sugar is raised but not high enough for a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes. Thirty-nine adults with prediabetes took part, and the research team provided all of their meals for the whole study.

The trial used a randomized, controlled crossover design, one of the most rigorous designs in nutrition research, because every participant tried both diets and so acted as their own comparison group. Each participant followed a fully controlled diet for two weeks, with the only difference being the type of bread: whole-wheat bread or refined white bread, at 160 grams of bread a day. The two periods were separated by a two-week washout, during which participants returned to their usual diet before switching bread type. After each phase came a 75-gram oral glucose tolerance test, plus blood and stool samples to measure fasting glucose and insulin and analyse gut bacteria.

The headline result: a 27% drop in the glucose spike

The result was clear: compared with the white-bread period, the spike in blood sugar after the glucose tolerance test fell by 27% on average after the whole-wheat bread period. This improvement was not affected by differences in body weight, inflammation, insulin concentration, cellular stress or short-chain fatty acids in the gut, suggesting that the bread itself was the driving factor.

Yet the average hides an important story: results varied widely between individuals. Some participants improved a great deal, while others improved only slightly. The researchers described some people as «super responders» and others as having only marginal responses, and this variation is what led the team to look to the gut microbiome for an explanation.

What does the gut microbiome have to do with it?

The researchers found that shifts in gut bacteria were linked to how much benefit people got from whole-wheat bread, on two levels. First, before any diet change at all, some participants already carried a gut microbe signature that predicted their blood-sugar control would improve. Second, after eating whole-wheat bread, the abundance of certain bacteria shifted towards a pattern linked to better blood-glucose results.

Specifically, greater benefit was linked to a higher abundance of Agathobacter, Agathobaculum and the Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group, and a lower abundance of Streptococcus, Lachnoclostridium and Mediterraneibacter. Notably, the baseline abundance of Lachnoclostridium and the Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group predicted healthier post-meal glucose responses. First author Jillian Pierson explained that Lachnoclostridium is often associated with a more pro-inflammatory state, such as that from a less healthy diet, and that improving certain aspects of the diet helped to lower this bacterium.

Richard Bruno, professor of human nutrition at The Ohio State University and co-senior author of the study, described «two separate paradigms»: some microbes predict the benefit before people change their diet, while whole grains also reshape the microbiome in ways likely to add to that benefit.

Why does whole-wheat bread affect people differently?

Whole grains have long been viewed as healthy foods that support metabolic health, but their effects have varied greatly in earlier studies, which is what makes these findings important. They suggest part of this variation may lie in each person's unique gut bacteria, and in the substances those bacteria produce when they ferment fibre.

This idea is the basis of «precision nutrition»: dietary advice tailored to a person's own biology rather than one recommendation for everyone. Co-senior author Jiangjiang (Chris) Zhu, professor of human nutrition at Ohio State, is using computer modelling to understand how diet and the microbiome interact at the molecular level, hoping one day to develop a test that tells each person which foods suit them best.

The study's limits, honestly stated

Despite its strong design, the study has important limits. It included only 39 participants, and each phase lasted just two weeks, too short to know whether the benefits last in the long term. All participants had prediabetes, so the results cannot be generalised directly to healthy people or to people with type 2 diabetes.

Most importantly, the microbiome findings show association, not causation: bacterial changes were linked to better blood sugar, but the study did not prove the bacteria caused the improvement. Testing a person's microbiome is also still too slow and expensive for everyday clinical use. Finally, overall gut-bacteria diversity did not change; only specific types shifted, so the picture is partial and needs more research.

Tip

Choose whole-wheat bread instead of refined white bread whenever you can, and check that «whole wheat» or «whole grain» is the first ingredient on the label. Increase fibre gradually and drink enough water to avoid bloating, and pair whole-wheat bread with protein and vegetables in your meals. If you have diabetes or prediabetes, talk to your doctor or a dietitian before changing your diet.

The bottom line

This trial confirms that a simple everyday food swap, choosing whole-wheat bread instead of refined white bread, can improve glucose tolerance in people with prediabetes, with an average 27% drop in the post-meal glucose spike. It also adds new evidence that gut bacteria may explain why some people benefit from whole grains more than others, whether through the microbes they already carry or through the changes whole-wheat bread itself produces.

These results do not make whole-wheat bread a treatment or a substitute for medicine. It is one practical step within a healthy lifestyle of balanced eating, regular activity and medical follow-up. One day, choosing the best bread may rest on an analysis of a person's gut bacteria, but until then whole grains remain an evidence-backed choice for most people.
