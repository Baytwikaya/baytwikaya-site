The US has officially lost its measles elimination status after 26 years amid a record outbreak

Share this article:

Twenty-six years end with a single announcement

Since 2000, the United States had held measles elimination status — meaning the virus was no longer spreading continuously inside the country, and every case arriving from abroad was stopped before it became a sustained chain of transmission. On 17 November 2026, that officially ended, when the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), the World Health Organization's regional office for the Americas, declared the United States endemic for measles once again.

What exactly happened? How is a status that took a quarter of a century to build lost? And what does it mean for people — and for other countries?

A single transmission chain that lasted over a year

The direct cause was a single chain of transmission that began on 20 January 2025 with the West Texas measles outbreak and continued for more than 12 months without a break. When a virus keeps spreading like that for over a full year, the definition of "elimination" no longer applies, under the organization's criteria.

The announcement followed a meeting of the Regional Verification Commission in Washington, D.C., on 10–13 November 2026, and PAHO letter CIM-150-26, dated 13 November 2026. On the same day, Bolivia and Mexico were also declared endemic, while the elimination status for rubella and congenital rubella syndrome was unchanged.

The numbers: the highest toll since 1991

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recorded 5,412 confirmed measles cases in 2026 as of 12 November — the highest total since 1991, and more than double the 2,289 cases of 2025. There were also 43 outbreaks and 591 hospitalisations.

As for deaths, the CDC counted 6 in its official tally, noting that some state reports cite higher death counts than the CDC figure. And 96% of cases were in unvaccinated people or people whose vaccination status was unknown — a proportion that says a great deal about the nature of the problem.

Why did the virus come back? Vaccination coverage is falling

Measles is among the most contagious diseases, but the MMR vaccine protects against it with 97% effectiveness after two doses. Yet MMR coverage among kindergarten children fell from 95.2% in the 2019–20 school year to 92.4% in 2025–26 — below the threshold that maintains community immunity. When the gaps widen, the virus finds its way back.

And the United States is not alone: Canada lost its measles elimination status in November 2025, and the United Kingdom in January 2026 — a sign that the decline reaches beyond a single country.

How can the US regain the status? And what did the CDC director say?

To regain elimination status, 12 months must pass without continuous transmission, together with a corrective action plan submitted to PAHO. The CDC has provided about $100m in additional funding to states since August to support vaccination, surveillance and outbreak response.

CDC Director Dr Erica Schwartz said: "The measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does." It is a sentence that sums up the situation: the designation is an administrative certificate, while the real protection is in the vaccine itself.

Tip

Check today whether you and your children are vaccinated against measles — two doses of the MMR vaccine protect at 97%, and that is the real protection against the disease. If you are unsure of your vaccination status, see your doctor or your nearest vaccination centre. And if a fever with a rash appears after contact with an infected person, call your doctor first before heading to the clinic, so you do not pass the infection to others in the waiting room.

The bottom line: the status is lost, but the protection is still in the vaccine

The United States losing its measles elimination status after 26 years is not just an administrative story; it is the result of clear figures: a transmission chain that lasted more than a year, 5,412 cases in under eleven months, and falling vaccination coverage. As the CDC director put it, the designation itself is not what protects people — vaccination does. The road back to the designation runs through 12 months without continuous transmission, and the road to personal protection is far shorter: two doses of the vaccine.
