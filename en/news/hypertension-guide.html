High Blood Pressure: The Complete Guide

Share this article:

What is high blood pressure and why is it called the silent killer?

Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing against the walls of the arteries as the heart pumps it around the body. It is measured with two numbers: the upper number is the systolic pressure, the pressure when the heart contracts, and the lower number is the diastolic pressure, the pressure when the heart rests between beats. Blood pressure is considered high when it stays above the normal range for a long time. High blood pressure is called the silent killer because in most cases it causes no clear symptoms, so many people live with it for years without knowing, while it slowly damages the heart, arteries, kidneys, eyes, and brain. That is why measuring blood pressure regularly is the most important step in prevention: it is a simple, cheap test, available in pharmacies and health centres in Morocco. In this guide we explain what high blood pressure is, its causes and risk factors, how it is measured and diagnosed, what really helps to control it, and when to see a doctor urgently.

What are the normal numbers?

The World Health Organization and health bodies use a common definition of high blood pressure in adults: a systolic pressure of 140 or more, or a diastolic pressure of 90 or more, when measured twice on two different days. Normal blood pressure is usually below 120 over 80. Between these two levels there is a middle zone, sometimes called prehypertension, which is a signal to pay attention to your lifestyle before pressure rises further. It is important to know that a single high reading does not necessarily mean you have high blood pressure, because pressure changes during the day with stress, movement, coffee, and smoking. Diagnosis therefore relies on repeated measurements in calm conditions, or on home blood pressure monitoring over several days, or on a device that measures pressure over twenty-four hours. Measurement must be done correctly: sit and rest for five minutes, keep your feet on the floor, do not talk during the measurement, place the cuff on a bare arm, and avoid coffee and smoking for half an hour beforehand.

What are the causes and risk factors?

In most cases there is no single clear cause; several factors combine. The most important are advancing age, as arteries lose some of their elasticity over time, family history, excess weight, lack of physical activity, eating large amounts of salt, drinking too much alcohol, smoking, chronic stress, and poor sleep. Some diseases also raise blood pressure, such as kidney disease, thyroid disorders, sleep apnoea, and some medicines such as certain painkillers and decongestants. In Morocco, diet plays an important role: salty foods, pickles, white bread, processed foods, and fast food contain large amounts of salt, and salt is often added during cooking and at the table. Lack of movement also increases the risk, especially for people who work sitting all day or use a car for every journey. The World Health Organization notes that high blood pressure is one of the most common conditions among adults worldwide, and that many people with it do not know they have it and do not receive treatment.

What are the symptoms and warning signs?

Most people with high blood pressure feel no symptoms, and that is what makes it dangerous. Sometimes there may be a headache, dizziness, ringing in the ears, shortness of breath, chest pain, or a nosebleed, but these symptoms are not specific to high blood pressure and can have many other causes, and they cannot be relied on for diagnosis. Signs that need urgent medical care include severe chest pain, severe shortness of breath, a sudden severe headache, sudden weakness in the face, arm, or leg, sudden difficulty speaking, or a sudden change in vision. These may be signs of serious complications such as a stroke, and they require calling emergency services immediately. In the long term, uncontrolled high blood pressure increases the risk of heart disease, stroke, kidney failure, and loss of vision, which is why controlling blood pressure is not only a numerical goal but real protection for your organs.

What really helps to lower blood pressure?

There are measures proven to help lower blood pressure, and they begin with lifestyle. The first is reducing salt: try not to add salt at the table, cut down on pickles, salty cheese, processed meats, and ready meals, and read food labels when they are available. The second is eating a balanced diet rich in vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, and fish, while reducing saturated fat and fried food. Vegetables and fruit available in Moroccan markets, such as tomatoes, cucumbers, carrots, and oranges, provide potassium and fibre that support heart health. The third is regular movement: brisk walking for thirty minutes on most days of the week is an excellent step, and it can be split into short periods during the day. The fourth is losing excess weight gradually, because even a modest loss may help. The fifth is quitting smoking, reducing alcohol or avoiding it entirely, sleeping enough, and managing stress. These measures alone may not be enough for some people, and then the doctor prescribes medicines to lower blood pressure, which are effective and safe when used as prescribed, and must not be stopped suddenly without consulting the doctor.

Medicine and follow-up

If your doctor prescribes a blood pressure medicine, take it every day even if you feel well, because the medicine works to prevent complications in the long term, not to relieve immediate symptoms. Ask your doctor or pharmacist about the right time to take the medicine, its possible side effects, and what to do if you miss a dose. Do not share your medicine with another person, do not take someone else's medicine, and do not stop the medicine because one reading was normal. Keep a record of your home readings and bring it to every visit, because it helps the doctor assess how well the treatment is working. If you take other medicines or herbal supplements, tell your doctor about them, because some can raise blood pressure or interact with blood pressure medicines. In some situations, such as high blood pressure during pregnancy, treatment and follow-up are completely different and must be under specialist medical supervision; never treat yourself.

Common misconceptions

One of the most common misconceptions is that high blood pressure always causes a headache; the truth is that it is usually without symptoms, and a headache alone does not diagnose it. Another is that if you feel better you can stop the medicine; the truth is that the medicine is what keeps the pressure controlled, and stopping it brings the rise back. Another is that herbs alone treat blood pressure; the truth is that there is no strong evidence that any herb replaces medical treatment and a healthy lifestyle, and some herbs may be harmful. Another is that high blood pressure affects only older people; the truth is that it affects young people too, especially with excess weight and lack of movement. Another is that measuring blood pressure once a year is enough; the truth is that regular measurement is the only way to detect it early. If you hear medical advice on social media, check it with your doctor before following it, because not all health information on the internet is reliable.

Tip

Measure your blood pressure regularly with a reliable device, write down the readings, bring them to your doctor's appointment, and do not change your medicine dose without consulting your doctor.

The bottom line: measure your pressure and know your number

High blood pressure is common, dangerous, and silent, but it can be detected and controlled. Measure your blood pressure regularly, know your number, reduce salt, move more, eat a balanced diet, quit smoking, and take your medicine as prescribed if your doctor has given you one. Do not wait for symptoms to appear before you act, because prevention and early detection are the best protection for your heart, brain, and kidneys. If your blood pressure is high or you are unsure, consult your doctor, who will determine the right diagnosis and treatment for you.
