Years of strong painkillers and your back still hurts: what a 2026 study revealed

A question asked by millions living with chronic back pain

Imagine taking a strong medicine every day, for years, hoping it will ease your back pain — only to discover that your pain today is almost the same as that of someone with the same condition who does not take the medicine at all. This is not a theoretical assumption, but the conclusion of a scientific study published on 1 October 2026 in the journal Brain, conducted by researchers at Northwestern University in the United States — the first of its kind to link long-term opioid painkiller use with brain characteristics in patients with chronic back pain.

The context matters here: about 5 million adults in the United States alone regularly use prescribed opioid painkillers to manage chronic pain. And the question that has remained open for years is: does long-term use justify these medicines? Does pain really improve over time, or does the body get used to the medicine while the pain stays where it is? The new study answers this question with clear numbers — and a second surprise inside the brain itself.

What exactly did the researchers do?

The research team, led by researcher Marwan Baliki and principal investigator Vania Apkarian, compared two similar groups of chronic back pain patients: the first included 70 patients taking prescribed opioid painkillers daily for 7.8 years on average, at low or moderate doses and under medical supervision. The second included 70 patients with the same condition, carefully chosen to match the first group in key characteristics, but not taking these medicines. The researchers then measured pain levels, physical function and mental health in everyone, and imaged their brains with functional MRI, which tracks brain activity through changes in blood flow.

It is important to understand the limits of this study before jumping to the results: it is a controlled comparative study, not a randomized trial that gave the medicine to one group and withheld it from another. In other words, the researchers compared people who already take the medicine with others who do not, with careful matching between them, but they did not control who takes the medicine and who does not. Participants were also taking low or moderate doses under medical supervision, so the results cannot automatically be generalized to all doses or all types of pain.

The result that surprised even the researchers

The first result was striking in its simplicity: pain levels were almost identical between the two groups, about 5.4 out of 10 on both sides. After nearly eight years of daily medicine, the pain of the painkiller group was not lower than that of the other group by any meaningful amount. In fact, the medication group scored slightly worse on physical function and mental health. The principal investigator put it bluntly: the team found no sign that pain had improved in these patients after all those years of using the medicine.

But the study also carried relatively reassuring news, which the researchers emphasized: the team found little evidence that years of use had changed the brain's anatomical structure, and that the participants' core psychological and brain characteristics were essentially intact. In other words, the picture was not entirely bleak: the medicine did not improve pain, but — at these doses and under medical supervision — it did not destroy the brain's structure as some had feared.

The brain changed from the inside without changing its shape

Here lies the second surprise. While the brain's structure remained largely intact, the brain's spontaneous activity — the way brain regions work and communicate with each other — was clearly different between the two groups, especially in the brain's reward and motivation network, and in the frontal cortex responsible for decision-making. This distinction is precise and important: the shape is intact, but the way it works is different.

To understand this difference more deeply, the team developed a new computational method combining participants' MRI images with published maps of neurotransmitter receptors in the brain. Using this method, they identified molecular signatures linked to different states of chronic pain and to long-term painkiller use, including activity of serotonin receptors and opioid receptors in the brain. The researchers believe these signatures could open the door to new treatments — they even plan to test whether an antidepressant targeting the same serotonin pathway could help treat chronic pain and reduce reliance on painkillers. This is a future research project, not a proven treatment yet — a necessary distinction so that scientific hope does not turn into a premature medical promise.

In a small part of the study, the team followed 21 patients who completed a four-week pain rehabilitation program. Eight of them managed to reduce their painkiller use by more than 30 percent, accompanied by changes in brain activity linked to serotonin and opioid receptors. This is an encouraging signal, but on a small sample, and it does not by itself prove that the program was the cause of improvement for everyone.

A second study confirms the picture from another angle

The Northwestern study did not come alone in October 2026. In the British Journal of General Practice, a systematic review and meta-analysis from the University of Alberta in Canada was published, pooling 27 randomized trials comparing opioid painkillers with non-opioid alternatives in treating chronic lower back pain or joint osteoarthritis. Its criterion for benefit was achieving clinically meaningful relief — an improvement of 30 percent or more.

The result was consistent with the brain study, but from a time angle: in the first four weeks, the chance of achieving meaningful relief was higher among those taking painkillers. But in the medium term (4 to 12 weeks) this advantage almost disappeared, and in the long term (12 weeks or more) there was no longer any advantage — results even leaned in favor of the comparison groups, with the possibility that pain control worsened after 12 weeks, perhaps because the body becomes more sensitive to pain with prolonged use. The average difference in pain scores also never reached, at any stage, the threshold of clinical importance set by the review. In scientific fairness, it should be noted that this review excluded from its main analysis a type of trial known as post-selection withdrawal trials — a methodological decision debated by some researchers — but the overall direction of the results, a short-term benefit that weakens over time, remains consistent with what the Northwestern study found in real clinical practice.

The shared message of the two studies is not that strong painkillers are worthless in all cases. These medicines have their known role in short-term acute pain, and in entirely different medical circumstances such as after surgery or in palliative care — circumstances these two studies did not examine. The message is narrower and more precise: in chronic back pain specifically, continuing these medicines for years does not seem to achieve additional improvement in pain, while their burdens and risks persist.

What does this mean in practice?

First, never stop any prescribed medicine on your own. Suddenly stopping opioid painkillers after long use can cause severe discomfort and a strong rebound of pain, and any reduction must be gradual and agreed with the treating physician, with a clear follow-up plan. The two studies do not call on anyone to stop immediately; they call for regularly reviewing the treatment plan: is the medicine still achieving a clear goal? Can the same goal be achieved with less burdensome means?

Second, ask your doctor about a comprehensive plan that does not rely on medicine alone. Chronic back pain is a multifactorial condition: gradual movement, physiotherapy, good sleep, strengthening the trunk and leg muscles, and managing stress and weight are all elements usually included in modern management plans, chosen based on each patient's condition and clinical examination, not on a single general prescription. And the realistic goal in chronic pain is often not the complete disappearance of pain, but the recovery of function: walking longer distances, sleeping better, and returning to activities that stopped because of pain.

Third, track the indicators that really matter to you, not the pain number alone. Each week, note: are you walking more? Are you sleeping better? Do you need extra doses? Has your ability to work or do daily activities declined? These simple notes give your doctor a clearer picture than a general impression, and help you both judge: is the current plan working, or is it time to adjust it?

Key facts: the key numbers from the research

  • The Northwestern study (journal Brain, 1 October 2026): 70 patients taking opioid painkillers daily for 7.8 years on average, versus 70 matched patients not taking them.
  • Pain level: about 5.4 out of 10 in both groups, with slightly worse physical function and mental health in the medication group.
  • Doses were low or moderate and under medical supervision, and the study was a controlled comparative study, not a randomized trial.
  • MRI: slight change in brain structure, but a clear difference in spontaneous activity, especially in the reward and motivation network and the frontal cortex.
  • Rehabilitation program (4 weeks, 21 patients): 8 patients reduced their painkiller use by more than 30 percent, with brain changes linked to serotonin and opioid receptors.
  • The Alberta review (27 randomized trials): meaningful benefit only in the first four weeks, then the advantage faded in the medium and long term, with pain control possibly worsening after 12 weeks.

Tip

If you have been taking a strong painkiller for a long time for back pain, do not decide alone and do not stop suddenly: book an appointment to review your treatment with your doctor, and ask two specific questions: what goal is the medicine still achieving for me today? And what gradual plan — movement, physiotherapy, sleep, and follow-up — could gradually reduce my reliance on it? A medicine may be a bridge at some stage, but a bridge is not built to be a permanent home.

Conclusion: medicine is not a complete treatment plan

What the October 2026 studies say is not a call to endure pain without treatment, but a call for honesty about the results: years of strong painkillers did not give chronic back pain patients less pain than their peers who do not take them, while their brains' activity changed from the inside, and the medicines' benefit faded with longer duration in randomized trials. And chronic pain deserves a broader plan than a single pill: a plan reviewed regularly, measured by function, sleep and daily life rather than the pain number alone, and built with the doctor, not away from them. Start with the right question at your next appointment — that alone is a real therapeutic step.
