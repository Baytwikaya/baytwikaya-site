Sugar Rationing in the First 1,000 Days: Slower Biological Ageing Decades Later

Share this article:

What did the study find?

Researchers from the Chinese University of Hong Kong found that people who lived through sugar rationing during the first 1,000 days of their lives looked biologically younger in their 50s than peers who were not exposed to rationing. The study was published on 13 November 2026 in Nature Communications and included 54,201 participants from the UK Biobank, all born in Britain between October 1951 and March 1956, around the date when post-war sugar rationing ended abruptly in September 1953. The first 1,000 days are the period from conception to about the second year of life, a time of rapid growth in which much of future health is established. The researchers found that exposure to rationing during this period was associated with lower biological age acceleration, and the association was stronger the longer the exposure lasted: exposure before birth alone was linked to a biological age about 0.26 years lower, roughly three months, the difference reached about four and a half months when rationing also covered part of the first year after birth, and about five months when it extended into the second year. Exposure to rationing was also linked to a 17% lower risk of age-related cataract and a 22% lower risk of chronic kidney disease later in life.

Biological age here is not the number of years since birth, but an estimate of the body's condition calculated with a method called PhenoAge, based on chronological age and nine blood markers, including glucose, albumin, creatinine, C-reactive protein and white blood cell count: if your biological age is lower than your chronological age, your body is in better shape than expected for your age.

How did the researchers use history?

The strength of this study is that it did not rely on people's memories of what they ate decades ago, but on a natural experiment from history. After the Second World War, sugar was strictly rationed in Britain, then rationing was abolished suddenly in September 1953, and the amount of sugar available changed overnight. The researchers compared people born before that date, who were exposed to rationing during their mothers' pregnancy or their early childhood, with people born after it, who were not exposed. Because history, not family choices, decided who was exposed and who was not, the comparison is closer to a natural randomised experiment than to an ordinary observational study. The researchers adjusted their results for age, sex, place of birth and maternal smoking around birth, and found the association was more pronounced in women and remained consistent across different strata of genetic susceptibility. They also examined a comparison group of 6,105 people born outside the UK in the same period and found no comparable pattern linked to birth timing, which strengthens the idea that rationing itself is the factor behind the difference, rather than other general changes in those years.

Why might sugar in early childhood affect ageing?

The study rests on a scientific idea known as the «developmental origins of health and disease», which holds that nutritional conditions in early life programme metabolism, hormones and organ development in ways that can last for decades. Excess sugar during a period of rapid growth may affect insulin sensitivity, chronic inflammation and cellular ageing processes, which are among the nine major hallmarks of ageing studied by biologists. Earlier analyses of the same natural experiment showed that exposure to sugar rationing in the womb and early life was linked to a lower risk of type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure and a delayed onset. The new study adds another dimension: the effect is not limited to one disease, but extends to the pace of biological ageing itself and to specific conditions such as cataract and chronic kidney disease. However, no similar association appeared for several other age-related diseases the researchers examined, meaning the effect is selective rather than covering all diseases.

What does the study not prove?

Despite the strength of its design, this remains an observational study: it shows that exposure to rationing was associated with slower ageing, but it does not prove that reducing sugar in childhood directly prevents disease. UK Biobank participants are also generally healthier and less diverse than the British population as a whole, which may limit how widely the results can be generalised. In addition, wartime sugar rationing was part of a different overall food system, which included rationing of other foods and harsh living conditions, and the effect of sugar cannot be fully separated from the rest of those conditions. Lead researcher Yuelan Gao said the findings suggest that early exposure to a population-level nutritional policy is associated with biological ageing and some later-life diseases, and that they highlight the potential long-term relevance of the early-life nutritional environment. What matters for the reader is that this study concerns the first 1,000 days, pregnancy and the first two years, and it is not a call to deprive older children of all sugar, but to pay attention to added sugar in the earliest years in particular.

Tip

The practical message for mothers and fathers today is simple: cut added sugar in children's food in the early years, read food labels, and choose whole fruit and water instead of sweetened drinks and juices, because the early years are a formative period whose effects may last for decades.

What does this mean for you today?

If you are the father or mother of a young child, this study is one more scientific reason to watch added sugar in the early years: many baby foods and drinks contain added sugar without parents noticing, including juices, sweetened breakfast cereals and biscuits. Choosing whole fruit, water and plain milk, and reading labels for added sugar under its different names, are simple steps that may affect health for decades. If you are an adult, do not worry about the sugar of your childhood, which you cannot change; focus on what you can change now: cutting added sugar in your food today, physical activity and good sleep remain the strongest proven ways to slow biological ageing and protect the heart, kidneys and eyes. And remember that the study offers hope, not worry: it shows that the early nutritional environment matters, and that caring for children's nutrition today is a health investment that extends fifty years into the future.
