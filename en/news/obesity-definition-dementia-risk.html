BMI alone may miss dementia risk: a new obesity definition flags more people at risk

Share this article:

BMI is not the whole story

When people ask about their weight, the answer usually comes as a single number: body mass index, or BMI. But that number compresses a complex story into a simple equation — weight divided by height squared. It does not distinguish fat from muscle, it says nothing about where fat accumulates in the body, and it reveals nothing about whether it comes with high blood pressure or diabetes. Two people with the same BMI could be very different: one an athlete with strong muscles, the other carrying fat around the abdomen with high blood pressure and uncontrolled blood sugar.

A new study, published on 25 November 2026 in Neurology, the journal of the American Academy of Neurology, puts that number under the microscope. It was led by Dr Rui Zhou of Guangdong Pharmaceutical University in Guangzhou, China, and asked a simple question: what happens to dementia risk if obesity is defined more broadly than by BMI alone?

What is the new definition of obesity?

The new definition tested in the study is the framework of the European Association for the Study of Obesity (EASO). It does not stop at BMI: it adds the waist-to-height ratio, a measure of abdominal fat, and it also takes into account obesity-related conditions such as high blood pressure and diabetes. Obesity here is not just a number on the scales, but a picture that combines weight, fat distribution and general health.

The researchers applied both definitions, old and new, to two large groups of older adults: the ELSA study in England, with 6,542 participants averaging 65 years of age, followed for about 12 years, and the CHARLS study in China, with 12,402 participants averaging 58 years of age, followed for about 8 years.

More than 80% were reclassified as having obesity

The first finding was striking: more than 80% of the people whom BMI classified as overweight were reclassified as having obesity under the new definition. Under this definition, obesity affected 37.7% of participants in the English group and 24.9% in the Chinese group. High blood pressure was the most common condition in this group, at 42.6%, followed by diabetes at 35.1%.

In other words, the new definition does not add marginal cases: it reveals a large group of people carrying excess abdominal fat or weight-related conditions, whom BMI alone had placed in the less worrying category of “overweight”.

Dementia: a 93% higher risk — and the overweight paradox

Pooling the two groups together, people with obesity under the new definition were 93% more likely to develop dementia than people of normal weight with no other health conditions — a hazard ratio of 1.93. Dementia rates were about 8 cases per 1,000 person-years in Britain and about 12 in China among people with obesity.

The surprise came in the overweight category: people classified as overweight were 39% less likely to develop dementia, and 68% less likely to die during the study, than people of normal weight — a hazard ratio of 0.61. Their dementia rates were about 3 cases per 1,000 person-years in Britain and about 7 in China, compared with 9 in Britain and 14 in China among people of normal weight who had other health conditions. In the Chinese group alone, people with obesity were 30% more likely to die during the study.

This paradox does not mean that being overweight protects the brain. The likeliest explanation is that the comparison is made against people of normal weight who already suffer from chronic conditions, and that unintentional weight loss in older adults may itself be an early sign of disease, not of better health. The message is not an invitation to gain weight, but a warning against judging risk by a single number.

Read the results carefully: what did the study not prove?

As with any study of this kind, caution is warranted. The study is observational: it records an association between obesity and dementia, and it does not prove that obesity causes dementia — other factors, such as diet, physical activity and social circumstances, may play a role that was not fully measured. Obesity was also measured only at the start of the study, and changes in weight and health over the years of follow-up were not tracked.

Dementia, too, was defined by algorithmic criteria or by a physician's diagnosis reported by the participants themselves — a method that may miss some cases or misclassify others. The findings should therefore be read as an association, not as proof of causation.

Dr Zhou said: “These findings underscore the inadequacy of using BMI alone to define obesity,” adding that “relying solely on BMI may overlook people who could benefit from earlier risk assessment and intervention.”

Tip

Do not rely on BMI alone: measure your waist as well, check your blood pressure and blood sugar regularly, and see your doctor if your waist is wide or your blood pressure or blood sugar is high — early assessment opens the door to acting before the risk grows.

The bottom line: one number is not enough to judge risk

The bottom line is that BMI alone may hide a real risk: by adding a waist measurement and looking at blood pressure and diabetes, the new definition revealed that more than 80% of people previously classed as overweight actually have obesity, and that these people are 93% more likely to develop dementia. Overweight itself looked less risky in this study, but that does not make it a health goal. Preventing dementia starts from a broader picture: a healthy weight, a slimmer waist, blood pressure and blood sugar under control, and regular medical follow-up.
