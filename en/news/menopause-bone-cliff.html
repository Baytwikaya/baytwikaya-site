Menopause is not a slow decline… but a sudden "cliff"? What 300 million lab tests revealed

Share this article:

When the whole body changes at once

Many women think of menopause as a gradual stage: a hot flash here, some tiredness there, and bones that slowly weaken over the years. But a huge study published in October 2026 in the journal Nature Medicine paints a very different picture: at the final menstrual period, the body does not decline slowly — it jumps. A sudden jump that involves the bones, the liver, the kidneys and lipids, all at once.

Researchers at the Weizmann Institute of Science analysed more than 300 million lab tests from more than one million women in the United States and Israel, over a quarter of a century. And the headline result: 52 of 90 routine tests showed an abrupt change at the final menstrual period, while men of the same age showed no similar change. And it was not only the hormones that changed — almost all of the body's systems did.

The study: 300 million tests from over one million women

How do you study a moment that no woman records in her medical file? The team, led by researchers Glenn Pridham and Yoav Hayut in Professor Uri Alon's laboratory, borrowed a technique from astronomy — the same mathematics used to sharpen a blurry telescope image — to reorder millions of lab results by their distance from the final menstrual period, instead of by age.

The study relied on two large sources: the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) between 1999 and 2022, and the records of the Clalit health fund in Israel, which covers about half of the country's population and includes 1.3 million women aged 30 to 70 between 2004 and 2024. The team then checked the results against the long-running American SWAN study, which followed the same women year after year through the transition.

The result: when the tests are ordered by age alone, the change looks gradual — but when they are ordered by the final menstrual period, the "cliff" appears: a sharp jump in 52 of 90 tests, with jumps ranging from 0.1 to 1.4 standard deviations, the largest in the sex hormones, followed by bone, liver and lipid markers.

Bones first: a sudden jump toward density loss

If there is one system this study deserves to be read for, it is the bones. Bone health markers were among the most affected by the cliff: bone mineral density drops by about half a standard deviation on average at the transition — a large jump by public health standards. And after the cliff, osteoporosis appears in women at a much faster rate than in men, to the point that about a quarter of women have osteoporosis by age 70, according to the researchers.

This explains why fracture remains the greatest silent risk after menopause: a hip or spine fracture does not come from a single accident, but from years of density loss that suddenly accelerates at the moment of transition. And the researchers themselves say that fractures and heart disease are the most serious long-term consequences of this cliff.

Not bones alone: liver, kidneys and lipids

The cliff did not stop at the bones. At the final menstrual period, liver enzymes such as ALT and GGT also jumped — signs of liver strain — and total cholesterol, harmful LDL cholesterol and triglycerides rose, while kidney function markers and the body's fluid balance changed. In short: the whole body resets itself at once, and the general direction of most of these changes is toward a higher risk of disease.

There is one striking exception: iron and haemoglobin improved after periods stopped, and anaemia became less common, because the monthly blood loss ended. As for blood sugar — fasting, HbA1c and insulin — it rose gradually with age in women and men alike, with no clear jump at menopause, which means diabetes is a story of ageing in general, not a story of the cliff.

The cliff has a long approach: changes begin more than ten years earlier

The most important news in the study is not the jump itself, but what comes before it. Hormonal changes begin to accelerate long before the final menstrual period: AMH, which reflects the egg reserve, begins its accelerated decline about 15 years before the final period, FSH begins its accelerated rise about 14 years before, and oestradiol begins its accelerated fall about 12 years before. In other words, a woman who reaches menopause at age 50 has had hormones changing course since she was about 35.

This turns the idea of "wait for the symptoms, then act" upside down: the prevention window is open many years before the final period, not after it. The bones that will face the cliff at 50 are built — or neglected — in the forties.

Hormone therapy: smaller changes — but the evidence is still observational

The study found that women taking menopausal hormone therapy had noticeably smaller jumps, especially in bone, liver and kidney markers, so their results after the final period looked more like a smooth continuation of what came before. But the researchers themselves add a clear warning: these are observational data, from a small minority of women who take the therapy, and they do not prove that the therapy is the cause. They are planning a randomised trial to test hormone therapy before the final menstrual period, before any firm conclusion.

The practical message, then, is not "take hormone therapy", but "discuss your options with your doctor early" — the hormone decision is an individual medical choice, balancing benefits and risks for each woman separately, and it is not made from a news headline.

Tip

If you are in your forties, do not wait for the symptoms: move every day, especially resistance exercises that load the bones — brisk walking, climbing stairs, and light weights at least twice a week. Make sure you get calcium and vitamin D from food (milk and dairy products, sardines, and leafy greens), and ask your doctor about measuring your bone density regularly, and about your health options before menopause, not after it. Real prevention begins years before the cliff, not at its edge. And if you take any medicine or are considering hormone therapy, do not start or stop anything on your own — consult your doctor first.

Conclusion: prevention begins years before symptoms

The Weizmann study does not say that menopause is a catastrophe; it says it is a decisive turning point: the whole body resets itself at once, and the bones pay the highest price. But it also says something reassuring: the cliff has a long approach, and the changes begin more than ten years earlier — which means there is time to prepare. Movement, resistance exercise, a calcium-rich diet and bone density measurement, when they begin in the forties, are the investment that softens the jump when it comes. Menopause is not an unavoidable slow decline, but a cliff whose location is known — and those who know where it is can prepare for it.
