Osteoporosis prevention: the complete guide before the first fracture

Share this article:

What is osteoporosis? And why is prevention more important than treatment?

Osteoporosis is a disease that makes bones weak and fragile, so they are more likely to break even after a minor fall or a light knock. It happens because bones gradually lose density: in our youth, bones renew constantly, as bone-building cells lay down new tissue while other cells remove old tissue, but with age, and especially after the menopause in women, breakdown becomes faster than building, so density falls and the internal structure of the bone weakens. The biggest problem is that osteoporosis is a silent disease: it causes no pain in its early stages, and it is often discovered only after the first fracture, which may be a hip or spine fracture that changes the person's whole life. That is why prevention is the real foundation: bones built strong in youth and maintained in middle age resist breaking in old age. The good news is that prevention is possible at any age, because bones respond to food, movement and treatment even in advanced years, although starting early is always better.

In this guide we explain how strong bones are built, who is most at risk of osteoporosis, the signs that call for testing, and the main steps of prevention: food, vitamin D, exercise, daily habits, and when medicine becomes necessary.

How are strong bones built?

Bones reach their highest density in the late twenties, then slowly decline with time. Calcium is the main mineral in bone building, and vitamin D is what helps the body absorb calcium from food, so calcium is of little use without enough vitamin D. Protein is also essential for bone structure, and weight-bearing exercise stimulates bones to renew and strengthen. The body also needs other minerals and vitamins, such as magnesium and vitamin K, which are found in a varied, balanced diet. In Morocco, excellent sources of calcium are part of the daily diet: milk and its derivatives, sardines eaten with their bones, leafy greens such as chard, almonds and sesame. The abundant Moroccan sun is a natural source of vitamin D, but exposure should be moderate and at suitable times, with the skin protected from burning. It is important for the reader to know that building bone is a long-term process: what we eat and do today shows its effect in our bones years later.

Who is most at risk?

The risk of osteoporosis rises with age, and women after the menopause face a greater risk, because falling oestrogen speeds up bone loss. The risk is also higher for people with a family history of hip fractures, those who have broken a bone before after a minor fall, or those with a slim, small build. Medical factors include taking corticosteroids for long periods, thyroid disease and overactivity, bowel diseases that weaken the absorption of food, and some rheumatic diseases. Lifestyle factors include lack of physical activity, smoking, heavy alcohol use, and too little calcium and vitamin D in the diet. Other medicines can also weaken bones with long use, so patients should ask their doctor or pharmacist about the effect of their medicines on bones. When several risk factors come together in one person, this calls for early assessment and a bone-density test, without waiting for the first fracture.

Signs and testing: how do we detect osteoporosis early?

As mentioned, there are no clear symptoms in the early stages, but there are signs worth noting: gradual loss of height over time, a forward curve of the back, back pain with no clear cause, or a fracture after a minor fall that should not have broken a healthy bone. The main test is a bone-density measurement with a device called DEXA, a painless scan that measures bone density in the hip and spine and compares the result with that of a healthy young adult. Medical guidance recommends testing women after the age of sixty-five and men after seventy, and testing younger people if risk factors or a previous fracture exist. The doctor may also order blood tests to check calcium, vitamin D, thyroid function and the kidneys. It is important for the patient to know that a test result is not a final verdict, but a tool for assessing risk and deciding on prevention or treatment with the doctor. If the doctor diagnoses osteoporosis, this does not mean a fracture is inevitable; it means prevention and treatment have become more urgent.

Food: the foundation of osteoporosis prevention

A balanced diet is the cornerstone of prevention. Make sure to take calcium daily from food first: milk, yoghurt and cheese, sardines and anchovies with their bones, leafy greens, almonds and sesame seeds. Vitamin D comes from the sun and from food: oily fish such as sardines and salmon, egg yolks, and fortified foods. If vitamin D is low on testing, the doctor may prescribe a supplement at a suitable dose, and high doses should not be taken on one's own, because excess vitamin D can harm the kidneys. Enough protein is also important for bones, so include a protein source in every meal: eggs, fish, chicken or legumes. Reduce excess salt, because too much sodium increases calcium loss in the urine, and cut down on soft drinks that replace milk in the diet. The traditional Moroccan diet, rich in vegetables, legumes, fish and olive oil, is an excellent foundation for bone health, if we keep it and avoid excess processed foods.

Exercise and movement: the free medicine for bones

Bones respond to pressure and movement: weight-bearing exercise stimulates bone to build and strengthen. The best are brisk daily walking, climbing stairs, dancing, light resistance exercise with small weights or elastic bands, and balance exercises that reduce the risk of falling. It is recommended to do weight-bearing activity most days of the week, muscle-strengthening exercises two or three times a week, and balance exercises regularly, especially for older people. Avoid prolonged sitting: stand up and move every hour, because inactivity speeds up bone loss. If you already have osteoporosis, consult your doctor or physiotherapist before starting an exercise programme, to avoid movements that increase fracture risk, such as suddenly bending forward while carrying a heavy load or twisting the trunk forcefully. As for massage and cupping, they may relax tense muscles around the back, but they do not build bone density and do not replace food, exercise and medical treatment when needed. At home, make movement a habit: walk to the market, take the stairs instead of the lift, and take part in active household work.

Tip

Make your bones a simple daily priority: a brisk walk every day, calcium-rich foods such as milk, yoghurt, sardines and leafy greens, and moderate sun exposure — these three habits together are stronger than any single supplement.

Daily habits that protect your bones and prevent falls

Most osteoporotic fractures happen after a fall, so preventing falls is an essential part of prevention. Review your medicines with your doctor, as some medicines cause dizziness or drowsiness and increase the risk of falling. Stop smoking, because smoking weakens bones and slows fracture healing, and reduce alcohol or avoid it. At home, remove obstacles from walkways, fix down rugs, add good lighting, fit handrails in the bathroom, and wear stable, non-slip shoes. Look after your eyesight and hearing, and have them checked regularly, because poor vision or hearing increases the risk of tripping. Keep a healthy weight: severe thinness weakens bones, and excess weight increases the risk of falling and strains the joints. If you take corticosteroids or other medicines that may weaken bones for a long time, ask your doctor about a bone-density test and about ways to protect yourself. These simple habits, taken together, reduce fracture risk more than any single step.

The bottom line: strong bones start today

Osteoporosis is a silent disease, but preventing it is possible at any age: a diet rich in calcium and vitamin D, daily weight-bearing movement, stopping smoking, a fall-safe home, and early testing for those with risk factors. If the doctor diagnoses osteoporosis, modern medicines greatly reduce fracture risk when taken regularly alongside a healthy lifestyle. Start today: your bones are building their future now.
