Migraine plus smoking: stroke risk more than triples before age 60

Share this article:

Two migraine attacks a month, and a cigarette after every meal

She knows migraine attacks well: a throbbing pain on one side of the head, nausea, sensitivity to light and sound, forcing her to lie down in a dark room for hours. Between attacks, she smokes. She is in her forties, and she thinks of migraine as “just pain” and smoking as “a lung problem only”. But a large Danish study published in the journal Neurology on 18 November 2026 says that the two together raise the risk of stroke before age 60 to more than three times higher.

And one point must be stated clearly from the start: the study covered people who had given birth — women who had previously had a child — so its results cannot simply be transferred to men or to women who have never given birth. (The picture with this article is a generic illustration only.)

A huge study from Denmark: 1.7 million people over 27 years

The study was led by Dr Andreas Lund Pedersen of Aarhus University in Denmark, and it drew on Danish health and birth registries, covering about 1.7 million people aged 15 to 60 who had given birth, over a 27-year period, with 20 years of follow-up. That huge size is what makes the study important: stroke before age 60 is relatively rare, and its risk can only be measured precisely in a group this large.

The researchers divided participants into four groups: neither migraine nor smoking, migraine only, smoking only, and migraine plus smoking. They then compared the groups on three outcomes up to age 60: ischaemic stroke (a blocked artery in the brain), haemorrhagic stroke (bleeding inside the brain), and death from any cause.

The numbers: when migraine meets smoking

For ischaemic stroke, the most common type, 0.8% of the “neither” group had one before age 60, compared with 1.3% with migraine only, 1.5% with smoking only, and 2.8% with both together. In other words, the risk with both factors was 3.6 times the risk with neither.

For haemorrhagic stroke, the figures were 0.3% with neither, 0.5% with migraine only, 0.6% with smoking only, and 0.9% with both — 2.6 times higher. For death before age 60, the figures were 1.0% in the comparison group, 1.2% with migraine only, 2.3% with smoking only, and 3.4% with both — more than three times higher.

After adjusting the results for other factors, the researchers found that migraine alone raised the risk of ischaemic stroke by 85%, and smoking alone more than doubled it. Each factor is dangerous on its own; together they become more dangerous still.

The effect is bigger than the sum of its parts: what does synergy mean?

The most important finding is not the size of the risk alone, but its shape: the combined effect of migraine and smoking was greater than the sum of their separate effects, for ischaemic stroke and for death. Researchers call this “synergy” — the two factors do not simply add their risks together; one amplifies the effect of the other.

The study did not prove the mechanism of this synergy, but the likely explanations are known: migraine, especially with aura, is linked to disturbed blood-vessel function and a greater tendency of the blood to clot, and smoking damages the lining of the arteries, raises blood pressure and also increases clotting. When the two meet, the blood vessels take two hits from two different directions at the same time.

Who was in the study — and who was not?

This is where the most important caveat belongs, and it must not get lost among the numbers. First, the study is observational: it links factors with outcomes, and it does not prove that migraine or smoking directly cause stroke, however strong the association and however large its size.

Second, the study covered only people who had given birth — women who had previously been pregnant and given birth — because it relied on Danish birth registries. So the results cannot simply be transferred to men, or to women who have never given birth, or to countries where smoking patterns and health care differ from Denmark. That does not mean the risk does not exist in others; it means these particular figures were measured in this group only, and other groups need their own studies.

Third, migraine and smoking may be underreported in registries, because some people never see a doctor for migraine, and some do not declare their smoking. Smoking was also recorded during pregnancy, which may not reflect smoking over a whole lifetime. And the study did not report aura status — migraine with or without aura — even though aura is known to carry a higher stroke risk.

Fourth, and most reassuring: the absolute risk is still small. Even in the highest-risk group — migraine plus smoking — 97.2% did not have an ischaemic stroke before age 60. A large relative risk (3.6 times) means multiplying a risk that was small to begin with, not a risk that strikes everyone.

Tip

If you have migraine and you smoke, quitting smoking is the single strongest step you can take — it lowers the risk of stroke and death regardless of migraine, and benefits your heart and lungs together. If your headaches are frequent or severe, discuss a prevention plan and attack treatment with your doctor, and ask about your blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar, which are other risk factors that can be controlled. And learn the signs of stroke so you can act fast if they appear: sudden weakness in the face or arm, sudden trouble speaking, sudden trouble seeing or with balance — every minute of delay costs brain cells. Do not stop any prescribed medicine on your own — talk to your doctor first.

The bottom line: a large relative risk, a small absolute risk

The message of the 2026 Neurology study is twofold: on one hand, migraine together with smoking raises the risk of stroke before age 60 by more than three times, with a synergistic effect larger than the two factors added — a strong reason to quit smoking and to treat migraine seriously. On the other hand, the absolute risk remains small — most people in even the highest-risk group did not have a stroke — and the study is observational, covering only people who had given birth, so its figures do not transfer to others without caution. Read the news as it is: a serious warning, not a sealed fate.
