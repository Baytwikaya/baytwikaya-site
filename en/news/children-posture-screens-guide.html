Children's Posture and Screens: The Complete Guide to Protecting the Back, Sleep and Eyesight

Share this article:

Why have children's posture and screens become a health issue?

Children today spend a long time in front of phones, tablets, computers and television, whether for study, play, watching videos or talking with friends. With this great change in lifestyle, body posture while using screens has become one of the most important health issues for children and teenagers. Sitting for a long time with the head bent forward, rounded shoulders and a curved back puts pressure on the neck, back and shoulders, and it can cause pain, tiredness and poor concentration, especially when it is repeated every day for many hours.

Screens also affect other aspects of a child's health: sleep, physical activity, weight, eyesight, mood and school performance. So it is not enough to ask a child to sit up straight; we need a complete understanding that brings together correct posture, screen time, the type of content, physical activity and sleep. This guide offers parents a practical, realistic explanation, without fear or exaggeration, to help them protect their children in a digital world that cannot be switched off.

How do screens affect body posture?

When a child looks at a phone in the hand, the head tilts forward and down, increasing the weight carried by the neck muscles and the spine, and the muscles of the neck and shoulders become tense. When this posture is repeated for hours every day, the child may complain of neck pain, headaches, pain between the shoulders or lower back pain, and it may become hard to sit up straight. Sitting for a long time on a sofa or bed in a twisted posture, or carrying a heavy school bag on one shoulder, also increases pressure on the growing spine.

It is important to know that a child's body is still growing, and its bones, muscles and joints are more flexible and more affected by daily habits than an adult's body. Bad habits that start in childhood may therefore continue and worsen in adolescence and adulthood, while good early habits protect the body for many years. The good news is that posture pain in children often improves when habits improve: raising the screen to eye level, sitting on a suitable chair, taking breaks for movement, and strengthening the muscles of the back and abdomen through play and sport.

How much screen time? Recommendations by age

The World Health Organization recommends that children under two years old avoid screen time completely, and that screen time for children aged two to five years be limited to one hour a day at most, with good content and parental company. For older children and teenagers, health bodies recommend limiting recreational screen time, encouraging daily physical activity, enough sleep and family activities without screens. There is no single magic number that suits every child, but the general rule is that screen time should not take the place of sleep, movement, play, study and family time.

It is important to distinguish between screen time for study and screen time for entertainment. Studying on a computer has become necessary, but entertainment extended for hours in front of videos and games is what needs to be limited. The type of content also matters: educational, interactive content suitable for the age differs from violent or unsuitable content or short clips in succession that are hard to stop watching. Parents should set clear rules agreed with the child, such as fixed times and places for screens, and they should be role models, because a child imitates what he sees more than what he hears.

Correct posture: simple rules for home and school

Correct posture in front of a screen is simple: the screen at eye level or slightly below, the back straight and supported by the back of the chair, the feet on the floor, the shoulders relaxed, and the head in a neutral position without strong bending forward. When using a phone, it should be raised to eye level instead of looking down, and when using a tablet, it should be placed on a stand or table instead of being held in the hand. The desk and chair should suit the child's height, and the lighting should be good without reflections on the screen.

As for the school bag, it should not be heavy, it should be carried on both shoulders with two straps, close to the back, with everything the child does not need every day removed. At school, the child should sit on a suitable chair and change position regularly. At home, a comfortable study corner can be created with good lighting and a suitable chair, instead of studying on the bed or sofa. Remember that the ideal posture is not one fixed position; variety in movement and regular changes of position matter most.

Movement breaks and physical activity: the strongest solution

The strongest solution for posture and screen problems is regular movement. The World Health Organization recommends that children and teenagers practise moderate to vigorous physical activity for an hour a day on average, and limit the time they spend sitting, especially in front of screens. Playing outside, sport, walking, cycling and swimming all strengthen the muscles of the back and abdomen, improve balance and flexibility, reduce pain, and improve sleep, mood, concentration and school performance.

One good practical rule is that a child takes a short break every half hour of screen use: standing up, stretching, walking a little and looking far away, and following the twenty rule: every twenty minutes of near looking, look at something far away for twenty seconds, which rests the eyes and the neck. Family activities without screens should also be encouraged, such as walking together, playing in the garden and cooking together, because these activities build healthy habits and strong relationships at the same time. In Morocco, playing in the neighbourhood, school sport and walking to school when it is safe can all help.

Tip

Raise your child's screen to eye level, remind him to take a short movement break every half hour, and switch off screens an hour before bed.

Screens, sleep, eyesight and mood

Screens affect sleep because the blue light they emit and exciting content make sleep harder, especially when screens are used in the last hours before bed. Health bodies therefore recommend switching off screens at least an hour before bed, removing phones and tablets from the bedroom, and keeping a fixed time for sleeping and waking. School-age children need between nine and eleven hours of sleep, and teenagers need eight to ten hours, depending on age, and enough sleep is essential for growth, memory and school performance.

As for eyesight, long use of screens can cause dry eyes, eye strain, headaches and temporary blurred vision, and it increases the risk of short-sightedness in children, especially with little time spent outdoors. Playing outside in daylight should therefore be encouraged, regular breaks should be taken, and the doctor should be told about symptoms such as frequent rubbing of the eyes, getting very close to the screen, or complaining of unclear vision. As for mood, constant comparison with others on social media and cyberbullying can affect a child's self-confidence, so what the child watches should be followed and discussed with him regularly, without spying or punishment, but with open and supportive dialogue.

Signs that need a doctor's advice

Most posture pain in children improves when habits and movement improve, but there are signs that need a doctor's advice. Consult a doctor if pain continues for more than two weeks, or if the pain is severe or getting worse, or if it is accompanied by numbness or weakness in the hands or feet, or if a clear curve in the spine or a difference in the level of the shoulders or hips is noticed, or if the child suffers from frequent headaches, disturbed sleep, or a change in mood or school performance.

Also consult a doctor if the child gains weight quickly, is very inactive, or shows a clear addiction to screens that is hard to stop, because these situations need professional support, not blame. Do not hesitate to ask the paediatrician about posture, the school bag and the screen time suitable for your child's age, because the doctor can examine the spine and posture and give tailored advice. Remember that the aim is not to ban screens completely, but to teach the child to use them wisely and in balance, a skill he will need throughout his life.

The bottom line: screens used wisely and bodies that move

Children's posture and screens can be managed with simple, clear rules: a screen at eye level, supported sitting, a movement break every half hour, screen time set by age, daily physical activity, play outside, enough sleep without screens before bed, and open dialogue with the child about content. There is no need to ban technology or fear it, only to teach the child balance from an early age. Start today with one rule, such as switching off screens an hour before bed or a movement break every half hour, apply it with your child as a family, and consult a doctor about any persistent pain or worrying sign, because your child's health deserves care and follow-up.
