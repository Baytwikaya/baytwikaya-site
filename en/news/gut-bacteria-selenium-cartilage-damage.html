Gut bacteria changes and selenium disorder: new markers linked to cartilage damage in osteoarthritis

Share this article:

When cartilage damage starts somewhere unexpected

A knee that hurts climbing stairs, morning stiffness that takes a while to ease, a joint that no longer feels like it used to. Osteoarthritis is usually described as local wear and tear of the cartilage with age, excess weight or an old injury. But a new study from Sechenov University in Moscow suggests the story may reach beyond the joint itself: gut bacteria and the level of a rare mineral in the body — selenium — also appear to be linked to cartilage damage.

The study, published in the journal Biological Trace Elements Research, included 136 people and compared healthy participants with patients with knee osteoarthritis and patients with osteoarthritis of the knee and hip together. What exactly did it find? And what do the results mean — and what do they not mean?

What did the researchers do? 136 participants in three groups

The researchers divided the participants, aged 26 to 76, into three groups: 50 healthy people, 45 patients with osteoarthritis in both knees, and 41 patients with osteoarthritis in the knee and one hip at the same time. They then measured selenium in the blood, urine and hair, the concentration of selenoprotein P — the protein that delivers selenium to organs and tissues — as well as markers of cartilage damage and inflammation, and they analysed the composition of the gut bacteria from stool samples using gene sequencing.

The main marker of cartilage damage here is a protein called COMP (cartilage oligomeric matrix protein), which leaks into the blood when cartilage breaks down and is used in the laboratory as a marker of how severe the damage is.

First finding: the cartilage-damage marker is much higher in patients

COMP levels were clearly higher in osteoarthritis patients than in healthy participants: 51% higher in those whose disease was limited to the knees, and 75% higher in those with both knee and hip affected. Inflammation markers, such as C-reactive protein (CRP), were also raised in patients, and the rise was most marked in the group with multiple joints affected.

This gradient matters: the more joints affected, the higher the cartilage-damage marker — suggesting this is not about one tired knee, but about a process affecting the whole body.

Second finding: selenium and its carrier protein are lower

Selenium is a trace element the body needs in small amounts; it is part of proteins that protect cells from oxidative stress and help keep bone and cartilage healthy. The study found the lowest blood selenium and selenoprotein P levels in patients with multiple joints affected (knee and hip together), where the carrier protein was 43% lower than normal, alongside a statistically significant fall in blood serum selenium.

More important than the figures alone is how they relate: the higher the COMP level — the more cartilage damage — the lower the selenoprotein P level. The researchers also found an inverse relationship between the carrier protein and the level of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) in the blood, components of the cell walls of gut bacteria that can trigger inflammation.

Third finding: the gut bacteria are clearly different

Analysis of the gut microbiota revealed clear differences between patients and healthy participants. The share of bacteria from the Bacteroidota group was more than four times higher in knee osteoarthritis patients, and more than three times higher in patients with multiple joints affected, compared with healthy people. Other groups, such as Bacteroidia and Negativicutes, were also increased, while groups such as Coriobacteriia and some bacterial families involved in regulating inflammation and metabolism were reduced.

The researchers also found that certain bacterial taxa themselves were statistically significant predictors of selenoprotein P and COMP levels — meaning that in these data, the composition of the microbiome was linked to both selenium disruption and the severity of cartilage damage.

How do these three threads connect? The picture the researchers suggest

The researchers sketch a possible chain linking the gut to the joint: an imbalance in gut bacteria may increase the permeability of the intestinal wall, allowing bacterial components such as LPS to leak into the blood and trigger low-grade systemic inflammation, while at the same time selenium metabolism is disrupted and the carrier protein that delivers it to tissues — including cartilage — falls, leaving cartilage less protected and quicker to break down. One expert commenting on the study compared selenium to the building material of cartilage, selenoprotein P to the truck that delivers it, and the microbiome to the dispatcher that organises the trucks: if the dispatcher fails, the building material reaches the joints with greater difficulty.

But this picture is still an explanatory hypothesis built on statistical associations, not an experiment proving that changing gut bacteria or raising selenium improves cartilage. The researchers themselves stress that the next step is larger studies, including a detailed analysis of patients' diets and testing whether correcting the microbiome really affects the condition of the joints.

Read the results with care: association, not causation, and a small sample

This is the most important point in the whole article: the study found an association, not causation. It cannot say that changed gut bacteria caused the cartilage damage, or that low selenium caused it — the reverse could be true, or a third factor, such as diet, weight or lack of movement, could be affecting both. Doctors commenting on the study said so plainly: a link is not a cause, and nothing changes in medical practice today because of these results.

The sample is also relatively small — 136 people — and the study captured a snapshot at a single moment, not follow-up over time, from a single research centre. And lower selenium in arthritis patients is not new in itself; what is new is linking selenium, the microbiome and cartilage damage together in a single study of osteoarthritis.

Advice

If you have osteoarthritis of the knee or hip, do not conclude from this study that you need selenium supplements or a probiotic — the study tested no treatment, and selenium has a narrow safety margin: high doses are toxic and can cause hair loss and digestive and nerve problems. The steps proven to help today do not change: a healthy weight that lightens the load on the knee, regular movement and exercises to strengthen the thigh muscles, a varied diet rich in fibre and vegetables — which supports gut health in general — and the treatment your doctor prescribed, taken as directed. If you want your selenium level checked, ask your doctor first, and do not take any supplement on your own.

The bottom line: a new direction worth following, not a ready-made recipe

The Sechenov study of 136 people adds an important piece to the osteoarthritis puzzle: the cartilage-damage marker COMP is 51% higher in knee osteoarthritis and 75% higher when knee and hip are affected together, the selenium carrier protein is 43% lower in multi-joint disease, and the gut bacteria are clearly different — all statistically linked. But it is one piece of the puzzle, from a small sample, at a single moment in time, without proof of causation. The real value of the study is that it opens a new research direction — the gut–joint axis — that may one day lead to personalised tests or treatments. For today, the practical message stays the same: look after your weight, your movement and your diet, stick to your prescribed treatment, and leave supplements to your doctor's decision.

Key facts: the essential numbers

  • Study size: 136 people (aged 26–76): 50 healthy, 45 with osteoarthritis of both knees, 41 with osteoarthritis of the knee and hip together.
  • Cartilage-damage marker COMP: 51% higher in knee osteoarthritis alone, and 75% higher when knee and hip were affected together, compared with healthy participants.
  • Selenoprotein P, the selenium carrier protein: 43% lower in patients with multiple joints affected; the lowest blood selenium levels were in the same group.
  • Bacteroidota bacteria: their share was more than four times higher in knee osteoarthritis patients, and more than three times higher in multi-joint patients.
  • Lipopolysaccharides (LPS): the higher their blood level, the lower the selenium carrier protein (an inverse relationship).
  • The study: Sechenov University (Center for Bioelementology and Human Ecology) with an international team; Biological Trace Elements Research, 8 September 2026; DOI: 10.1007/s12011-026-05331-y.
  • The warning: association, not causation; a small sample (136); it does not prove that selenium supplements or probiotics treat osteoarthritis.
