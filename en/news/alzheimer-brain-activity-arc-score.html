Could your brain activity predict Alzheimer's years before the first forgotten name?

Share this article:

A warning written in brain activity, years before memory slips

Alzheimer's disease is usually diagnosed only after memory problems become obvious to the person and to those around them, by which time the disease has often been progressing silently. But new Stanford Medicine-led research, published on 4 November 2026 in Science Advances, suggests the brain may carry a much earlier warning, written not in what a person remembers, but in the way regions of their brain work together while they lie quietly inside a scanner.

The researchers developed a new scoring system called the Alzheimer's resilience-vulnerability connectome, or ARC score, derived from resting-state functional MRI. They found that this pattern of brain activity could predict cognitive decline, the build-up of amyloid-beta plaques, and even an Alzheimer's diagnosis, years before any symptom appeared.

What exactly is the ARC score, and how was it built?

The score is based on resting-state functional MRI, a scan that measures brain activity while a participant lies still, performing no task. The researchers focus on what is known as functional connectivity: the degree to which activity in different brain regions rises and falls together, also called the functional connectome.

To build the measure, the team compared two opposite ends of the cognitive ageing spectrum in people aged 75 and older: 180 "super agers", people whose memory capacity is typical of a 45-year-old along with normal executive function for their age, and 110 people diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease. They found distinct patterns of underactivity and overactivity across brain regions that separated the two groups, and derived the ARC score from them; it distinguished super agers from people with Alzheimer's with roughly 85% accuracy. The distinction was confirmed again in an independent group of 143 people. About 10% of older adults may qualify as super agers, depending on the criteria applied.

The point of comparing the two clear extremes was to avoid using apparently "typical" peers as a control group: such peers may already harbour early amyloid-beta plaques or other signs of brain ageing invisible on memory tests. Contrasting the extremes revealed what truly marks a resilient brain versus a vulnerable one.

Testing the score on healthy adults in their late 50s and 60s

The next step was the most important: could this score predict the future in people who were still entirely healthy? The researchers applied the ARC score to more than 1,300 people aged 55 to 70 who showed no signs of cognitive impairment. Their scores fell on a graded spectrum, lower scores resembling the super-ager pattern and higher ones the Alzheimer's pattern.

At the time of imaging, there were no clear cognitive differences between people with higher or lower scores. But as the years passed, a split emerged: those with the more Alzheimer's-like pattern declined faster in memory, attention and executive function, accumulated amyloid-beta plaques more quickly, and were more likely to be diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer's over follow-up of up to 12 years (most data covered about five to six years). Those with the most super-ager-like pattern showed the slowest declines.

Strikingly, the ARC score predicted cognitive decline better than demographics, established biomarkers such as amyloid-beta and tau, and genetics — and combining it with existing biomarkers improved prediction further. ARC scores also correlated with a broader measure of brain health known as brain age.

Why an earlier signal matters

The value of this finding lies in timing. Most known Alzheimer's biomarkers, such as amyloid-beta and tau, are only observable at relatively late stages of the disease, senior author Yu Zhang of Stanford explained. A brain activity pattern, by contrast, may reveal risk much earlier, at a stage when intervention is still possible and worthwhile.

Zhang hopes future interventions, such as cognitive training or transcranial magnetic stimulation, could nudge brain patterns toward the resilient end of the spectrum. But he stressed these interventions have not been tested for cognitive decline, and it is unknown whether shifting brain patterns would redirect the disease. His team is planning a trial to study ARC scores before and after cognitive training — a first step toward identifying which brain regions could be targeted.

Tip

If you are worried about your memory, do not wait and do not try to interpret a brain scan on your own — see a doctor for a proper assessment, because an early evaluation opens the door to follow-up and timely help. The factors with the strongest evidence for protecting the brain are within your reach today: regular physical exercise, good sleep, keeping blood pressure under control, and treating hearing loss — together, these are currently the most powerful tools we have for keeping the brain healthy as we age.

The limits, and the conclusion

Despite the excitement, the ARC score is not a diagnosis and should not be treated as one. It is a research tool for monitoring cognitive status and stratifying risk, not a clinical test that can be ordered at a clinic. It is also a single snapshot, not destiny: with age, after brain injuries, or with neuropsychiatric conditions, the share of super-ager-like scores tends to fall, while early interventions might shift the pattern the other way.

The results also need confirmation in more diverse populations and larger studies before any clinical use, and the score relies on MRI — an expensive, not widely available scan — rather than a simple blood test. Even so, the core message is clear: the way the brain works at rest may reveal the future of memory before memory begins to fade, and the earlier we know the risk, the greater our chance of acting against it.
