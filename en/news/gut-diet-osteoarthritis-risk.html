A gut-friendly diet and stronger joints: can the way you eat protect your knees from osteoarthritis?

Share this article:

Can what you eat protect your knees?

Osteoarthritis is the most common degenerative joint disease in the world, affecting more than 500 million adults. It is usually seen as an inevitable result of ageing or of wear and tear on the joint, but a new study published on 26 October 2026 in The Journal of Nutrition suggests that what we eat may matter more than we thought, and that an eating pattern friendly to gut bacteria may be linked to a lower risk of osteoarthritis, especially of the knee.

The researchers followed 97,569 participants from the UK Biobank, none of whom had osteoarthritis at the start of the study, for a median of 11.6 years. During that time, 12,860 new cases of osteoarthritis of all types were recorded, including 4,392 cases of knee osteoarthritis. So what set apart those who developed the disease from those who did not? According to the study, the answer begins on the plate.

The headline figures: 6% and 9% lower risk

Comparing participants by their scores on the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota, the researchers found that those with the highest scores were 6% less likely to develop osteoarthritis than those with the lowest scores, for all types of osteoarthritis combined, a hazard ratio of 0.94. For knee osteoarthritis specifically, the risk was 9% lower, a hazard ratio of 0.91. These are relative risks: they describe the difference between the two groups, and they do not mean that diet prevents osteoarthritis, or that 6 in every 100 people will avoid it.

A figure of 6% may look modest for one person, but across a whole population it means thousands of fewer cases, especially for a disease this common, affecting more than 500 million people worldwide. And more importantly, this was not the only link in the study: when the researchers looked at what was happening inside the body, they found a much stronger link.

What is the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota?

The Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota, shortened to DI-GM, is a score made up of 14 dietary components, developed by researchers led by Kase and colleagues, which reflects how closely eating habits align with foods linked to healthy gut bacteria. The foods that raise the score are dietary fibre, whole grains, fermented dairy products such as plain yoghurt and kefir, and legumes such as lentils, chickpeas and beans. The foods that lower the score are red meat and refined grains, such as white bread and white rice.

What matters here is that the index does not measure gut bacteria themselves; it measures the eating pattern linked to them, so it is an indirect proxy. No one can tell from a single meal what is happening in their gut, but eating patterns repeated over years leave their mark. That is what makes the findings interesting, but it is also one of the study's limitations, as we will see.

The metabolic signature: a much stronger link

The striking part of the study is that the researchers did not stop at what people ate: they also studied a metabolic signature in the blood linked to this eating pattern, a set of chemical markers that reflect how the body responds to food. Comparing the highest third of this signature with the lowest third, the risk of osteoarthritis was 23% lower for all types, a hazard ratio of 0.77, and 34% lower for knee osteoarthritis, a hazard ratio of 0.66. In other words, the link through the metabolic signature was almost four times as strong as the link through the diet score alone.

Mediation analyses suggest that lipid-related metabolism plays a role in this link, the way the body processes fats and cholesterol. A gut-friendly diet may therefore affect the joints not directly, but through changes in fat metabolism and in the body's internal environment. This is a hypothesis that needs more research, but it opens the door to a deeper understanding of the relationship between diet and joints.

What about genes and body weight?

Another question many people ask is: what if osteoarthritis runs in my family? In this study, the associations stayed robust across different levels of genetic susceptibility, and a favourable metabolic profile was linked to a lower risk of osteoarthritis even in people with high genetic risk. In other words, genes did not cancel out the link, nor did they make diet irrelevant, according to these data.

But before getting carried away, the study's limits need a careful read. First, it is observational: it records an association and does not prove that diet protects against osteoarthritis, because people with healthier eating patterns may also differ in weight, movement, health care and other factors. Second, gut bacteria were not measured directly, so the index is only a proxy for them. Third, diet was assessed from 24-hour food recalls, which may not reflect usual eating habits. Fourth, most participants were of White European ancestry, so the results may not apply to everyone. And finally, body weight explained part of the associations, and weight itself is an important factor in knee osteoarthritis.

Tip

Start small: swap white bread for whole-grain bread, add a dish of lentils or chickpeas to your meals, and eat plain yoghurt regularly, with olive oil as part of a balanced diet. Diet is a helping step, not a treatment, and anyone with knee pain should see a doctor.

The bottom line: a gut-friendly plate, within a bigger picture

In short, the 2026 study in The Journal of Nutrition, following 97,569 people for a median of 11.6 years, found that an eating pattern rich in fibre, whole grains, legumes and fermented dairy was linked to a lower risk of osteoarthritis, 6% lower for all types and 9% lower for knee osteoarthritis, and that the metabolic signature linked to this pattern was associated with a risk lower by 23% and 34% respectively. But these are relative figures from an observational study, not a medical prescription: diet is one piece of a bigger picture that includes weight, movement and medical care, and anyone with knee pain should see a doctor before changing their diet or treatment.
