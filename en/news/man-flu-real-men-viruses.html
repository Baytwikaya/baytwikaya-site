Is 'man flu' real? A mouse study finds males suffer worse viral complications than females

Share this article:

When a mild cold seems to hit a man harder

Everyone knows the joke: a man catches a mild cold and takes to bed as if it were a severe flu, while a woman carries on working through the same cold. The phenomenon even has a popular name — "man flu" — usually told as a joke. But a new study from McMaster University in Canada suggests there may be something real behind the joke: biological sex may genuinely affect how severe a viral infection becomes.

Caution is needed from the start, though: the study was done in mice, not in people, and it used the Zika virus — not the flu or the common cold. It does not prove that men suffer the flu more than women, but it offers new evidence that the immune system may respond to viral infection differently in males and females.

What did the McMaster researchers do?

The study was led by Professor Ali Ashkar of McMaster University in Hamilton, Canada, together with PhD student Alexa Mansour, and was published in the Journal of Infectious Diseases on 5 November 2026. The team infected mice of the C57BL/6 strain that lack the type I interferon receptor (Ifnar−/−) — a key part of antiviral defence — with Zika virus, strain PRVABC-59, at a dose of 4 × 10⁵ plaque-forming units, injected into the footpad. The researchers used male and female mice of the same age (8–16 weeks), nearly genetically identical, infected with the same amount of virus from the same source: 14 mice in each group.

The aim was not to study Zika itself, but a broader question: does biological sex influence the severity of neurological disease caused by an acute viral infection?

What did they find? Males were hit harder despite the same viral load

The result was clear: male mice developed far more severe neurological symptoms than females, including weakness and paralysis of the hindlimbs, and the difference was highly statistically significant (P < 0.001). Most females, by contrast, were largely protected, and only a few showed mild weakness.

The surprise was that this difference was not caused by the amount of virus: viral levels in the blood and brain were comparable between the sexes, and weight loss was comparable too. The virus itself was no worse in the males — the body's response to it was what differed.

When the researchers examined the central nervous system, they found that the total number of infiltrating immune cells was similar in both sexes, but their composition differed: females had a higher proportion of CD4+ T cells and a lower proportion of CD8+ and NKG2D+CD8+ bystander-phenotype T cells. This suggests that the type of immune response — not the number of its cells — is what makes the difference in disease severity.

A discovery made by accident

First author Alexa Mansour says the finding came about almost by chance: the lab had been studying Zika in mice for some time when, suddenly, the virus's characteristic neurological symptoms stopped appearing. It turned out that all the mice in that cohort were female. When male mice were reintroduced into the study, the severe complications returned. Mansour says the team first doubted its own methods before becoming convinced that biological sex was the differing factor.

Why does the male immune system respond more strongly?

Professor Ashkar explains that the male immune system seems to respond more strongly to viral infections, leading to more inflammation or damage at the site of infection. The problem is not a weak immune system, but an overstrong response: the severe inflammation itself is what damages tissue and worsens the disease.

This result fits with earlier studies showing that males are more likely to experience severe outcomes from viral infections, including COVID-19, influenza and West Nile virus. Ashkar says the field is increasingly accepting that the outcomes of acute viral infections typically differ between males and females.

He sees practical implications for medical care: for any acute viral infection, physicians should focus early on controlling inflammation in male patients, noting that approved and effective anti-inflammatory medicines already exist, and that what is needed is not necessarily new treatments but treatment plans tailored by sex. That is the researcher's view, not yet an official recommendation in medical guidelines.

The study's limits: what it has not proven

Before generalising the result, its limits must be read honestly. First, it is a mouse study — genetically modified mice lacking the type I interferon receptor, meaning they are deprived of an antiviral line of defence that a healthy human has, so the results cannot be transferred to people directly. Second, the virus studied is Zika, a virus that causes neurological complications — not the flu or cold virus that the phrase "man flu" refers to. Third, the study is a brief report, not a clinical trial in humans, and it says nothing about how severe the symptoms a patient feels are, such as coughing, body aches or fever. It is evidence for a biological principle — that sex affects the immune response — not a verdict on who suffers more from a cold.

And in the opposite direction: women's pain is not taken as seriously

Emarat Al Youm adds to the story a finding from a separate study, in exactly the opposite direction: American researchers found that observers described women's pain as lighter, and more treatable by psychotherapy than by medicine, even when the level of pain was the same in both sexes. Experts warn that this bias may deprive women of the medicines they need, as a result of an old stereotype that men are more patient with pain. The picture is therefore two-sided: men may suffer worse complications from viral infection, while women may not get the same recognition for their pain when they seek treatment.

Advice

Whether you are a man or a woman, the practical message is the same for any acute viral infection: rest, fluids, and fever relief when needed, and watch for danger signs — difficulty breathing, chest pain, a persistently high fever, or a sudden deterioration — and see a doctor immediately if they appear, especially for older people and those with chronic illness. Do not take antibiotics for a cold or flu on your own: they do not treat viruses. And if you suffer pain that is not being taken seriously, describe its severity and its effect on your sleep and work clearly to your doctor, and ask for a full assessment.

The bottom line: "man flu" is not just a joke — but it is not a final verdict either

The study's conclusion is that biological sex is an important factor in how severe a viral infection becomes: in a controlled mouse experiment, males suffered far worse neurological complications than females despite the same viral load, because of a difference in the composition of the immune response. This gives the phrase "man flu" a possible biological basis, and it fits earlier observations of worse outcomes in males from COVID-19, influenza and West Nile virus. But the evidence is still in mice, about Zika virus, and in mice lacking a key immune defence — so it cannot be presented as a confirmed human fact. What can be said with confidence today is that a viral infection is not the same experience for everyone, and that medicine is moving towards treatment that takes biological sex into account.
