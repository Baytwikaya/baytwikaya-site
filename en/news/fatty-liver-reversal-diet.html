Fatty liver: can food reverse it? What two randomised trials said in 2026

Share this article:

A fatty liver with no symptoms… until it is too late

Many people learn their liver is fatty by accident, during an ultrasound done for another reason, without feeling any pain at all. Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease — MASLD, formerly known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease — is today the most common chronic liver condition worldwide, linked to obesity, diabetes and insulin resistance. It ranges from a simple build-up of fat in liver cells, through fatty liver inflammation (MASH) to fibrosis, and it can end in cirrhosis if the cause is not treated.

The good news is that the early stages can be reversed, and that food and exercise are the first treatment doctors recommend for most patients — no drug replaces them for most people. But the question that keeps coming up in clinics is: which diet? The Mediterranean diet? Low-carbohydrate? Low-fat? Two randomised trials published in 2026 answered with a reply that may surprise many: the type matters less than people think.

Trial one: Mediterranean diet or low-carbohydrate diet?

In a study published in JHEP Reports in 2026, researchers followed 173 patients with fatty liver plus overweight or obesity for six months; 148 of them completed the trial. Patients were randomised into three groups: a Mediterranean diet, a low-carbohydrate high-protein diet, or standard dietary advice. Liver fat was measured with the controlled attenuation parameter (CAP), a non-invasive measure of how much fat the liver holds.

The result: all three groups improved in weight, body mass index and metabolic profile, and liver fat (CAP) fell significantly in the low-carbohydrate and standard-advice groups, with no significant difference between the diets. In other words, the Mediterranean diet did not beat the others, and the low-carbohydrate diet did not beat the others either — every route led to a similar improvement, as long as it produced weight loss and less ultra-processed food.

Trial two: Mediterranean or low-fat?

The same finding came from a second trial, published in Hepatology Communications on 24 November 2025, which enrolled 250 adults with fatty liver and a body mass index of 25 or more, randomised to a moderately hypocaloric Mediterranean diet or a low-fat diet for 12 weeks. Both diets reduced liver fat (CAP), liver stiffness (LSM) and weight, with no significant difference between the groups.

What stands out is that the researchers also looked at the PNPLA3 gene type, a gene known to influence how much fat builds up in the liver — and found that it did not change the response: people carrying the "riskier" gene type benefited from the diet just as much. Genes, in other words, do not condemn anyone to failure, and changing what you eat helps regardless of gene type.

Does the improvement last after the diet ends?

The most important question after any short diet is: does the benefit stay, or does everything return to how it was? A study published in the Scandinavian Journal of Gastroenterology in 2025, which followed 74 patients for a full year after short dietary interventions, found that the improvements in liver fat, weight and liver stiffness were still there one year later. When a dietary change becomes a habit rather than a temporary phase, it leaves a lasting effect — an encouraging message for anyone who fears their effort will be wasted after a few months.

But these studies are relatively small and short, and they did not compare diets on longer-term outcomes such as fibrosis or complications over years. They prove that liver fat falls with food — they do not prove that every case is cured, or that anyone can skip medical follow-up.

What is the practical message? Losing 5 to 10% of your weight

The medically accepted target for reducing liver fat is losing 5 to 10% of body weight — a realistic goal: a person weighing 90 kilograms needs to lose between 4.5 and 9 kilograms. No drug replaces diet and exercise for most patients; when a drug is prescribed, it complements them rather than replacing them. Simple habits help along the same path: coffee, vegetables and whole grains, less ultra-processed food and sugary drinks, and avoiding alcohol entirely.

And the step that must not be skipped is having a doctor assess the stage of the disease: an ultrasound alone says the liver is fatty, but it does not say whether there is fibrosis — and fibrosis is what determines how serious the condition is and what follow-up is needed. So do not diagnose yourself from a single scan result, and do not rely on a supplement or a "liver-cleanse" drink — there is no drink that cleans the liver; the liver cleans itself when we lift the burden from it.

Tip

If your doctor tells you your liver is fatty, do not panic and do not ignore it: choose the diet you can stick to for months, not weeks — Mediterranean, low-carbohydrate or low-fat, since the studies say the result is similar — and aim to lose 5 to 10% of your weight gradually, with daily movement, less ultra-processed food and sugary drinks, and no alcohol. See your doctor to assess the stage of the disease (a fibrosis check), follow your progress with them, and do not rely on self-diagnosis from an ultrasound result alone.

The bottom line: not the diet's name, but what it does for you

Two randomised trials in 2026 agreed on one finding: the Mediterranean, low-carbohydrate and low-fat diets all reduced liver fat by the same amount, genes did not change the response, and the improvement lasted a full year after short interventions. Early-stage fatty liver can be reversed — but what reverses it is not the name of the diet written on a piece of paper; it is weight loss, less ultra-processed food and sticking with it — together with a medical assessment of the disease stage, since fibrosis is what determines the real risk.
