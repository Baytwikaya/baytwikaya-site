Milk peptides and memory: what a new Korean trial and earlier studies actually showed

When names start slipping away

You walk into a room and stop, wondering why you came in. The name of a neighbour you have known for years sticks on your tongue, and an everyday word vanishes, then returns an hour later. These moments are familiar to many people after 55, and they are often a normal part of ageing — but they raise a fair question: can food really help memory?

News from South Korea has put that question back in the spotlight: a trial announced that peptides derived from milk improved memory, concentration and verbal ability in adults living with memory decline. Behind it sits a Japanese study published this year, testing one specific milk peptide in very small doses. So what do we actually know — and what is still just an announcement?

What did the Korean trial announce?

The trial was announced on 18 November 2026, as reported by the Korean newspaper Herald Corp, and was led by Neo Cremar together with Korea University, CJ Wellcare, Yonsei University and Yonsei Dairy, and announced by the Korea Institute of Planning and Evaluation for Technology in Food, Agriculture and Forestry.

It enrolled 100 adults aged 55 to 85 with memory decline, who took 1,000 mg of milk-derived peptides daily for 12 weeks, against a placebo. According to the announcement, the peptide group showed statistically significant improvements in memory, concentration and verbal ability.

But here is the decisive point: the press release published no specific figures — no effect sizes, no per-group numbers, no trial design details. We do not know how large the improvement was, how many participants finished the trial, or how each aspect of memory was measured. That makes the news a first signal, not complete evidence.

The published evidence: one small peptide, in small doses

The picture is clearer in a Japanese study published this year in Food Research Supplement (2026, volume 1, issue 2, article 10006), led by Yamada and colleagues. It was a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in healthy elderly Japanese adults aged 65 and over, who ate a test food containing 2.4 mg a day of a peptide called LNDP — short for lactononadecapeptide — a commercial product named Surasura Care from Asahi, for 24 weeks. Memory improved in the peptide group on the Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure test and on the total score of the Symbol Digit Modalities Test.

This is not the first study of this peptide: in 2018, a trial by Ohsawa and colleagues, published in the International Journal of Food Sciences and Nutrition, found that eight weeks of milk fermented with Lactobacillus helveticus and containing the same peptide improved attention and delayed memory in healthy middle-aged adults.

And the doses in this literature are very small and narrowly ranged: between 2.1 and 4.2 mg a day for 8 to 24 weeks. That is entirely different from the Korean trial, which used a milk peptide mixture at 1,000 mg a day — a different product with a different composition, so the results of one cannot simply be transferred to the other. In June 2026, Maypro launched a product called PeptiDrive-LNDP in North America, meaning this peptide has begun reaching the market as a supplement.

Read the results carefully: what was not published?

The gap between the Korean announcement and the Japanese study is the gap between news and evidence: the Japanese study is published and peer-reviewed, with a named dose, a named duration and named memory tests, which any researcher can read and check. The Korean trial, so far, is a press release without figures, and we do not yet know whether its results will be published in a peer-reviewed journal, or when.

And even with the published study, exaggeration must be avoided: the participants were healthy older adults, the improvement was on specific memory tests, not on daily life, and nothing shows that the peptide prevents or treats dementia. Memory declines for many reasons — from poor sleep and stress to vitamin B12 deficiency and thyroid disorders — and no single product treats them all, however encouraging its early results.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the results

  • The Korean trial: 100 adults, aged 55–85, with memory decline; 1,000 mg of milk peptides daily for 12 weeks, against a placebo.
  • The announced result: statistically significant improvement in memory, concentration and verbal ability — with no specific figures published.
  • The LNDP study, 2026: healthy Japanese adults aged 65+; 2.4 mg daily for 24 weeks; memory improved on the Rey-Osterrieth and Symbol Digit Modalities tests.
  • The Ohsawa study, 2018: eight weeks of L. helveticus-fermented milk with LNDP; better attention and delayed memory in healthy middle-aged adults.
  • Doses in the literature: 2.1–4.2 mg a day for 8–24 weeks.
  • Commercial launch: PeptiDrive-LNDP in North America, June 2026 (Maypro).
  • The Korean announcement: 18 November 2026, via Herald Corp; announced by the Korea Institute of Planning and Evaluation for Technology in Food, Agriculture and Forestry.

Tip

If you notice your memory slipping, do not buy a supplement expecting it to prevent dementia — no product has been shown to do that. The useful steps are the unglamorous ones: a medical check to look for treatable causes such as poor sleep, low vitamin B12 or thyroid problems, regular physical activity, steady sleep, and a varied diet rich in vegetables, legumes, whole grains and fish. And if you take any medicine, do not stop it or replace it on your own — talk to your doctor first.

The bottom line: a promising signal, and evidence still in its early days

A Korean trial of 100 adults says milk peptides helped memory, concentration and verbal ability — but it has published no figures, so the claim cannot yet be weighed. Behind it stands one published study of a specific peptide, LNDP, at 2.4 mg a day for 24 weeks, with better scores on two named memory tests, plus an earlier eight-week trial with the same peptide. That is a real, modest foundation — and a reminder that a press release is the start of the story, not its end. For now, the honest message is simple: the signal is worth watching, the evidence is still young, and memory deserves a medical check before any supplement.
