Sleeping less than 6 hours? Your joints pay the price — what a 2026 study revealed

Sleep and joints: an unexpected relationship

Many people think joint pain is caused only by age, effort, or weight, and that sleep is just a rest period with nothing to do with joints. But recent research overturns this picture: poor sleep does not only result from pain, it may itself be one of the causes of joint deterioration. A large study published in 2026 in the journal Arthritis Care & Research followed the relationship between sleep and osteoarthritis, and its results deserve to be known by everyone who wakes up tired with aching knees.

What did the study find?

Researchers examined sleep habits and their relationship with the risk of developing knee and hip osteoarthritis over time. The results were clear: people who regularly sleep less than six hours a night faced a much higher risk of knee or hip osteoarthritis compared with those who sleep about seven hours. In general, short or poor-quality sleep was associated with a 20 to 40 percent higher risk of osteoarthritis.

Nor did it stop at sleep duration: people who regularly work night shifts recorded a 24 percent higher risk of knee osteoarthritis, and a 28 percent higher likelihood of needing a full knee replacement. These relationships held even after adjusting the results for weight and other health factors that may affect the joints.

The key point: sleep may be a cause, not just a result

What makes this study different is that the relationship appeared even among participants who had no chronic joint pain at the start of the study. This suggests that poor sleep contributes to the development of joint problems, and is not merely a result of them. The effect was also stronger on the knee than on the hip, suggesting that the body’s biological clock may affect joints in different ways.

How does short sleep harm the joints? Three mechanisms

  • Inflammation: During healthy sleep, the body regulates its immune activity and repairs damaged tissue. Short sleep disrupts this process and leads to chronic low-grade inflammation. Another study in older men found that poor sleep health in people with osteoarthritis was associated with higher C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), two inflammation markers directly linked to arthritis.
  • Pain sensitivity: People who do not sleep well feel pain more sharply; disturbed sleep changes pain perception, so the same inflammation feels more painful.
  • Disrupted nightly repair: Disruption of the circadian rhythm (the biological clock) interferes with the natural repair processes that take place during sleep, and over time may contribute to cartilage erosion and joint damage.

The picture is completed by a well-known vicious circle: joint pain interrupts sleep, interrupted sleep raises inflammation, higher inflammation increases pain, and stronger pain interrupts sleep further. Breaking this circle starts with sleep itself, not with painkillers alone. A multicenter study also found that better sleep was associated with less worsening of knee pain in people with widespread pain — meaning that improving sleep may actually slow the deterioration of pain.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the research

  • Regularly sleeping less than 6 hours: higher risk of knee and hip osteoarthritis, compared with about 7 hours as the reference sleep.
  • Short or poor-quality sleep: 20 to 40 percent higher risk of osteoarthritis.
  • Regular night work: 24 percent higher risk of knee osteoarthritis, and 28 percent higher likelihood of knee replacement surgery.
  • Poor sleep health in people with osteoarthritis: associated with higher inflammation markers CRP and IL-6.

Tip

Make your sleep a priority like medicine: a fixed bedtime and wake-up time, 7 to 9 hours, a dark and cool room, no screens for an hour before bed, and light daily movement. And if joint pain keeps waking you despite improving your sleep, consult a specialist — sleep is an important step, but it does not replace proper diagnosis and treatment.

The practical message

The practical message of this research is simple and strong: sleep is a modifiable factor, like movement and weight. Improving your sleep may not remove existing osteoarthritis, but it may slow its worsening, ease pain, and delay the need for surgery. Start tonight: sleep well, and your joints will thank you tomorrow.
