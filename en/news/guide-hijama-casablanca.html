A guide to hijama in Casablanca: types, how to choose a centre, and what happens in a session

Share this article:

What is hijama?

Hijama is an ancient therapeutic practice in which special cups are placed on the skin to create local suction. Many people in Morocco turn to it to ease muscle tension and the feeling of heaviness, and to look after their body in general. It is a practice rooted in traditional medicine, still widespread today in Casablanca and in every Moroccan city — so much so that "hijama Casablanca" is one of the most frequent searches by the city's residents when they look for a nearby, trustworthy centre.

This guide explains it simply: what the types of hijama are, how to choose a good hijama centre, and what actually happens during a session, so that you walk into your first session knowing what to expect.

The types of hijama

Hijama is not a single practice, and the difference between the types matters when you choose the session that suits you:

Wet cupping (hijama sanguine)

This is the best-known type. After the skin is cleaned and disinfected, the practitioner places the cups to create suction, then makes very fine, shallow scratches at specific points, and replaces the cups to draw out a small amount of blood. It is done with sterile equipment and single-use medical plastic cups — a condition that is not negotiable.

Dry cupping (hijama sèche)

It relies on suction alone, with no scratching of the skin. It is mostly used for muscle relaxation and to stimulate blood circulation in the treated area, and it is a good option for those who do not want wet cupping or for whom it is not suitable.

Sunnah cupping

This is the cupping that follows the points and days mentioned in the Prophetic Sunna. Many people in Morocco make a point of having Sunnah cupping on its known days of the lunar month; you can check the dates on the cupping times page of our website.

How to choose a hijama centre in Casablanca?

The right choice starts with hygiene and professionalism, not with advertising. Before booking at any hijama centre, check these points:

  • Hygiene first: a clean, organised place, sterilised equipment, and single-use medical plastic cups opened in front of you.
  • A qualified practitioner: the person who performs the hijama is experienced, explains what they will do, and asks about your health before starting.
  • Questions about your health: a good centre asks about your illnesses, medicines and pregnancy before the session, and refuses to perform hijama if it is not suitable for you.
  • Clear information: you know in advance what the session includes, how long it takes, and what to do afterwards.
  • A genuine reputation: look for real reviews and experiences from the centre's clients, and for a centre with a fixed, well-known address in the city.

If you visit a centre and these conditions are not met, it is better to look for another one. Hijama touches the skin directly, and hygiene in it is not a secondary detail.

What happens during a hijama session?

If you have never tried hijama, here is what usually happens, step by step:

  1. Welcome and conversation: the practitioner welcomes you, asks why you came and about your state of health, and explains the type of hijama suited to you.
  2. Preparation: you lie down on the treatment table, and the skin is cleaned and disinfected at the chosen points.
  3. Placing the cups: the cups are placed on the skin to create suction. You feel a slight pulling of the skin — a familiar sensation the body quickly gets used to.
  4. The session itself: the cups stay in place for a short time; in wet cupping, the practitioner makes the fine scratches and then replaces the cups. You can tell the practitioner at any moment about any discomfort you feel.
  5. After the session: the cups are removed, the areas are cleaned and covered. The practitioner explains what to do after the session, such as resting, drinking water and avoiding intense effort on the same day.

A single session usually takes less than an hour. The cups may leave light circular marks on the skin that fade on their own within a few days — a normal, well-known effect.

An important safety note

Hijama is a traditional practice and not a substitute for medical treatment. If you are pregnant, take blood-thinning medication, or suffer from a chronic illness or a blood-clotting problem, consult your doctor first before any session, and tell the practitioner your full state of health. A serious centre is one that asks you about all of this before starting.

The takeaway: choose with your eyes open

Hijama Casablanca is offered by many centres, but the real difference is made by the centre itself: hygiene, single-use cups, and a practitioner who asks about your health before starting. Frequently asked questions

Does hijama hurt?

Most people describe it as a slight pulling of the skin rather than pain. In wet cupping, the scratches are shallow and fine, and the practitioner tells you about every step before doing it.

How long does a hijama session take?

A single session usually takes less than an hour, from welcome to the end of the session and the aftercare advice.

Does hijama leave marks on the skin?

Yes, the cups may leave light circular marks that fade on their own within a few days — a known effect of hijama.

Is hijama suitable for everyone?

No. There are situations where hijama is not suitable, such as pregnancy, some blood disorders, and taking blood thinners. That is why a good practitioner asks about your state of health first, and it is best to consult your doctor if in doubt.
