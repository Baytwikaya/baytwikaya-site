Can type 2 diabetes go into remission without losing muscle? What a 2026 trial revealed

Share this article:

When weight loss becomes a new problem

Anyone living with type 2 diabetes and obesity hears the same advice over and over: lose weight, and your blood sugar will improve. The advice is correct — but it hides a cost that few people talk about: every kilogram lost quickly takes some muscle with it. And muscle is not just about appearance: it is the body's engine, burning sugar and preserving strength and balance. Losing it makes it harder to keep the new weight off, and increases the risk of frailty as you age.

That is why a new trial published in October 2026 caught attention: instead of asking "does weight go down?", it asked two questions at once — can diabetes go into remission, and can that happen without losing muscle? The trial combined a very-low-calorie diet with supervised exercise, and its results after 24 weeks were strong — with important caveats that must be read before any excitement.

The trial: an intensive programme from two Canadian and British universities

The trial, called RESET for Remission, was led by researchers from McGill University in Montreal and the University of Leicester in England, with the University of Alberta taking part, and its results were published in The Lancet Regional Health – Americas in October 2026, and presented at the annual meeting of the European Association for the Study of Diabetes in Milan. It enrolled 96 young men and women aged 18 to 45 with early-onset type 2 diabetes — diagnosed less than about six years earlier — plus obesity, and not using insulin.

Participants were randomly assigned to two groups: 50 people in the intensive programme and 46 in usual care. The programme lasted 24 weeks, starting with an intensive 12-week phase: a very-low-calorie diet of 800 to 900 calories a day, with ready-made meal replacements in the first two weeks, plus supervised exercise twice a week and a third session on their own — aerobic and resistance exercise together. Then came a second 12-week phase: a healthy diet to maintain the lost weight, while continuing exercise. The care team stopped the programme participants' diabetes medicines from the start, under full medical supervision.

First result: less weight, muscle preserved

After 24 weeks, the programme group had lost on average 8.4 kilograms, compared with only 1.2 kilograms in the usual-care group — about 7.5 kilograms more. More important than the number itself is where it came from: body measurements showed that what was lost was mostly fat, while muscle was preserved. Lead researcher Thomas Yates of the University of Leicester said the participants "were losing a lot of weight, but they were preserving their muscle mass."

The programme was not easy, but participants stuck with it: they attended on average 17 of 20 supervised exercise sessions. This is a key point, because preserving muscle during rapid weight loss does not happen by itself — it needs regular resistance exercise and enough protein, both of which the programme provided under specialist supervision.

The most important number: diabetes in remission in more than half

Remission in this trial has a strict, internationally agreed definition: glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) — a measure of average blood sugar over about three months — falling below 6.5%, the threshold for diagnosing diabetes, while stopping blood-sugar-lowering medicines for at least 12 weeks. By that definition, 27 of 50 people in the programme group reached remission — 54% — compared with only 2 of 46 in the usual-care group, about 4%.

The difference is large, but it needs to be read carefully: the adjusted odds ratio was 21.6, meaning the chance of remission in the programme group was far higher, but the confidence interval was very wide (6.2 to 114.5), which means the estimate is imprecise because the trial was small. The practical message is not that "diabetes is cured", but that remission is possible and scientifically documented in young people with early diabetes and obesity, when they combine a very-low-calorie diet with supervised exercise under medical supervision.

Read the results carefully: what the trial did not prove

This is where a good article differs from an exaggerated one. First, follow-up stopped at 24 weeks — just six months — so nobody knows yet whether remission lasts a year or five years, and remission is not a cure: regaining weight is common after weight-loss programmes, and blood sugar may rise again. Second, the trial did not include a group following the diet alone without exercise, so it cannot prove how much of the muscle preservation was due to exercise alone — and the researchers themselves say so openly.

Third, the trial was small and open-label: participants knew which group they were in, although those who measured the main outcome did not. Most programme participants reported at least one side effect — usually constipation, headache, dizziness or temporary fatigue — with no serious events. Finally, the results apply only to a specific group: young adults aged 18 to 45, with early diabetes, obesity, and not using insulin — they do not extend to older people, people who have had diabetes for many years, or people using insulin.

Tip

Any very-low-calorie diet must be followed with medical supervision and accompanied by exercise — never stop your diabetes medicine on your own, and never start an 800-calorie diet by yourself. But know that remission is possible and scientifically documented for many young people, and that protecting your muscles while losing weight starts with two simple steps: resistance exercise twice a week, and enough protein in your meals. Discuss a realistic weight goal and an exercise plan that suits you with your doctor, and start today — the muscle you preserve today is what protects your blood sugar tomorrow.

The bottom line: remission is possible, and muscle is worth protecting

The RESET for Remission trial adds an important result to the science: combining a very-low-calorie diet with supervised exercise put more than half of young adults with early-onset type 2 diabetes into remission after six months, while preserving muscle — something no previous trial had shown in this way. But the result remains limited to six months, to a specific group, and to a small trial that did not compare diet alone with exercise. The promise is real, but the road to lasting remission still needs longer follow-up and larger trials. Today, though, the practical message does not change: losing weight helps diabetes, but losing it without exercise costs you muscle — so make resistance training part of any plan.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the trial

  • RESET for Remission trial, The Lancet Regional Health – Americas, October 2026: 96 participants (aged 18–45) with early-onset type 2 diabetes and obesity, not on insulin; 50 in the programme, 46 in usual care.
  • The programme: 800–900 calories a day for 12 weeks (meal replacements in the first two weeks), with supervised exercise twice a week and once alone — aerobic and resistance — then 12 weeks to maintain the weight.
  • Weight loss after 24 weeks: 8.4 kg on average in the programme group versus 1.2 kg in usual care; fat decreased and muscle was preserved.
  • Diabetes remission (HbA1c below 6.5% with no medicines for at least 12 weeks): 27 of 50 (54%) versus 2 of 46 (about 4%); adjusted odds ratio 21.6 (confidence interval 6.2–114.5).
  • Adherence: 17 of 20 supervised exercise sessions attended on average; most participants reported at least one side effect (constipation, headache, dizziness or fatigue), with no serious events.
  • Limits: only 24 weeks of follow-up; no diet-only group; small open-label trial; results apply only to young people with early diabetes and obesity not using insulin.
