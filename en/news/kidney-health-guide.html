Kidney Health: The Complete Guide

Share this article:

What are the kidneys and why are they so important?

The kidneys are two small bean-shaped organs, located on either side of the spine in the lower back. Despite their small size, they do enormous work: they filter the blood of waste and excess fluid, removing it in urine, regulate blood pressure and the balance of salts and water in the body, and produce hormones that help make red blood cells and keep bones healthy. When the kidneys gradually weaken, waste builds up in the blood, the body's balance is disturbed, and other organs such as the heart and bones are affected. The most dangerous thing about kidney disease is that it develops silently for years, and many people do not know they have it until it reaches an advanced stage. In this guide we explain how the kidneys work, what chronic kidney disease is, its causes and symptoms, and how to protect your kidneys in daily life.

What is chronic kidney disease?

Chronic kidney disease is a gradual weakening of kidney function lasting three months or more. It is usually measured by the glomerular filtration rate, a number expressing how much blood the kidneys filter per minute, and by a urine test looking for protein in the urine. When large amounts of protein leak into the urine, this is a sign of kidney damage. Doctors divide the disease into stages according to the severity of the weakness, from the early stage, which may cause no symptoms, to the advanced stage, which may need dialysis or a kidney transplant. The aim of early diagnosis is to slow the progression of the disease, protect the remaining kidney function, and reduce the risk of heart disease, because people with chronic kidney disease have a higher heart risk than others. That is why checking the kidneys regularly is very important for people with risk factors, even if they feel well.

What are the causes and risk factors?

The two most important causes of chronic kidney disease in the world are diabetes and high blood pressure, because they slowly damage the tiny vessels in the kidneys over years. Other causes include inflammation of the kidneys, inherited polycystic kidney disease, blockage of the urinary tract by stones or an enlarged prostate, repeated urinary infections, and some medicines that harm the kidneys when used for long periods or in large doses, such as some painkillers. Risk factors also include advancing age, a family history of kidney disease, smoking, obesity, and heart disease. In Morocco, the risk rises with the spread of diabetes and high blood pressure, and with some people using painkillers and herbs without medical supervision for long periods. The important message is that controlling diabetes and blood pressure is the best protection for the kidneys, and much kidney damage can be prevented or slowed if it is detected early.

What are the symptoms and warning signs?

In the early stages, chronic kidney disease usually causes no symptoms, which is why a doctor often discovers it through a routine blood or urine test. As the disease advances, symptoms may appear, such as persistent tiredness, swelling of the feet, ankles, and face, especially in the morning, passing little urine or urinating often at night, foamy or bloody urine, dry and itchy skin, muscle cramps, loss of appetite and nausea, a metallic taste in the mouth, shortness of breath, and difficulty concentrating. These symptoms are not specific to the kidneys alone and can have other causes, but their appearance, especially swelling, bloody urine, or shortness of breath, calls for seeing a doctor without delay. Do not wait for symptoms if you have diabetes or high blood pressure; ask your doctor to check your kidney function regularly, because that is the test that detects the disease early.

How can you protect your kidneys?

The best protection for the kidneys begins with controlling the diseases that harm them: if you have diabetes, follow your treatment and keep your blood sugar within the target your doctor set; if you have high blood pressure, take your medicine and reduce salt. Eat a balanced diet: reduce salt, pickles, and processed foods, cut down on large amounts of animal protein, and ask your doctor or a dietitian about the amount that suits you, because needs differ according to the stage of the disease. Drink water regularly, especially in Morocco's hot weather, and avoid dehydration, but do not overdo drinking water without a reason, and ask your doctor about the right amount for you if you have kidney disease. Exercise regularly, keep a healthy weight, and quit smoking. Do not take painkillers daily without consulting a doctor, and do not take herbs or supplements without asking, because some harm the kidneys. Tell your doctor about all the medicines you take before any imaging test that uses contrast dye, because some dyes may harm the kidneys.

Tests, treatment, and follow-up

The doctor usually checks the kidneys with a blood test measuring creatinine and calculating the filtration rate, and a urine test looking for protein, and sometimes an ultrasound scan. The test is repeated regularly to follow the progression of the disease. Treatment depends on the cause and stage: it includes controlling diabetes and blood pressure, medicines that protect the kidneys and reduce protein in the urine, treatment for anaemia and bone disorders when needed, and a suitable diet set by the doctor and dietitian. In advanced stages, a patient may need dialysis or a kidney transplant, both life-saving treatments, but prevention and early treatment are far better than reaching this stage. Keep your follow-up appointments, bring your medicine list and test results to every visit, and ask your doctor about anything that worries you. Do not turn to alternative treatments that promise to cure the kidneys, because there is no strong evidence for them, and some may speed up kidney damage.

Misconceptions about kidney health

One misconception is that back pain always means a kidney problem; the truth is that most back pain is muscular or bony, and chronic kidney disease usually does not cause back pain. Another is that drinking huge amounts of water cleans the kidneys and treats their disease; the truth is that water is important to avoid dehydration, but excess water does not treat kidney damage and may be harmful in advanced stages. Another is that kidney disease affects only older people; the truth is that it affects young people too, especially with diabetes and high blood pressure. Another is that dialysis means the end of a normal life; the truth is that many dialysis patients live active lives with treatment and follow-up, but it is better to prevent reaching this stage. Another is that herbs are safe for the kidneys because they are natural; the truth is that some herbs harm the kidneys severely.

Tip

Drink water regularly during the day, reduce salt, do not take painkillers daily without consulting a doctor, and have your kidney function checked if you have diabetes or high blood pressure.

The bottom line: your kidneys are small and their work is great

Your kidneys work silently day and night to clean your blood, regulate your pressure, and balance your body, and they deserve your daily care. Control your blood sugar and blood pressure, reduce salt, drink water regularly, move more, quit smoking, and do not take medicines or herbs without consulting your doctor. If you have diabetes or high blood pressure, or a family history of kidney disease, ask for regular kidney function checks, because early detection is the best protection. Healthy kidneys mean a longer, better life.
