Paxlovid and long COVID: can the drug cut the risk of lingering symptoms? What the largest real-world study found

Share this article:

Long COVID remains a puzzle with no cure

More than four years after the start of the pandemic, long COVID still troubles millions of people around the world: persistent fatigue, shortness of breath, problems with memory and concentration, disturbed sleep, joint pain, and changes in smell and taste, lasting weeks and months after the acute infection has passed. To this day there is no approved medicine that treats long COVID itself — all doctors can do is treat the symptoms and support the patient.

But one question has lingered since the first antiviral treatments appeared: could treating the acute infection early prevent the long symptoms in the first place? A large new study, published on 28 October 2026 in PLOS Global Public Health and covered in the news on 24 November 2026, gives the clearest answer yet about the drug Paxlovid.

The largest real-world study to date

The study was carried out by researchers at Weill Cornell Medicine, led by Dr Zang, as part of the RECOVER Initiative of the US National Institutes of Health. It was not a randomised clinical trial, but a target trial emulation study based on electronic health records from 29 repositories in the PCORnet network.

It included 497,499 outpatients with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, between 1 March 2022 and 28 February 2023. Of these, 159,659 high-risk patients took Paxlovid (nirmatrelvir/ritonavir) within 5 days of infection, while the others did not. Long COVID was defined in this study as new diagnoses appearing 30 to 180 days after infection.

In high-risk patients: a 12% lower risk

The most important finding concerned high-risk patients — those with at least one risk factor for severe COVID-19. Taking Paxlovid was associated with a 12% lower risk of long COVID, a hazard ratio of 0.88, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.87 to 0.89. The absolute risk reduction was 2.99 events per 100 persons — about 30 fewer cases per 1,000 people who took the drug.

The benefit did not stop at long COVID: patients who took Paxlovid had a 30% lower rate of hospitalisation and a 47% lower rate of death from any cause in the 30 to 180 days after infection, compared with those who received no treatment. These figures are a reminder that the drug is already approved for high-risk patients to prevent severe acute COVID-19, and that its benefit may extend beyond the acute phase.

Fewer symptoms too: memory, sleep, fatigue and breathing

Alongside the overall reduction in long COVID risk, patients who took Paxlovid were also less likely to develop a range of specific symptoms: cognitive issues, sleep problems, joint pain, fatigue, shortness of breath, and changes in smell and taste. In other words, the benefit was not limited to a single diagnosis — it covered the very symptoms that long COVID patients complain of most.

This consistency across different symptoms strengthens the credibility of the finding, but it does not turn it into conclusive proof, as we shall see.

What about low-risk patients? And what did the study not prove?

In low-risk patients — those with no documented risk factors for severe COVID-19 — the findings were inconclusive: a hazard ratio of 1.03, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.95 to 1.11, a range that includes the value 1, so it cannot be said that the drug helped or harmed them. This means the observed benefit concerns high-risk patients — the very group the drug is already prescribed for.

Caution is still warranted when interpreting the results. The study is observational, not a randomised trial: patients were not randomly assigned to the drug or to no treatment, so other factors may explain part of the findings — people who seek the drug early may be more careful about their health, or closer to medical care. The study also tracked only new diagnoses, and did not examine the worsening of conditions that already existed before infection.

So although this is the largest observational study on this question to date, it remains evidence of an association, not of causation, and on its own it cannot settle whether Paxlovid prevents long COVID.

Tip

Paxlovid is not a treatment for long COVID, but a preventive medicine for high-risk patients that must be taken within 5 days of infection. If you are in a high-risk group and catch COVID-19, contact your doctor immediately to discuss whether to take it, and do not delay seeking medical care.

The bottom line: a possible benefit for high-risk patients, not a cure for long COVID

The bottom line is that the largest real-world study to date found that Paxlovid, taken within 5 days of infection, was associated with a 12% lower risk of long COVID in high-risk patients — about 30 fewer cases per 1,000 people — along with a 30% lower rate of hospitalisation and a 47% lower rate of death in the following months. But the drug is not a treatment for long COVID, and it is no reason to abandon prevention, and its benefit has not been shown in low-risk patients. If you are in a high-risk group and catch COVID-19, the right move is to contact your doctor immediately — the drug only helps within the first five days.

  • Size: 497,499 SARS-CoV-2-positive outpatients, 1 March 2022 to 28 February 2023, from electronic health records in 29 repositories in the PCORnet network.
  • Treatment: Paxlovid (nirmatrelvir/ritonavir) within 5 days of infection; 159,659 high-risk patients took it.

  • Fewer symptoms: cognitive issues, sleep problems, joint pain, fatigue, shortness of breath, and changes in smell and taste.

  • Long COVID here means new diagnoses in the 30–180 days after infection; limitations: an observational study that does not prove causation, tracking only new diagnoses and not the worsening of pre-existing conditions.
