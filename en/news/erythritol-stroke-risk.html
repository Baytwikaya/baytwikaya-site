The sugar substitute erythritol and stroke risk: what a new genetic study found

Share this article:

A sweet spoonful with no sugar… so why are heart doctors talking about it?

Many people stir it into coffee and tea instead of sugar, and find it in “sugar-free” products, keto sweets and diet drinks. It is erythritol, a sugar substitute whose taste almost matches table sugar, yet gives the body almost no calories and does not raise blood sugar sharply. That is why millions of people chose it — especially people with diabetes and those trying to lose weight — as a safe option.

But a new study from China reopens the file: researchers found that people who carry genes that make erythritol levels higher in their blood face a higher risk of ischaemic stroke — the stroke caused by a blocked blood vessel feeding the brain. So what did the study actually find? And does it mean that sweetening your morning coffee puts you at risk? The answer is more precise than a breaking-news headline, and it is worth reading in full.

What is erythritol? And where is it found?

Erythritol is a sugar alcohol found naturally in small amounts in some fruits, such as pears and grapes, and the human body itself makes small quantities of it from glucose. It is about 70% as sweet as table sugar, while providing only about 0.24 calories per gram, compared with about 4 calories per gram of sugar. Since the 1990s it has become a popular low-calorie sweetener, used in chewing gum, sweets, drinks and “sugar-free” products.

One essential point must be understood before following the study: the level of erythritol in the blood does not come from food alone. The body produces it internally, the kidneys handle it, and metabolism affects it. That is why interpreting the new study calls for caution, as we shall see.

What did the Chinese researchers do?

The study was led by endocrinologist Ao Zhong of Huazhong University of Science and Technology in China, with colleagues, and was published in the International Journal of Molecular Sciences on 31 August 2026. The team used a method called Mendelian randomisation, which goes beyond merely observing statistical associations: instead of tracking what people eat, the researchers used natural genetic differences that make some people carry higher erythritol levels in their blood, then compared stroke risk between them and others. Because genes are inherited randomly at birth, this is like a natural experiment that lifestyle habits alone are unlikely to distort.

Nor did the team stop there: they ran two-sample and multivariable Mendelian randomisation across cardiovascular, kidney and metabolic outcomes, then followed with computational analyses of genes, biological pathways and machine learning, looking for the mechanism that might link erythritol to stroke. Among the sweetener-related traits they tested, erythritol showed the strongest association with stroke.

The key figure: risk higher by about a quarter

The researchers found that a genetic predisposition to higher blood erythritol was linked to about 25% higher odds of ischaemic stroke: an odds ratio of 1.246, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.101–1.410 and a p-value below 0.001. The association remained after the analysis was adjusted for factors such as blood pressure, blood sugar and lipids, meaning it did not disappear once those factors were taken into account.

But the figure needs correct translation. A 25% increase is a relative increase in the odds, not a 25-percentage-point increase in absolute risk. In other words, a person whose stroke risk was, say, 4% would see it become about 5% — a real increase, but not one person in four being affected. This distinction between relative and absolute risk is the first thing a reader should understand before worrying or feeling reassured.

How could erythritol harm blood vessels?

In additional computational analyses, the researchers identified four possible biological pathways linking erythritol to stroke: inflammation, oxidative stress, hypoxia and vascular injury. Three genes also kept appearing in their analyses — MMP9, TLR4 and HIF1A — as factors that may play an important role in stroke risk. These genes are known for their links to inflammation, the remodelling of blood-vessel walls and the response to low oxygen.

Yet the researchers themselves describe these findings as exploratory, and as not proving erythritol-specific molecular regulation. They are a first map of possible pathways that deserve further research, not proof of a confirmed mechanism. Zhong wrote that the long-term effects of these compounds on cerebral blood vessels and inflammation remain insufficiently defined, despite their promotion as a means of controlling weight and blood sugar — which is concerning, because the people who consume sugar substitutes most already suffer, at high rates, from cardiovascular problems.

This is not the first study

The new Chinese study does not come out of nowhere; it joins a chain of evidence that has been building since 2023. That year, a Cleveland Clinic team led by Marco Witkowski and Stanley Hazen, in a study published in Nature Medicine, found that people with the highest blood erythritol levels — the top quartile versus the bottom quartile — faced about double the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events, such as heart attack or stroke, over three years. The study included a discovery group of 1,157 people undergoing cardiac risk assessment, and the association was then confirmed in two independent groups: a US group of 2,149 people (hazard ratio 1.80) and a European group of 833 people (hazard ratio 2.21).

In laboratory experiments in the same study, erythritol at physiological levels increased platelet reactivity and clot formation. And in a small intervention pilot in 8 healthy volunteers, a single 30-gram dose of erythritol raised plasma levels about 1,000-fold, and they stayed elevated for more than two days, above the threshold linked to increased platelet reactivity. Then, in 2025, laboratory experiments at the University of Colorado showed that erythritol may damage the cells of the blood–brain barrier, and a European genetic study of 8,167 people found that genes linked to higher erythritol levels were associated with about 16% higher stroke risk and about 8% higher coronary heart disease risk. The picture is therefore accumulating — but every piece of it has its limits.

Read the result carefully: what does the study not prove?

Here comes the most important point in the whole article, and it is one the study authors stressed themselves. The findings concern erythritol present in the blood, and do not directly prove that consuming it in the diet causes stroke, because its blood levels may also reflect production inside the body, metabolic processes and how the kidneys handle it. They wrote plainly that their results “should not be interpreted as direct evidence that dietary erythritol intake causes stroke”.

They added, however, that eating erythritol can raise its concentrations in the blood, and so the association they observed represents “a possible warning signal regarding its safety”. The study is therefore neither an acquittal nor a conviction: it is a signal calling for more research, not a final verdict. The good news is that direct evidence is on its way: a clinical trial is now running at the University of California, Davis, in which participants drink beverages sweetened with erythritol or aspartame for two weeks each, in turn, while researchers measure platelet activation, platelet adhesion and markers of vascular inflammation. That trial — not genetic studies alone — is what will answer the direct question: what does eating erythritol actually do in the human body?

Tip

There is no need to panic, and no need to throw everything out of your cupboard because of a single study — and do not make a medical decision based on a news headline. If you use erythritol every day in large amounts, especially if you have diabetes or high blood pressure, or have already had a heart or blood-vessel problem, discuss it with your doctor at your next visit and ask what alternative suits you best. In general, moderation is the golden rule: reduce sweetness gradually until your taste buds get used to a less sweet taste, read labels — erythritol hides in many “sugar-free” products — and prefer water and unsweetened drinks. And do not stop any medicine prescribed to you, and do not replace it on your own: the prescribed medicine protects you today, while erythritol is still under investigation.

The bottom line: a warning signal, not a final verdict

The 2026 genetic study adds an important piece to a picture that began forming in 2023: a genetic predisposition to higher blood erythritol was linked to about 25% higher odds of ischaemic stroke, erythritol was the sweetener most strongly linked to stroke among those tested, and the possible pathways — inflammation, oxidative stress and vascular injury — look biologically plausible. But the study did not track what people eat, and did not prove that eating erythritol causes stroke, and the body makes erythritol too. The practical message today is therefore neither a ban nor indifference, but moderation and caution: reduce your reliance on artificial sweetness, talk to your doctor if you are among those at risk, and wait for direct evidence from the ongoing clinical trials before any final judgement.
