Frozen shoulder: the complete guide to causes, symptoms and treatment

Share this article:

What is frozen shoulder?

Frozen shoulder, also called adhesive capsulitis, is a condition in which the shoulder joint gradually becomes painful and stiff, until raising the arm, rotating it or reaching behind the back becomes difficult. It happens because the capsule, the tissue that surrounds and stabilises the shoulder joint, becomes thick, tight and inflamed, so the space available for movement inside the joint shrinks. The condition usually begins with mild pain that grows over time, then stiffness limits movement, and then it slowly improves over many months. Frozen shoulder most often affects one shoulder, though some people later develop it in the other shoulder. It is most common in people between the ages of forty and sixty, and it affects women more than men. Although the pain and stiffness can be severe and disruptive in daily life, most cases improve gradually with time and appropriate treatment, without the need for surgery.

In this guide we explain what happens inside the shoulder, why the condition occurs, its three known stages, how a doctor diagnoses it, which treatments have proven useful, the exercises and daily habits that help restore movement, and the mistakes to avoid.

Why does the shoulder freeze? Causes and risk factors

In many cases there is no single clear cause, and the condition is then called primary or idiopathic. Doctors know, however, that inflammation inside the shoulder capsule is the main driver: the capsule tissues thicken and stick together, and adhesions form that prevent the joint from gliding freely. Some factors increase the risk, including diabetes: medical sources note that people with diabetes are more likely to develop frozen shoulder, and their condition may be more severe and longer-lasting. Thyroid disease, heart and blood-vessel disease, and Parkinson's disease also raise the likelihood. Another important factor is keeping the shoulder still for a long time, as happens after an arm fracture, surgery or a stroke, because lack of movement allows the capsule to shrink and stick. Doctors therefore advise moving the shoulder gently as soon as possible after any injury or surgery, following the treating doctor's instructions. In Morocco, as elsewhere, many people delay seeking care because they assume the pain is a passing problem, so stiffness increases over time and treatment becomes longer.

Symptoms and the three stages

Frozen shoulder usually passes through three stages that overlap. The first is the freezing stage, in which pain is the dominant symptom: pain that grows gradually, worsens at night and with any shoulder movement, and may spread towards the upper arm. This stage usually lasts several months. Then comes the frozen stage, in which pain eases somewhat but stiffness reaches its peak: raising the arm above the head, reaching into a back pocket or combing the hair becomes difficult or impossible. This stage also lasts for months. Finally comes the thawing stage, in which range of movement gradually begins to improve, slowly, until most or all of it returns. The whole condition, from start to finish, can take a year or more, and that is its natural course, not a sign that treatment has failed. It is important to know that severe night pain that wakes you from sleep is common in the early stages, and that weakness in the arm may appear because of disuse rather than damage to the muscles themselves.

How does a doctor diagnose it?

Diagnosis rests mainly on the clinical examination: the doctor asks about when the pain started and what it feels like, then examines the shoulder's range of movement, asking you to move your arm in different directions and then moving your arm for you. In frozen shoulder, range of movement is limited in both active and passive movement, and this is what distinguishes it from other problems such as a rotator-cuff tear, in which passive movement remains possible. The doctor may order X-rays to rule out other causes such as arthritis or fractures, or an MRI in some cases to examine the tendons and soft tissues. There is no blood test that diagnoses frozen shoulder, but the doctor may order tests for diabetes or thyroid disease if either is suspected. See a doctor if shoulder pain lasts more than a few weeks, if the pain is severe at night, if movement is clearly shrinking, or if the pain interferes with sleep, work and daily activities.

Treatment: what genuinely helps?

Treatment aims to relieve pain and restore movement, and it depends on the stage of the condition. In the painful stage, painkillers and anti-inflammatory medicines prescribed by a doctor or pharmacist help, along with warm or cold compresses according to what brings relief. The doctor may suggest a corticosteroid injection into the joint, which reduces pain and inflammation for many patients, especially in the early stages, but its effect is temporary and it must be combined with exercises. Physiotherapy is the cornerstone at every stage: gentle, gradual stretching exercises that aim to lengthen the tight capsule and restore range of movement, explained by a physiotherapist who follows your progress. In cases that do not improve with conservative treatment after many months, the doctor may suggest moving the shoulder under anaesthesia, hydrodilatation of the capsule under imaging guidance, or arthroscopy to remove adhesions; these are options the doctor discusses with the patient, weighing benefits and risks. It is important for the patient to know that improvement is slow by the nature of the condition, and that regularity in exercising matters more than intensity. Good control of diabetes, where present, is also part of treatment, because high blood sugar may prolong the condition.

Exercises and daily habits that ease the pain

Simple daily movements help preserve the movement that remains and prevent stiffness from worsening. One of the best known is the pendulum exercise: lean slightly forward, let the affected arm hang down, then move it in small, slow circles in both directions. The towel stretch: hold a towel with your healthy hand behind your back from above and with the affected hand from below, then pull gently upwards until you feel a bearable stretch without sharp pain. And the finger walk on the wall: stand facing a wall and walk your fingers up as high as you can, then come down slowly. Repeat these exercises daily, slowly, and stop if the pain suddenly becomes severe. In daily life, avoid carrying heavy weights with the affected arm, sleep on the healthy side or on your back with a pillow supporting the arm, and spread tasks through the day instead of straining the shoulder all at once. If you work at a computer, adjust your sitting position so you do not have to raise your shoulder or reach far with your arm. Consult your doctor or physiotherapist before starting any exercise programme, especially if you have another shoulder injury.

Tip

Keep your shoulder moving gently every day, even during the painful stage: small, slow movements are far better than stopping movement altogether, which makes stiffness worse.

What does not help? Common mistakes

A common mistake is to stop moving the shoulder completely out of fear of pain; this increases stiffness and prolongs the condition. The opposite mistake is forceful exercise or heavy lifting to try to break the stiffness, which increases inflammation and pain and may injure the tissues. Relying on painkillers alone, without exercises, does not restore movement either. Be cautious of unproven treatments promoted as curing frozen shoulder quickly: there is no magic cure that shortens the course of a condition that takes months. As for cupping and massage, some patients find temporary relief from pain and muscle tension around the shoulder, but the scientific evidence for their benefit in restoring range of movement in frozen shoulder is limited, and they do not replace physiotherapy and the exercises that remain the foundation. If someone offers you a treatment that promises instant healing, remember that the condition improves gradually by its nature, and any sudden rapid improvement is most often part of the natural course or a temporary effect of pain relief.

The bottom line: frozen shoulder is painful but it can improve

Frozen shoulder is a slow and troublesome condition, but in most cases it improves with time and the right treatment. Start early: see a doctor if the pain persists or movement starts to shrink, keep up gentle stretching exercises every day, and be patient, because regaining movement is measured in months, not days. If you have diabetes, controlling it is an important part of treatment. With medical follow-up and regular physiotherapy, most patients regain their shoulder movement and return to their daily activities.
