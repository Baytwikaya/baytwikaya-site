A twice-yearly cholesterol shot that cuts heart attacks and strokes? What the ORION-4 trial proved in 2026

Share this article:

A twice-yearly shot… do heart attacks really fall?

Many people with high cholesterol swallow a statin tablet every morning, yet the risk of a heart attack or a stroke remains, because LDL cholesterol does not fall far enough, or because sticking to a daily pill slips over the years. What if prevention were a single injection every six months instead of a pill every day?

That is what a large trial led by the University of Oxford tested, and Novartis announced its topline results on 23 November 2026: inclisiran, given twice a year, reduced the risk of major cardiovascular events in more than 16,000 patients who already had cardiovascular disease. But before any excitement, there is one number that has not been announced yet — and it is the most important number in the whole story.

What is inclisiran, and how does it work?

Inclisiran, sold under the brand name Leqvio, is not a statin and not a daily pill. It is a medicine based on a newer technology called small interfering RNA (siRNA). It switches off the production of a protein called PCSK9 in the liver, a protein that normally destroys the receptors that pull LDL (“bad”) cholesterol out of the blood. With less of the protein, more receptors stay at work, and the liver clears LDL cholesterol more effectively.

The injection is given under the skin at a dose of 300 mg on a fixed schedule: a first dose, a second dose after three months, then one injection every six months, administered by a healthcare professional in a clinic. The drug has been approved since December 2021 in the United States and in more than 100 countries to lower LDL cholesterol — but its current licence does not cover preventing heart attacks and strokes. That is exactly what the ORION-4 trial is testing. Earlier research showed that inclisiran lowers LDL cholesterol by around 50% over 18 months.

What did the ORION-4 trial actually prove?

ORION-4, also known as HPS-4/TIMI 65, is a phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial led by the University of Oxford in collaboration with the TIMI Study Group, and co-sponsored by Oxford and Novartis. It enrolled 16,124 participants in the United Kingdom and the United States, all with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease — men aged 40 and over, and women aged 55 and over — and the drug or placebo was added to their standard lipid-lowering treatment, with a planned median follow-up of about five years.

The primary endpoint was met: inclisiran, added to standard care, reduced the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events — death from coronary heart disease, non-fatal heart attack, fatal or non-fatal ischaemic stroke, and urgent coronary revascularization. The company described the reduction as statistically significant and clinically meaningful, and the trial’s lead investigator, Louise Bowman, Professor of Medicine and Clinical Trials at Oxford, said the trial provides evidence that the drug is well tolerated and can reduce the risk of major cardiovascular events in people with established vascular disease.

The historic significance is that this is the first time an siRNA drug has been shown not only to lower the cholesterol number on a lab report, but actually to reduce heart attacks and strokes in patients.

The number that has not been announced — and why it matters more than the headline

This is where we must pause. Novartis has not disclosed the size of the reduction: no relative risk reduction, no absolute risk reduction, no event counts in each group, no p-value, and no results for any single component of the primary endpoint. The phrase “statistically significant and clinically meaningful” is a description, not a number: we do not know whether the reduction was 5%, 15% or 25%, nor how many patients must be treated to prevent one event.

The full results have not been published and have not been peer reviewed, and publication is expected in the first half of 2027. That means today’s news is a proof of principle — that lowering cholesterol with this technology does translate into fewer events — not a ready prescription, and not a number a doctor can promise a patient.

Who is this drug for? And who is it not for?

The first thing to make clear: the trial enrolled patients who already had established cardiovascular disease — secondary prevention after a disease is present, not primary prevention in healthy people. The drug’s ability to prevent a first event in a healthy person with high cholesterol alone has not been tested, and we do not know whether it would help in that situation.

Second: the drug was added to standard lipid-lowering treatment, led by statins — not given instead of it. The news does not mean a patient can stop a statin or swap it for the injection, nor that the injection replaces healthy food, movement, quitting smoking, and controlling blood pressure and blood sugar. A twice-yearly schedule may help people who forget a daily pill, but it is a clinic injection, not a pill at home, its price and availability vary from country to country, and it has not been announced whether health authorities will extend the drug’s licence to cover event prevention.

What about safety?

The lead investigator said the drug was well tolerated, but no detailed safety figures have been published: no adverse event rates, no injection-site reaction data, and no discontinuation figures. The full details will come with the scientific publication in 2027. A second large trial is still running, VICTORION-2 PREVENT, with results also expected in 2027; together the two trials form a programme of more than 33,000 participants, one of the largest advanced lipid-lowering secondary prevention programmes.

Tip

If you take a statin or have heart disease, do not stop your medicine and do not ask for this injection yourself — the news concerns patients with established heart disease under medical supervision, and the drug today is an add-on treatment, not a replacement. The right step: have your LDL cholesterol checked regularly, stick to your prescribed treatment, cut back on saturated fat and salt, walk every day, quit smoking, and ask your doctor whether any additional treatment suits your case. And if you take any medicine, do not stop it or replace it on your own — consult your doctor first.

The bottom line: an important first step, not a final number yet

The ORION-4 trial is the first proof that an siRNA drug does not only lower cholesterol on a lab report, but actually reduces heart attacks and strokes in people with heart disease. Yet the size of the benefit is still unknown, the full results are awaiting publication, and the drug today is an add-on to statins, not a replacement for them. The message that does not change: high LDL cholesterol is a silent risk, and checking it and treating it early — with medicine and lifestyle together — is what prevents events before they happen.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the results

  • The trial: ORION-4 (also known as HPS-4/TIMI 65), phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; led by the University of Oxford with the TIMI Study Group, and co-sponsored by Oxford and Novartis.
  • Participants: 16,124 patients with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, in the United Kingdom and the United States; men aged 40 and over, women aged 55 and over.
  • Planned follow-up: about 5 years (the median follow-up actually achieved has not been reported).
  • Primary endpoint: a statistically significant and clinically meaningful reduction in major cardiovascular events (coronary heart disease death, heart attack, ischaemic stroke, urgent coronary revascularization) — but the size of the reduction, relative and absolute, has not been disclosed.
  • Dosing: 300 mg under the skin; a first dose, then after 3 months, then every 6 months; added to standard lipid-lowering treatment.
  • LDL cholesterol reduction in earlier research: around 50% over 18 months.
  • The drug: inclisiran (Leqvio), the first siRNA therapy to lower LDL cholesterol by targeting the PCSK9 protein; approved since December 2021 in more than 100 countries to lower cholesterol, not yet for preventing events.
  • Full results: expected in the first half of 2027, not yet peer reviewed; the VICTORION-2 PREVENT trial is ongoing, with more than 33,000 participants across the two trials.
