Hepatitis A in Morocco: cases surge after the pandemic and cluster in two regions

Share this article:

Hepatitis A returns to the spotlight in Morocco

After years of decline during the pandemic, hepatitis A is rising sharply again in Morocco, according to a new Moroccan scientific study that tracked laboratory-confirmed cases over eight full years. The figures leave little doubt: cases in 2024 and 2025 alone exceeded the total of the previous six years combined.

The study, led by Moroccan researchers from universities and research and health institutions and accepted for publication in BMC Infectious Diseases, does more than count cases: it maps precisely where they are concentrated and reveals an immunity gap among children and adolescents that may explain this comeback. So what exactly did it find?

The numbers: from a sharp fall to a big surge

Over 416 weeks of national surveillance, between January 2018 and December 2025, 3,051 laboratory-confirmed cases of hepatitis A were recorded. The distribution across the years tells the whole story: 969 cases in 2018–2019, then only 183 in 2020–2021 and 199 in 2022–2023, before cases jumped to 1,700 in 2024–2025.

Compared with pre-pandemic levels, the incidence rate fell by 82.4% during the pandemic and remained 81.7% lower in the early post-pandemic phase, then rose by 65.9% in 2024–2025, exceeding pre-pandemic levels. The annual rate reached its lowest in 2021 at 0.17 cases per 100,000 people, then climbed to 1.71 in 2024 and 2.88 in 2025, the highest of the eight years.

The data also show the rise was not gradual: at the start of the pandemic in March 2020 there was an immediate drop of 90.6%, then the weekly average settled at around 1.4 cases between late 2019 and mid-2023, before jumping to an average of around 17.5 cases a week from June 2024. The researchers also noted a seasonal autumn rise between September and November, with monthly peaks in October and November 2024 and in January 2025.

Where are cases concentrated? A regional map

Cases were not spread evenly across the country. In 2022–2025, the Fes-Meknes region recorded 811 cases — 42.7% of the national total — with a standardised incidence index of 3.52 times the national level. Rabat-Sale-Kenitra came second with 405 cases (21.3%) and an index of 1.53 times the national level, so the two regions together accounted for about 64% of cases.

The highest relative rate, however, was recorded in Dakhla-Oued Ed-Dahab, where the standardised index reached 5.29 times the national level, with 59 cases in 2022–2025 after zero cases in the previous four years. At the other end, Marrakech-Safi recorded one of the lowest rates, with an index of just 0.17 of the national level.

Why did the virus come back so strongly? Less immunity among the young

One of the study's most important findings comes from a national antibody survey carried out in 2019: while 88% of the people surveyed carried antibodies against the virus, the share was only 61% among those aged 5–18, compared with 97% among those aged 19–40. In other words, many children and adolescents are reaching older ages without acquired natural immunity, at a time when the symptoms of the disease are clearer in adults.

This partly explains what happened during and after the pandemic: with prevention measures and distancing, transmission of the virus fell sharply, then returned strongly as normal life resumed, reaching groups that had not acquired natural immunity during the quiet years.

How does the virus spread? And what role does the vaccine play?

The hepatitis A virus spreads through the fecal-oral route: through contaminated water or food, poor hygiene, or direct contact with an infected person. Most cases recover spontaneously, though serious complications can appear in limited groups.

As for the vaccine, it is available in Morocco as part of complementary vaccinations in the private sector, but it is not part of the national routine childhood programme. The national strategic plan 2022–2026 recommends evaluating its introduction in early childhood. Experience in countries such as Argentina, Brazil and China shows that introducing the vaccine into routine childhood vaccination led to major drops in cases.

Advice

Preventing hepatitis A starts with hygiene: wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water after using the toilet and before preparing or eating food, drink safe water, and wash vegetables and fruit well before eating them. If you are travelling or in contact with confirmed cases, ask your doctor about the hepatitis A vaccine — it is available in the private sector — and talk to your doctor about who benefits most from it.

Conclusion: what do the researchers recommend?

The study's conclusion is clear: hepatitis A is rising again in Morocco after years of calm, concentrated mainly in two regions, with the highest relative rate in Dakhla-Oued Ed-Dahab and an immunity gap among children and adolescents that calls for attention. The researchers recommend stronger surveillance with finer detail on age, place and exposure, a new antibody survey, wastewater analysis, genetic typing of strains, safe water and sanitation, and evaluating the introduction of the vaccine into routine immunisation.
