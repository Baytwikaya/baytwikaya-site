Salt and Health: The Complete Guide

Share this article:

Salt: an old friend to handle with care

Salt is present in every Moroccan kitchen, in tagines, harira, bread and preserved olives, and it is essential for the body in small amounts, but excess salt is one of the most common nutritional problems in the world. Most of the salt we eat does not come from the salt shaker alone, but from bread, cheeses, processed meats, ready meals and pickles, hidden sources that are hard to estimate. The World Health Organization recommends reducing salt intake to less than five grams a day, an amount equal to about one teaspoon, but many people eat far more than that without noticing. In this guide we explain why the body needs salt, how excess affects blood pressure, the heart and the kidneys, where salt hides in our daily food, and how to reduce it gradually without losing the pleasure of eating. The goal is not to remove salt, but to return it to its proper place: a small amount that adds flavour, not a burden on health.

Why does the body need salt?

Salt consists mainly of sodium and chloride, and sodium is a mineral essential for the body: it helps balance fluids inside and outside cells, transmit nerve signals, contract muscles, and make the kidneys work. The body needs only a small amount of it, and gets it easily from natural food, even without adding salt to cooking. The problem is that the kidneys can get rid of excess sodium only up to a point, but when intake rises day after day, the volume of fluids in the body rises, blood pressure rises, and the burden on the heart and kidneys grows. Excess salt is also linked to a higher risk of heart disease and stroke, among the main causes of death in the world. It is important to understand that the effect of salt differs from one person to another: some people are more sensitive to salt than others, and older people and people with high blood pressure or kidney disease are more affected by it, so they should be more careful. This does not mean that salt is a poison to avoid completely, but that the amount is what makes the difference between what is necessary and what is harmful.

Salt and blood pressure: the relationship you should know

High blood pressure is a common and silent disease, meaning that it may cause no clear symptoms for years, yet it harms the arteries, heart, kidneys and brain silently. Among the main risk factors for high blood pressure are excess salt, excess weight, lack of movement, smoking, excess alcohol, chronic stress, and family history. Research suggests that reducing salt helps lower blood pressure, especially in people with high blood pressure, and that this reduction, even if modest for one person, makes a large difference for society as a whole. Reducing salt also benefits even people who do not have high blood pressure, because it reduces the burden on the kidneys and heart in the long term. If you have high blood pressure, reducing salt alone may not be enough, and you may need a medicine prescribed by a doctor, as well as weight loss, daily movement and stopping smoking. Do not stop blood pressure medicine on your own because you reduced salt; follow up with your doctor and regular measurements. Measuring blood pressure at the pharmacy or at home with a reliable device is a simple and important step, especially after the age of forty, because discovering a rise early makes it easier to treat.

Where does salt hide in our food?

Many people think they eat little salt because they do not add much to cooking, but the truth is that most salt comes from hidden sources. Bread, the basis of the Moroccan table, contains salt in every loaf, and eating several loaves a day adds a large amount without us noticing. Cheeses, especially processed and salted cheese, are rich in sodium. Preserved olives, the companion of tagines and salads, are very rich in salt, and eating a large handful adds a large amount. Processed meats such as sausages and cold meats, ready meals, ready sauces and packet soups are all rich in salt. Even sweets and some types of sweet bread contain salt. For this reason reducing salt in cooking alone is not enough; you must pay attention to these hidden sources: read the labels when you buy ready products, choose the types with less salt, reduce preserved olives to a small amount, wash olives with water before eating them to reduce the salt, reduce processed meats to occasions, and cook at home as much as possible, because you control the salt there.

How to reduce salt without losing flavour?

Reducing salt does not mean food without taste, but food with other, richer flavours. Lemon, a friend of Moroccan cooking, adds acidity that wakes up the taste and reduces the need for salt, especially with fish, salads and tagines. Fresh herbs such as coriander, parsley and mint, and spices such as cumin, turmeric, ginger, paprika and cinnamon, add strong flavour without salt. Garlic and onion give depth of taste, and natural vinegar adds a light acidity. The practical advice is to reduce salt gradually: if you add two spoons to the tagine, make it one and a half spoons for two weeks, then one spoon, and you will find that your taste adapts, and that food which once seemed saltless has become normal. Do not put the salt shaker on the table, because its presence pushes you to add without thinking. Taste the food before adding salt, because we often add it out of habit without tasting first. In restaurants, ask for food with less salt, and avoid adding salty ready sauces. With these steps, your salt intake falls without feeling deprived, and you regain your taste's sensitivity to natural flavours.

Iodised salt and sea salt: what is the difference?

A common question in Morocco is: is sea salt better than ordinary salt? And is iodised salt necessary? The answer is that salt, whatever its type, contains the same sodium, and the difference in other minerals is very small and does not change its effect on blood pressure. Sea salt, pink Himalayan salt and coarse salt are all salt, and excess in them harms like excess in ordinary salt, and there is no healthy version that allows excess. As for iodine, it is a mineral essential for the health of the thyroid gland, and its deficiency was a common problem in many regions, so iodine was added to table salt in many countries, including Morocco, as a public health programme. If you use salt that is not iodised, such as some types of sea salt, make sure that you get iodine from other sources such as fish, dairy products and eggs, or ask your doctor. Do not take iodine supplements without medical advice, because excess iodine also harms. The simple rule is: choose iodised salt from a trusted source, use it in a small amount, and do not rely on the type of salt as an excuse for excess.

A practical plan for a week with less salt

Try this plan for one week and watch the difference. First, remove the salt shaker from the table, and taste the food before adding any salt. Second, reduce the salt in cooking by a quarter, and replace it with lemon, herbs and spices. Third, reduce preserved olives to a small spoon per meal, and wash them with water before eating. Fourth, choose bread with less salt if available, reduce the number of loaves a day, and complete your fullness with vegetables. Fifth, avoid processed meats and ready meals this week, and cook at home. Sixth, read the label of two ready products you usually buy, compare the amount of sodium in them, and choose the lower one. Seventh, drink water regularly, and eat vegetables and fruit rich in potassium such as bananas, oranges, potatoes and lentils, because potassium helps balance the effect of sodium, but do not take potassium supplements without medical advice, especially if you have kidney disease or take blood pressure medicines. Finally, measure your blood pressure before the plan and after it if you can, record what you noticed, and share the result with your doctor at your next visit.

Tip

The bottom line: less salt, more flavour, and a healthier heart

Salt is essential in small amounts, but excess raises blood pressure and increases the burden on the heart and kidneys. Most salt comes from hidden sources such as bread, cheese, preserved olives and processed meats, not from the salt shaker alone. Reduce salt gradually, use lemon, herbs and spices for flavour, do not put the salt shaker on the table, and monitor your blood pressure regularly. If you have high blood pressure or kidney or heart disease, consult your doctor about the amount suitable for you, and do not stop your medicine without consulting them.
