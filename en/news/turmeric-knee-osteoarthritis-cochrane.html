Does turmeric ease knee osteoarthritis pain? A Cochrane review doubts the benefit

Share this article:

When a kitchen spice becomes a promise of healing

In many homes, turmeric sits on the spice shelf as a golden seasoning for food. But for millions of people it has moved beyond the kitchen into the medicine cabinet: capsules and powders sold to people with knee osteoarthritis, on the claim that curcumin, its anti-inflammatory compound, eases pain and improves movement. Does that promise stand up to the strongest tools of evidence-based medicine?

The newest answer comes from a Cochrane review published on 19 November 2026, one of the highest levels of scientific evidence: after gathering every trustworthy trial and analysing them together, the researchers concluded that turmeric may ease pain a little, or not at all, and that the available evidence is low-certainty. So what exactly did the review find?

What did the Cochrane review do?

An international team led by rheumatologist Ivan Shirinsky gathered 9 randomised controlled trials covering 1,245 people with knee osteoarthritis, whose average age ranged from 52 to 62 years, two-thirds of them women, in Australia, Belgium, India and Thailand. The trials compared oral turmeric extracts or curcuminoids with placebo (4 trials, 379 people), with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen and diclofenac (4 trials, 673 people), or with paracetamol (1 trial, 193 people). And the trials were short: between four weeks and three months only.

More important than the numbers is how the trials were screened: the researchers used a formal tool to check the trustworthiness of each trial, and set aside 10 trials that raised concerns, leaving them out of the analysis until their authors provide clarification. That is what sets this review apart from an earlier one in 2018, whose results were more optimistic, because it was built on evidence that had not passed this screening.

Pain: a small improvement that misses the clinically important threshold

On a pain scale from 0 to 10, pain fell by 1.57 points after three months with placebo, and by 2.29 points with turmeric — an extra difference of only 0.72 points in favour of turmeric (4 trials, 349 people). That difference is below the threshold the researchers consider clinically important, which is 1.5 points: in other words, the average patient may feel no noticeable difference between turmeric and a dummy pill.

And the problem is not only the small number, but how certain it is: the researchers rated the evidence as low-certainty, downgrading it because of publication bias and because the turmeric preparations differed between trials. Put simply, even this small improvement could change or disappear if larger, more rigorous trials are done.

Function and quality of life: a small difference too

The same picture repeats for function: on a scale from 0 to 100, function improved by 5.42 points with placebo and by 8.92 points with turmeric — an extra difference of only 3.5 points, and the confidence interval stretches from an improvement of 7.29 points to a worsening of 0.29 points, meaning the true result could be zero or slightly worse (4 trials, 348 people).

For quality of life the difference is smaller still: it improved by 9.83 points with placebo and by 12.02 points with turmeric, an extra difference of 2.19 points on a 100-point scale, and the confidence interval includes zero as well (3 trials, 291 people). By these numbers, turmeric does not change a patient's daily life in a noticeable way.

A striking paradox: more patients said the treatment worked

Here is a paradox worth pausing on: when patients themselves were asked whether the treatment was a success, 191 out of every 1,000 people taking turmeric said it worked, compared with 107 out of every 1,000 on placebo — 84 more people per thousand, a risk ratio of 1.78 (2 trials, only 170 people).

How can a treatment succeed in patients' eyes while pain improves only slightly? The most likely explanation is that a global assessment is shaped by expectation, hope and the placebo effect, and that the sample here is very small (170 people), with low-certainty evidence. So this result is a signal worth studying, not proof that overturns the main conclusion.

Compared with usual medicines: no clear advantage

The review did not show turmeric to be better than the usual medicines: compared with NSAIDs and paracetamol, turmeric was not shown to be more effective at relieving symptoms. That matters for anyone taking turmeric in the hope of giving up a prescribed medicine: there is no trustworthy evidence that the golden capsule can replace standard treatment.

That does not mean turmeric is of no use at all, only that the current evidence does not prove a clinically important benefit. The review did not say "turmeric does not work" — it said "we do not have trustworthy evidence that it works as well as claimed".

Safety: we do not know enough

On side effects, the picture is incomplete: 226 out of every 1,000 people taking turmeric reported side effects, compared with 224 out of every 1,000 on placebo — practically no difference. And 66 out of every 1,000 stopped turmeric because of side effects, compared with 46 out of every 1,000 on placebo. But the researchers say they are "very uncertain" about these numbers, because the evidence is very low-certainty and the trials were short and small. No serious side effects were recorded in three trials, and two were recorded in the placebo group in a fourth trial, unrelated to the study.

Why does this result differ from earlier, more optimistic studies?

Because earlier studies — including Cochrane's own 2018 review — included trials that had not passed the trustworthiness screening applied today, so 10 questionable trials were removed from the analysis. The trials also differed in their turmeric preparations (curcuminoid content ranging from 23% to 88%), and some added essential oils or absorption enhancers, so there is no single "turmeric dose" to judge. Finally, all the trials were short — three months at most — so we know nothing about benefit or safety in the long term, nor about hand or hip osteoarthritis, since every trial studied the knee only.

Advice

If you take turmeric supplements for knee osteoarthritis, do not stop your prescribed medicine or replace it with them on your own — this review found no trustworthy evidence that they can take its place. Turmeric is safe as a spice in food, but concentrated supplements can interact with blood thinners and other medicines, so tell your doctor or pharmacist about every supplement you take. What is proven to help knee osteoarthritis is what many people grow tired of hearing: strengthening the thigh muscles, regular movement, and keeping a healthy weight — steps that lighten the load on the knee every day, without waiting for a miracle capsule.

The bottom line: no strong evidence, and no final verdict

The Cochrane review's conclusion is clear and cautious at the same time: turmeric may ease knee osteoarthritis pain a little or not at all, may improve function and quality of life a little or not at all, and its safety is unconfirmed — all on low-certainty or very low-certainty evidence. That is not a final verdict that turmeric is useless, but a call for longer, larger and more rigorous trials, covering the hand and hip as well as the knee. For today, anyone looking for real relief for their knee will find the shortest path in what the evidence actually proves: movement, muscle strengthening, a healthy weight, and prescribed treatment — not a golden spice in a capsule.

Key facts: the essential numbers

  • Size of the review: 9 randomised controlled trials, 1,245 people with knee osteoarthritis; average age 52–62 years; 67% women; trials in Australia, Belgium, India and Thailand; duration 4 weeks to 3 months.
  • Pain versus placebo after 3 months: a 2.29-point fall with turmeric versus 1.57 with placebo — an extra difference of 0.72 points on a 0–10 scale, below the clinically important threshold (1.5 points); 4 trials, 349 people.
  • Function: an extra improvement of 3.5 points on a 0–100 scale (8.92 versus 5.42); the confidence interval includes zero; 4 trials, 348 people.
  • Quality of life: an extra improvement of 2.19 points on a 0–100 scale (12.02 versus 9.83); 3 trials, 291 people.
  • Global assessment of success: 191 versus 107 successes per 1,000 (risk ratio 1.78); 2 trials, 170 people.
  • Side effects: 226 versus 224 per 1,000; withdrawals because of them 66 versus 46 per 1,000 — very low certainty.
  • Other comparisons: 4 trials versus NSAIDs (673 people) and 1 trial versus paracetamol (193 people) — turmeric was not shown to be better.
  • Trustworthiness screening: 10 trials were left out of the analysis while clarification is awaited from their authors.
  • Certainty: low for pain, function, quality of life and success; very low for safety. The review was published on 19 November 2026, with evidence current to 7 November 2025.
