Cannabis, Even Occasionally: Higher Odds of Stroke and Heart Disease

Share this article:

What did the study find?

Researchers from Tulane University School of Medicine in New Orleans found that adults who use cannabis only occasionally had higher odds of cardiovascular disease and stroke than people who never use it. The results were presented on 9 November 2026 at the American Heart Association's Scientific Sessions in Chicago, and they are preliminary findings that have not yet been peer-reviewed. The analysis included 442,906 US adults with an average age of 49, about half of them women. After adjusting for many factors, people who used cannabis at a low rate, one to five days a month, had 23% higher odds of cardiovascular disease and 40% higher odds of stroke than non-users. Heavy users, 16 to 30 days a month, had 21% higher odds of cardiovascular disease and 25% higher odds of stroke. The researchers found no statistically significant link between cannabis use and heart attacks in these data.

Strikingly, the risk did not rise with more frequent use: it was already elevated among occasional users, which runs against the usual expectation that a larger dose means a larger risk. Lead researcher Aabha Divya said the findings suggest that even infrequent use may be linked to cardiovascular risk, and that more research is needed to understand the mechanism.

How was the study done?

The researchers drew on the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System, a large national health survey run by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. They pooled data from 2020 to 2024 from the states whose survey included the optional marijuana module, between 16 and 24 states depending on the year. Participants were divided into four groups: never users, low users (one to five days a month), moderate users (6 to 15 days), and heavy users (16 to 30 days). The team then compared the groups for diagnosed cardiovascular disease, stroke and heart attack, adjusting for age, sex, race, tobacco smoking, alcohol use, obesity, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, physical inactivity, education and income. That adjustment matters because it reduces the chance that the differences simply reflect other differences between the groups, though it cannot remove that possibility entirely.

What does this study not prove?

This is a cross-sectional study, a snapshot at a single point in time, so it cannot prove that cannabis causes the risk; it shows an association only. It is possible, for example, that people with more health problems are more likely to use cannabis, a problem known as reverse causation. The data also rely on participants reporting their own use, which is prone to forgetting or under-reporting, and the study had no information on the potency of the cannabis used or how it was consumed, whether smoked, eaten or vaped, factors that could affect the risk. One more important point: the specialist site HCPLive reported that the abstract's main adjusted estimates did not reach statistical significance, even though the American Heart Association's release presented the percentages as stated. These figures should therefore be read as associations reported by the association that still need confirmation, not as proven risk. The abstract itself also listed a different participant count, 577,057, while the association's release used 442,906, the figure we use here because it matches the full analysis.

What was known before?

This is not the first study to link cannabis with heart health. In 2024, a study in the Journal of the American Heart Association covering 434,104 adults found that daily cannabis use was linked to 42% higher odds of stroke and 25% higher odds of heart attack, with a dose-response relationship in that study. The new study adds a worrying element: the risk may appear even with infrequent use, and without a clear dose-response pattern. This comes as cannabis use is spreading: the share of US adults who said they used it rose from one in ten in 2020 to one in six in 2024, an increase of 51%. The American Heart Association notes that stroke is the fourth leading cause of death in the United States, claiming 162,639 lives in 2023, and that more than 795,000 Americans have a stroke every year. Expert Robert Page of the University of Colorado warned that people may underestimate the risks of cannabis because it has become legal in many states, while the scientific evidence on its cardiac effects is still in its early stages.

Tip

If you use cannabis and have heart or stroke risk factors such as high blood pressure, diabetes or smoking, talk openly with your doctor about your use, and do not assume that occasional use is risk-free.

What does this mean for you?

If you do not use cannabis, this study is one more reason not to start, especially if you have cardiovascular risk factors. If you use it occasionally, do not take comfort in the low frequency meaning safety: the study found the risk elevated even in the low-use group. Most importantly, these are preliminary findings from a conference abstract, not a final verdict; they are expected to go through peer review and full publication, and details may change. Until then, the practical step is to discuss your cannabis use with your doctor, especially if you have high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol or smoke, because only your doctor can assess your personal risk in light of your full health picture. And remember that quitting smoking, controlling blood pressure and diabetes, and regular physical activity remain the strongest proven ways to protect your heart and brain.
