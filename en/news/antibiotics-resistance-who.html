Antibiotics are failing: resistant Klebsiella infections rose 72% — the WHO warning of 2026

Share this article:

When the first-choice drug stops working

When a patient in hospital develops a serious infection, doctors first reach for a familiar, well-tested antibiotic — a carbapenem or a third-generation cephalosporin. But a new analysis published by the World Health Organization on 18 November 2026 says that this first-choice drug fails in many cases, and that the problem is getting worse year by year. What did the WHO find? And why have the targets set to stop resistance failed?

The analysis is a joint effort by the WHO Regional Office for Europe and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), based on 2025 data from two surveillance networks, covering the WHO European Region — 51 countries.

72%: the target was a fall — the result was a rise

In 2023, European Union countries set a clear target for 2030: cut bloodstream infections caused by carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae by 5% compared with 2019. But the result was exactly the opposite: these infections rose by about 72% between 2019 and 2025. The same pattern repeats with cephalosporin-resistant Escherichia coli (E. coli): the target was a 10% reduction, yet it rose instead by more than 16%.

This means resistance is not falling slowly — it is accelerating in the wrong direction, and the drugs that were the first line of defence are losing their power against two of the most common hospital bacteria.

Resistance has become the norm in nearly half of countries

The most worrying figures concern seriously ill patients: in about half of reporting countries, 50% or more of the Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter samples taken from these patients were resistant to key antibiotics. For Klebsiella alone, in 21 of the 43 countries that reported 2025 data, half or more of the samples were resistant to third-generation cephalosporins.

Dr Marc-Alain Widdowson, from the WHO Regional Office for Europe, said that in many countries in the Region, the antibiotics doctors usually reach for first are failing against serious hospital infections. When the first drug fails, doctors must turn to last-resort medicines that are stronger, more toxic and more expensive — and they may not work either.

Why is this happening? And what is still missing?

Resistance feeds on the overuse and misuse of antibiotics: prescribing them for viral infections they cannot treat, stopping a course before finishing it, and using them without a prescription — on top of resistant bacteria spreading inside hospitals. The WHO notes that 16 of the 51 countries analysed still lack a standardized national approach and coordination in tackling resistance. It is important to note that these data measure resistance in hospital samples, not community infections — in other words, the picture inside hospitals is the harshest one, and that is where the weakest patients are.

Tip

Never take an antibiotic without a doctor's prescription, never share your antibiotic with someone else, and finish the whole course even if you feel better — stopping early leaves the strongest bacteria alive. And remember that antibiotics do not treat colds and flu, because those are viral infections. Protect yourself and your family with vaccinations, regular handwashing and well-cooked food — every infection prevented is an antibiotic we will not need.

The bottom line: an early warning, and time is still on our side

The WHO warning of November 2026 is clear: the 2030 targets were not merely missed — the numbers moved in the opposite direction, and in nearly half of countries resistance has become the norm rather than the exception. But this is not a fixed fate: resistance can be slowed by using antibiotics wisely, by fighting infection in hospitals, by vaccination and hygiene, and by coordinated national plans in the countries that still lack them. And each of us has a part to play, starting with something simple: take an antibiotic only on a doctor's prescription, and never stop the course before finishing it.
