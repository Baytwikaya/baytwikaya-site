Heat weakens medicines: the SHIELD warning — your drug may lose its power without you noticing

Share this article:

A medicine that looks fine… but its power is gone

Imagine a sealed box of medicine, its expiry date not yet passed, its appearance unchanged, its pills the same colour as ever. You take it, reassured — but it does not work as it should. High heat may have weakened it silently, without any sign you can see. That is the warning from a new initiative by Unitaid, called SHIELD, launched at the COP31 climate conference in Antalya, Turkey, on 20 November 2026.

Medicines, like food, are affected by heat — but while spoiled food shows it by smell or colour, a damaged medicine may stay completely invisible. So what did the initiative say? And why does this warning matter to every home, not just hospitals?

What is the SHIELD initiative?

SHIELD stands for Safeguarding the Heat-stability of Essential Medicines and Diagnostics. It is an initiative launched by Unitaid at COP31, with £8 million in funding and backing from donors including the United Kingdom. Its aim is to understand — for the first time in a systematic way — the real heat and humidity that medicines face on their journey from factory to patient, and to protect them.

The idea is simple but important: most medicines are approved on the basis of storage at “room temperature”, meaning up to 30°C or less, but that room temperature, in a hot country, in a truck, or in a warehouse without reliable cooling, can be far higher — and for a long time.

How does heat weaken a medicine?

Vincent Bretin, a physicist at Unitaid, explains that heat speeds up the chemical reactions that break drugs down, so that a rise of 10°C can double the breakdown rate in some reactions. The result is that a medicine may lose part of its strength without changing its shape or colour, and without any change to the expiry date printed on the box.

That is the real danger: the patient takes the full dose, but the effective dose reaching the body is less than the doctor prescribed, without the patient or the doctor knowing. The medicine does not usually turn poisonous; it simply becomes weaker, and may fail to treat the illness or prevent it.

The medicine’s long journey under the sun

Before a medicine reaches a pharmacy or your home, it travels a long way: from the factory to the port, then in trucks, then days in warehouses whose cooling may not be reliable. At each stop it may be exposed to high heat for hours or days, and these exposures add up, even if no single stop crosses the limit dramatically.

And the problem is greatest in the hottest and poorest regions, where cold chains are weakest and where people need medicines most. Heat does not only threaten the medicine itself; it threatens fair access to effective treatment.

Heat and pregnancy: a double risk

The danger is not limited to medicines. A review published in the BMJ found that the risk of preterm birth rises by 5% with every 1°C rise, and by 16% during heatwaves. The heaviest burden falls on poor women in the hottest regions.

That means heatwaves threaten the health of mother and baby from two directions at once: the direct effect of heat on the body, and the weakening of the medicines that protect them. That is why the SHIELD warning came as part of a wider discussion at the climate conference about health in a warming world.

Oxytocin: a medicine that saves mothers and needs a fridge

The clearest example of this danger is oxytocin, the drug that prevents and treats bleeding after childbirth. Postpartum haemorrhage kills about 70,000 women every year, and oxytocin is the first line of defence against it. But this medicine must stay refrigerated between 2 and 8°C, from the factory to the delivery room, without a break.

If the power fails and the fridges warm up, oxytocin may lose its potency without changing its appearance, and be given to a mother weaker than it should be, at the very moment her life is at stake. The heat-stable alternative is carbetocin: a large WHO trial showed it has the same safety and efficacy as oxytocin, and it keeps its potency for 3 years at temperatures up to 30°C without refrigeration. Unitaid is working to reach those who need it.

What will SHIELD do?

The initiative will measure the actual heat and humidity that drugs face from port to patient, identify the drugs most at risk, and work on improving their formulations, packaging and cold chains. The knowledge it produces will be free to all countries, so that the poorest and hottest countries benefit first — the ones most affected.

The final goal is not warning alone, but medicines that can withstand heat, packaging that protects them, and reliable cold chains, so that a medicine reaches the patient with the same strength it left the factory with.

Tip

Store your medicines as written on the box: away from heat, humidity and direct sunlight, and never leave them in a hot car, near a sunny window or on top of the fridge. If your medicine needs a fridge, keep it there between 2 and 8°C, and do not store it in the fridge door, where the temperature swings. If you suspect your medicine was exposed to severe heat, or notice it no longer works as it used to, do not stop it on your own — ask your pharmacist or doctor whether it should be replaced. And remember: the expiry date alone does not guarantee a medicine’s strength if it was stored in high heat.

The bottom line: a strong medicine needs strong conditions

The SHIELD warning is simple and deep at the same time: a good medicine may silently lose its power if it lives in high heat, from factory to home. Heat speeds up the breakdown of medicines, and a 10-degree rise can double the breakdown rate in some reactions, without changing the medicine’s appearance or expiry date. The new initiative — with £8 million in funding — aims to measure the real risk and protect the medicines most exposed to it, led by mothers’ medicines such as oxytocin, which needs constant refrigeration, and its heat-stable alternative carbetocin. Our message today is practical: store your medicine in the right conditions, and ask your pharmacist when in doubt, because a medicine only works at full strength if it reaches you at full strength.
