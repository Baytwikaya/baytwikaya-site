9 November 2026 · Back pain and first-line treatment · Health world news

Before painkillers and before scans: what is the first treatment for back pain according to the World Health Organization?

Pain that one in 13 people in the world knows

In 2020 alone, around 619 million people suffered from low back pain — one in every 13 people on Earth — up 60 percent since 1990. Projections suggest the number could reach 843 million by 2050. Back pain is now the world's leading cause of disability, accounting for about 8.1 percent of all years lived with disability worldwide in 2020. And yet, when pain becomes chronic, the first question patients ask remains simple and confusing at the same time: where do I start?

The common reflex is to start with rest, then a scan, then a strong painkiller. But the world's biggest guidelines say exactly the opposite today. In December 2023, the World Health Organization issued its first-ever guideline for managing chronic low back pain, and in July 2026 the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) updated its guidance in the same direction. Their shared message is clear: the first treatment is not a pill or a scan, but an active plan built on movement, understanding and follow-up.

First: what is meant by "primary chronic back pain"?

Before talking about treatment, we need to define what we are talking about. The guidelines distinguish two types of back pain: pain caused by a specific disease that can be diagnosed — such as a fracture, an infection, a tumour or an inflammatory disease — the less common type, which needs its own treatment path. And primary chronic pain: pain lasting three months or more with no specific disease explaining it, which represents the overwhelming majority of chronic back pain cases in primary care, and which the World Health Organization estimates at no less than 90 percent of them.

This distinction is the key to everything that follows: in primary chronic pain there is usually no single "fault" that can be fixed with surgery, a needle or a pill. Instead, physical, psychological and social factors intertwine — lack of movement, weak muscles, stress, poor sleep and fear of movement — each feeding the others. That is why solutions targeting a single factor fail, while plans that treat the whole picture succeed.

The first step: stay moving, don't wait in bed

The first thing the guidelines recommend, before any other treatment, is understanding and movement. NICE asks practitioners to provide information and advice tailored to each person's condition, helping them manage their pain themselves, while encouraging them to continue their usual activities. The World Health Organization likewise recommends structured education programmes that explain the nature of pain and how to live with it, stressing in particular the benefit of staying physically active and continuing meaningful social and professional activities.

Prolonged bed rest, which doctors used to recommend in the past, is now one of the most common mistakes: it slows recovery, weakens muscles and increases the risk of pain becoming chronic. In acute and subacute pain episodes, the American College of Physicians recommends starting with non-drug options, including superficial heat applied locally — one of the few options rated as moderate-quality evidence in its 2017 guideline. The rule in short: move as much as you can, even in small steps, instead of waiting for the pain to disappear before you move.

The real first-line treatment: a structured exercise programme

If there is one treatment that deserves the title of "first line", it is structured exercise. The World Health Organization recommends offering a therapeutic exercise programme as part of care for adults, including older adults, with primary chronic low back pain. It stresses that the most useful programmes are those tailored to each person's clinical condition, supervised by a specialist, and encouraging adherence until an adequate dose is reached — no less than 20 hours of total programme time. NICE likewise recommends considering a group exercise programme, whether biomechanical, aerobic, mind-body based or a combination, when an episode or flare-up occurs.

And the question that always follows: which type of exercise is best? The honest answer from the evidence is that no single type is clearly superior to the others. Strengthening exercises, aerobic exercise such as brisk walking, stretching and motor-control exercises, and yoga-inspired movements have all shown modest improvement in pain and function. What matters more than the type of exercise is three things: that it suits your condition, that it is supervised at first, and that you stick with it. A programme that stops after two weeks builds no muscle and restores no confidence in the body's movement.

And here is a key point in the World Health Organization's guideline: exercise is not offered alone and in isolation, but as part of a wider package of effective treatments, built on a comprehensive assessment that takes physical, psychological and social aspects into account. Chronic pain is a multifactorial condition, and the plan that works is the plan that treats more than one factor at the same time.

Manual therapy and acupuncture: helpers within a plan, not substitutes for it

Alongside exercise, the World Health Organization allows some physical treatments to be offered as part of care, such as spinal manipulative therapy, massage and acupuncture, for adults including older adults. NICE is narrower here: it recommends considering manual therapy — such as spinal manipulation, mobilisation or soft-tissue techniques like massage — but only as part of a treatment package that includes exercise, a point confirmed in the 2026 update.

And in scientific fairness, it should be said that the guidelines do not agree on everything. While the World Health Organization allows acupuncture as part of the package, NICE does not recommend it for low back pain. This disagreement itself is useful for the reader: it shows that what all the guidelines agree on is what matters most — that hands-on care helps movement and supports it, but does not replace it. Whoever receives manual sessions without an exercise programme is building on an incomplete foundation.

The forgotten side: stress and fear of movement

One of the biggest changes in recent guidelines is viewing chronic pain as an experience that involves the mind as much as the body. The World Health Organization recommends offering psychological treatments, such as cognitive behavioural therapy, as part of the treatment package, and NICE recommends psychological therapies based on a cognitive behavioural approach, but only within a package that also includes exercise.

The core idea is simple but deep: persistent pain does not necessarily mean persistent tissue damage. Fear of movement, expecting the worst, stress and poor sleep all amplify the experience of pain and push the person to avoid movement, entering a vicious circle: less movement means weaker muscles, and weaker muscles mean more pain with any effort. Good education breaks this circle from the start, when the patient understands that safe movement is not a danger to their back, but the treatment itself.

And what about medicine?

Medicine is not outside the plan, but it is not at its head. The World Health Organization allows non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs to be offered to some patients, and NICE recommends considering an oral dose of one at the lowest effective dose and for the shortest possible time, with attention to stomach protection and other risks. As for paracetamol alone, NICE does not recommend it for low back pain.

As for strong painkillers, the guidelines' position is firm: the World Health Organization recommends against using them to manage primary chronic low back pain, and NICE does not recommend them for chronic pain, limiting weak opioids to specific cases — when an anti-inflammatory cannot be used, is not tolerated, or has not helped. This is entirely consistent with what the 2026 study in the journal Brain revealed, which we explained in our previous article on strong painkillers and back pain: after years of daily use, pain levels were almost identical to those of patients not taking them. And it is important to repeat the same warning here: never stop any prescribed medicine on your own; any reduction must be gradual and agreed with the treating physician.

What do the guidelines not recommend?

The strength of modern guidelines is that they do not only say what should be done, but also clearly say what should be avoided. The World Health Organization identified 14 interventions it does not recommend for most people in most contexts, including strong painkillers, traction — which NICE also explicitly does not recommend — and lumbar belts and braces. The guidelines also do not recommend routine imaging for non-specific back pain, because it rarely changes the treatment plan and often shows incidental findings — such as disc bulges or mild degenerative changes — that are also common in people with no pain at all.

This list is not stinginess with treatment, but protection for the patient from treatments that cost and do not help, or help little and harm much. Every unnecessary scan, every extra week on a strong painkiller without review, and every month of bed rest is time lost from the plan the evidence shows actually works: organised movement, correct understanding and regular follow-up.

When should you see a doctor immediately?

Everything above applies to primary chronic back pain — the common pain that no specific disease explains. But there are signs that require urgent medical attention and must not be ignored or handled with an exercise programme alone: new difficulty urinating or passing stool, numbness around the saddle area, worsening weakness in the legs, fever, unexplained weight loss, pain after a strong injury, or night pain that does not ease. These signs may point to a specific cause that needs a completely different assessment, and distinguishing them from common pain is the first step in any treatment plan.

Tip

This week, start with the first step, not the last: walk every day, even for twenty minutes, and ask your doctor or physiotherapist for a structured exercise programme suited to your condition, and ask two specific questions: which exercises are right for me today? And how many hours a week do I need to see real improvement? Don't ask for a routine scan or a strong painkiller before trying the plan the guidelines recommend — the first treatment is movement, medicine is a helper, and regular follow-up with your doctor is what makes the plan a plan.

Conclusion: the first treatment is organised movement, not a pill

What the World Health Organization and NICE are saying is not a call to endure pain without treatment, but a call to order treatment correctly: understanding first, then movement, then a structured exercise programme under supervision, supported by hands-on and psychological care when needed, and an anti-inflammatory medicine at the lowest dose and for the shortest time when necessary. And what the guidelines do not recommend — prolonged rest, routine scans, traction and belts, and strong painkillers for chronic pain — is not deprivation of treatment, but protection from a path proven not to lead anywhere. Chronic back pain deserves a plan built with the doctor, measured by walking, sleep and daily life, not by the pain number alone. Start moving today, and that alone will be a first real therapeutic step.
