Spine bone density linked to brain health and memory: what the Johns Hopkins study found

Share this article:

Your bones may tell us about your brain

Imagine that a single chest scan could reveal something about the future of your memory and concentration. That is what a new study from Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore suggests: measuring the bone density of the spine could one day become an early tool for identifying people most at risk of cognitive decline. The study was published in Radiology, the journal of the Radiological Society of North America.

The researchers found that people whose thoracic vertebral bone density was lower at the start of the study went on to show a faster decline in overall cognitive performance over time, along with clearer changes in the brain's white matter — changes usually linked to worsening memory and a higher risk of dementia. So how did the researchers reach this finding?

What is bone density, and why measure it in the vertebrae?

Bone density is a measure of how strong bones are and how much mineral they contain, above all calcium. When density falls, bones become weaker and more fragile — the condition known as osteoporosis — and the risk of fractures rises, especially in the hip, spine and wrist.

Bone density is usually measured with a dedicated scan, but the researchers in this study took a different route: they measured the density of the thoracic vertebrae — the vertebrae of the mid-back — from chest CT scans that participants had already undergone for other reasons, obtaining information about the bones without any extra examination.

How was the study done? AI reading CT scans

The study drew on data from MESA (the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis). Led by Dr. Sara Mamtazmanesh, a postdoctoral researcher in the Department of Radiology and Radiological Science at Johns Hopkins University, the team used a deep-learning algorithm to analyse the chest CT scans of 2,086 participants and measure thoracic vertebral volumetric bone mineral density at baseline.

The researchers then focused on 715 participants who met the inclusion criteria for the final analysis: they had bone-density measurements, plus brain MRI scans and cognitive tests, allowing the team to follow the link between bone status at the start and the changes that later appeared in the brain and in cognitive performance.

What did the researchers find in the brain?

The results showed that lower spinal bone density at baseline was linked to a faster decline in overall cognitive performance over time. It was also linked to a greater accumulation of white-matter hyperintensities in the corpus callosum — the structure connecting the two brain hemispheres, which supports executive function, working memory and attention.

In addition, the researchers observed a clearer decline in fractional anisotropy in the anterior internal capsule, a region involved in executive functions. Both white-matter hyperintensities and lower fractional anisotropy are linked to cognitive decline and a higher risk of dementia, making these brain markers important indicators of brain health as we age.

An important warning: the results do not prove osteoporosis causes dementia

Here is the most important point in this study: the results do not prove that osteoporosis causes dementia or brain deterioration. It is possible that bone and brain deterioration together result from shared biological ageing mechanisms, rather than one causing the other.

That is why the authors say the value of these findings lies in early detection: measuring bone density could help identify people at higher risk of neurological and cognitive decline, so they can undergo extra screening and have their risk factors — the ones affecting both skeleton and brain — managed jointly. More research is still needed to understand the mechanisms behind this link, and bone-density scans could become an additional early-identification tool, alongside their known role in diagnosing osteoporosis and assessing fracture risk.

Advice

Look after your bones and your brain together: regular physical activity, a diet rich in calcium and vitamin D, quitting smoking, limiting alcohol, and treating any vitamin deficiency or chronic health problem — the same risk factors often affect bones and brain alike. If you have risk factors for osteoporosis, ask your doctor about a bone-density scan, and do not hesitate to raise any change in memory or concentration with your doctor early.

Conclusion: a bone scan could become an early window on brain health

The Johns Hopkins study opens a new door: lower spinal bone density was linked to faster cognitive decline and white-matter changes in the brain among the 715 participants the researchers followed. But the link is an association, not proof of cause, and the mechanisms are still being investigated. Even so, the idea that a simple bone scan could give an early warning of cognitive risk — allowing timely follow-up and intervention — makes these findings a promising step worth watching.
