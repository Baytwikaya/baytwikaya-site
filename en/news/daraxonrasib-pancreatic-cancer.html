A new drug with chemotherapy shrank pancreatic cancer tumours in 60% of patients: what did the trial find?

Share this article:

Pancreatic cancer: among the hardest cancers to treat

Pancreatic cancer is among the hardest cancers to treat, because most cases are found only after the disease has spread beyond the pancreas, and because the chemotherapy available today — the current standard treatment — usually achieves limited results. That is why any new progress in this field draws great attention from patients and doctors alike.

New results presented on 20 November 2026 at the EORTC-NCI-AACR conference in Barcelona caught that attention: an experimental drug taken by mouth, called daraxonrasib, was given with chemotherapy to patients with metastatic pancreatic cancer who had never been treated before, and the numbers were far stronger than doctors are used to seeing with chemotherapy alone. So what did the trial actually find?

What did the trial find? The numbers

In trial RMC-GI-102, phase I/II, 61 patients with metastatic pancreatic cancer — previously untreated — received daraxonrasib daily by mouth together with gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel, and researchers followed them for a median of 18.3 months.

Tumours shrank or disappeared in 60.7% of patients, and the tumour vanished completely, so it was no longer detectable, in 8.2% of them — what doctors call a complete response. Disease control, meaning the tumour stopped growing or shrank, reached 90.2%, and one-year survival was 76.6%.

How does the drug work?

Daraxonrasib, from Revolution Medicines, targets RAS proteins — molecular switches that turn on cell growth, whose mutations, above all KRAS, are the main driver of most pancreatic cancers. The drug cuts the signal that orders the cancer cell to grow and divide.

And the logic of combining it with chemotherapy is clear: chemotherapy kills cancer cells, while the new drug stops the remaining cells from continuing to grow — a double hit instead of a single one.

Read the results carefully: four important caveats

First, this is an early phase I/II trial, with a single arm only, and it included just 61 patients — there is no comparison group that received chemotherapy alone, so we do not know how much of the benefit comes from the new drug and how much from the chemotherapy itself. Second, the trial is funded by the company that makes the drug. Third, the results were presented at a conference and have not yet been published as a full peer-reviewed paper. And fourth, a phase 3 trial, RASolute-303, has now been launched — that is the trial that will answer the decisive question: does this combination actually extend patients’ lives compared with standard treatment?

Tip

If you or a relative has pancreatic cancer, do not seek this drug outside clinical trials — it is not approved and not available, and any offer of it outside medical channels is dangerous. The right step: ask your oncologist about the clinical trials available and about the standard treatment best suited to your case, and stay in follow-up with a specialist team — early diagnosis and specialist care are what make the difference today.

The bottom line: encouraging numbers awaiting confirmation

The results of daraxonrasib with chemotherapy are among the strongest heard about metastatic pancreatic cancer in years: tumours shrinking in 6 out of 10 patients, a complete response in about 1 in 12, and disease control in 9 out of 10. But they come from a small early trial with no comparison group, funded by the manufacturer, and presented at a conference. So the promise is large, and the real confirmation will come from the phase 3 trial — until then, standard chemotherapy remains the foundation.
