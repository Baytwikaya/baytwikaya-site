WHO and 21 countries pledge to cut obesity by 5% by 2030: what is the plan?

Share this article:

More than a billion people live with obesity — and the curve is still rising

More than a billion people around the world live with obesity today — about one person in seven. And this is not a stable figure but a curve that has been climbing for decades: adult obesity prevalence has more than doubled since 1990, and adolescent prevalence has quadrupled. Obesity is not a matter of appearance; it is a risk factor that raises the likelihood of type 2 diabetes, heart disease, some cancers, joint problems and sleep disorders.

Against this background, the World Health Organization announced on 17 November 2026, together with UNICEF and 21 governments, a global call named «Bending the curve by 2030». It was announced after the Global Obesity Summit held in Madrid on 26–28 October 2026, then launched online on 18 November. So what are the signatories pledging? And what is the plan?

What exactly is the goal? A 5% cut by 2030

The stated goal is clear and carries a number: reduce national obesity prevalence by 5% by 2030. In the countries where prevalence is highest, the first step is to halt its rise, before talking about reducing it. Five percent may sound modest, but at the scale of a whole country it means millions of people avoiding obesity and its complications — and a curve that rose for decades finally bending downwards.

The effort sits within a wider programme of 34 countries in the so-called frontrunner programme, where countries share experience and apply tested policies. The new call is signed by 21 governments, led by Spain: Bahrain, Barbados, Botswana, Brazil, Brunei Darussalam, Egypt, Eswatini, Ireland, Jordan, Malaysia, Mauritius, Mexico, Panama, Philippines, Portugal, Seychelles, Slovenia, South Africa, Thailand and Uruguay.

Three fronts: prevention, earlier support, treatment

The call rests on three fronts working together. The first is population-wide prevention: environments that make the healthy choice the easiest one, not the hardest. The second is earlier support for people at higher risk, before obesity and its complications develop. The third is treatment and care that are accessible and affordable for people already living with obesity.

WHO Director-General Dr Tedros said that «the continued rise of obesity is not inevitable», pointing out that the curve can be changed by decisions and policies, not by waiting. Spain's Health Minister Mónica García Gómez said the goal will be judged on whether policies actually reach schools and communities, not on what is written in documents.

What measures are proposed? From schools to the nutrition label

The call sets out a set of practical measures, most of which depend not on individual willpower alone but on the environment around people. In schools: healthier food and school environments that encourage movement and good eating. At the start of life: breastfeeding support. In the market: taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages, clear front-of-pack nutrition labels, restrictions on marketing unhealthy foods to children, and regulation of ultra-processed foods.

These ideas are not new as such, but what is new is several countries pledging to apply them together and at the same time, with joint follow-up on progress. Unhealthy food is cheap, widely available and heavily advertised, and policies are what rebalance the scales, so that the healthy choice is not the most expensive and hardest one.

Read the pledge carefully: a political call, not a binding treaty

And here is the point that must be stated plainly: this call is a political call to action, not a legally binding treaty, and not new funding. The announcement contains no published national baselines for each country, no set budgets, and no penalty mechanism for those who do not comply. Its real value therefore depends on what each country does after signing: will words turn into laws, taxes, labels and school meals, or will the pledge stay on paper?

That is why the signatories themselves said success will be judged by policies reaching schools and communities. The 5% goal by 2030 is not far away, and the next four years are the real test of this pledge.

Tip

Obesity is a problem of environment before it is a problem of willpower, but what you do today still matters: cut down sugar-sweetened drinks, read the nutrition label before you buy, and make daily movement a habit — a 30-minute walk a day is a good first step. If your weight keeps rising, or you have complications such as high blood pressure, diabetes or sleep apnoea, see your doctor to set a realistic plan, and do not rely on a miracle solution or a product sold online.

The bottom line: a measurable goal, and delivery is the test

The «Bending the curve by 2030» call is the first joint pledge of this size to set a clear number: cutting obesity prevalence by 5% by 2030, led by Spain and 21 countries, with the support of WHO and UNICEF. The plan relies on prevention in schools and markets, earlier support, and accessible treatment. But it is a political pledge, not a binding treaty and not new funding, so its success will be measured in schools, shops and communities, not in conference halls. For individuals, the message does not change: less processed food, daily movement and early medical follow-up are the tools available today, while we wait for promises to become policies.
