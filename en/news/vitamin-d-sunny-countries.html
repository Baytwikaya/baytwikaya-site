The sun is there, but the vitamin is missing: why do people in sunny countries suffer from vitamin D deficiency?

Share this article:

A paradox that seems impossible: plenty of sun, little vitamin

The sun shines for about ten hours a day, the sky is clear most of the year, and people walk in bright light — so how can most of the population suffer from vitamin D deficiency, the very vitamin the body makes from the sun itself? This is not a theoretical question, but the conclusion of a systematic scientific review published in July 2025 in the journal Frontiers in Nutrition, which pooled 35 studies on 28,260 people in the United Arab Emirates and concluded that deficiency is widespread despite abundant sunshine.

And the numbers leave no room for doubt about the scale of the phenomenon: the average vitamin D level in adults over 18 was only 17.63 ng/mL — below the accepted deficiency threshold (20 ng/mL) — and study averages ranged from 5.2 to 42.5 ng/mL. Worse still, a large study in Dubai covering 7,924 patients found that 85.4 percent of them were below the 30 ng/mL threshold, and that 17.5 percent had levels of 10 ng/mL or less — severe deficiency. So what happens between the sun and the skin?

How does the body make vitamin D? And why does it matter in the first place?

Vitamin D is not an ordinary vitamin that comes only from food; it is a hormone the body makes: when ultraviolet B rays reach the skin, they convert the cholesterol in it into vitamin D, and then the liver and kidneys complete its conversion into its active form. Its best-known function is regulating the absorption of calcium and phosphorus — building bones and maintaining their density — but its role is broader: it takes part in muscle function and in the balance of the immune system, and studies link its deficiency to muscle weakness, bone pain and a higher risk of fractures, especially in older adults.

That is why vitamin D is not measured by the sun in the sky, but by the sun that actually reaches the skin. Sun exposure from behind a window does not help, because glass blocks UVB rays; sitting in the shade does not help; clothes that cover most of the body do not let sunlight through; and sunscreen — important as it is against sunburn — also reduces vitamin production. In short: the body does not know that the country is sunny; it only knows how many minutes bare skin was exposed to direct rays.

Why isn't the sun enough in a sunny country?

The systematic review itself identified four main reasons for this paradox: cultural clothing that covers most of the body, few outdoor activities, darker skin tone — dark skin needs longer in the sun to produce the same amount of the vitamin — and a lack of dietary sources. Modern life adds a fifth reason that is no less important: extreme heat pushes people to stay inside air-conditioned homes and offices during the day, and to go out mostly after sunset, when the rays no longer help produce the vitamin.

And here is a detail that matters for Morocco too: our country is as sunny as the UAE, and the same conditions exist here — working inside offices, going out in the evening, clothing, and relying on food alone, which provides vitamin D only from a few sources such as fatty fish, eggs and fortified milk. So the lesson from the Emirati study does not concern the UAE alone, but every sunny country whose people spend most of their day under a roof.

Who is most at risk of deficiency?

Deficiency is not distributed evenly. In the Emirati review, 65 percent of participants were women — the group in which little time outdoors and body-covering clothing combine — and the review also noted severe deficiency in infants and children, despite the small number of studies focused on them. In general, risk is higher in older adults — because the skin's ability to produce the vitamin weakens with age — in people who work inside offices all day, in people with darker skin, and in people with obesity, because the vitamin dissolves in fat and gets trapped in fatty tissue instead of staying in the blood.

And the problem is that deficiency is usually silent: fatigue, bone and muscle pain and frequent infections are common, non-specific symptoms that may be attributed to other causes for years. That is why many people do not know they are deficient until a doctor orders a blood test, or until a fracture after a minor fall reveals a fragility nobody knew about.

How do you know your level? And what do the numbers mean?

The only reliable way to know your vitamin D level is a blood test for 25(OH)D. The most commonly used thresholds are: below 20 ng/mL means deficiency, between 20 and 30 means insufficiency, and 30 and above means sufficiency. And here is an important detail for understanding the Dubai figure: the Dubai study used the 30 ng/mL threshold, meaning that the 85.4 percent includes everyone below full sufficiency, not only those in frank deficiency below 20. Even so, the figure remains shocking, because the study's own average was only about 20 ng/mL — right at the deficiency boundary — and 17.5 percent were at 10 or below, which is severe deficiency beyond dispute.

And another detail, for scientific accuracy: the Dubai data were collected between 2012 and 2014 from a single day-surgery hospital, and the patients' average age was 37, with 82 percent under 46 — so it is not a random sample of the whole city's population. But the strength of the result comes from its consistency with the rest of the studies: 35 studies in the systematic review, on 28,260 people, say the same thing — the overall average is 17.63 ng/mL, below the deficiency threshold.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the research

  • Systematic review, Frontiers in Nutrition, July 2025: 35 studies, 28,260 participants in the UAE; average vitamin D in adults over 18 was 17.63 ng/mL (95% confidence interval: 14.28–20.99), below the deficiency threshold (20).
  • Study averages ranged from 5.2 ± 2.8 to 42.5 ± 19.5 ng/mL; 65% of participants were women, 34% men, and 1% infants.
  • Dubai study (7,924 patients, data 2012–2014): average about 20 ng/mL; 85.4% below the 30 ng/mL threshold; 17.5% at 10 ng/mL or less; average age 37, and 82% under 46.
  • The UAE: an average of about 10 hours of sunshine a day — yet deficiency remains widespread.
  • Causes identified in the review: cultural clothing, few outdoor activities, darker skin tone, and a lack of dietary sources.
  • Most commonly used thresholds: below 20 ng/mL deficiency, 20–30 insufficiency, and 30 and above sufficiency.

Tip

Do not assume that sunshine alone is enough for you, and do not take high-dose supplements on your own. The right step: ask your doctor for a 25(OH)D test if you feel persistent fatigue or bone and muscle pain, or if you work inside an office all day or rarely get sun exposure. While you wait for the result: get out into direct sunlight regularly, even for a few minutes — face and arms uncovered — and include fatty fish such as sardines, eggs and fortified milk in your diet. And any vitamin D supplement — its dose and duration — should be decided by your doctor based on your result, especially if you have kidney disease or take medication: high doses without supervision can raise calcium in the blood and harm the kidneys.

Conclusion: sunshine in the sky is not enough — sunshine on the skin is

The story of vitamin D in sunny countries turns the common idea upside down: sunshine alone does not protect against deficiency, because what matters is what reaches the skin, not what reaches the sky. In a country with ten hours of sunshine a day, the average vitamin D level in adults was 17.63 ng/mL — below the deficiency threshold — and 85.4 percent of patients in the Dubai study were below the sufficiency threshold. And the cause is not the absence of the sun, but the absence of exposure to it: life inside homes and offices, clothing, skin tone, and a diet poor in this vitamin. The solution is not a supplement bought without a test, but a simple test that determines your level, then a plan you set with your doctor — regular sunshine, a rich diet, and a supplement only when needed.
