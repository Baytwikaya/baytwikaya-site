Silent signs that may reveal scoliosis in your child: the simple Adam's test at home

Share this article:

A silent curve the child rarely complains about

Your child may be growing fast, outgrowing clothes, and seeming perfectly fine — while the spine slowly curves without any noticeable pain. That is scoliosis, a sideways curvature of the spine, and it is more common among children and adolescents than many parents realise.

A recent systematic review, published in BMC Public Health in October 2025 and covering 239 studies and more than 46 million children and adolescents, found that about 1.65% of children and adolescents worldwide have scoliosis. The good news is that a simple one-minute check at home may draw your attention to the problem early — before it gets worse.

What is scoliosis? And how many children does it affect?

Scoliosis is a sideways curvature of the spine, often accompanied by rotation of the vertebrae. It is diagnosed when the curve, known as the Cobb angle, reaches 10 degrees or more on a standing spine X-ray.

In more than 80% of cases the cause is unknown — so-called idiopathic scoliosis — and it is most often diagnosed in adolescence, when growth speeds up before and during puberty. It is more common in girls, and about 30% of adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis have a family history of the condition — so if a relative is affected, it deserves extra attention.

The silent signs parents miss

Many cases of scoliosis are found by chance, because the early stages usually cause no pain. So do not rely on your child complaining of back pain alone; watch for these signs:

  • One shoulder higher than the other.
  • One shoulder blade protruding more than the other.
  • An uneven waist.
  • The trunk leaning to one side.
  • Clothes not sitting evenly on the body.

One or more of these signs does not mean a diagnosis, but it is a good reason for a closer check — especially during the years of rapid growth.

The Adam's test step by step: one minute at home

The Adam's forward bend test is a simple check you can do at home with no equipment:

  1. The child stands straight, and you look from behind at the level of the shoulders, the shoulder blades and the waist.
  2. The child bends forward slowly from the waist, letting the arms hang naturally — there is no need to touch the toes.
  3. You watch from behind: if one side of the rib cage or back appears higher than the other, it may point to rotation of the vertebrae.

It is important to know the limits of this test: it detects asymmetry only. It does not measure the degree or the type of the curve, it can miss some cases, and it does not replace a medical examination. If you notice any asymmetry, the next step is to see a doctor — not to diagnose it yourself.

Warning signs that need urgent evaluation

Most cases of scoliosis develop slowly, but some signs call for urgent medical evaluation:

  • The curve appearing before the age of 10.
  • Severe or persistent pain at night.
  • Weakness or numbness in the limbs.
  • Changes in the way the child walks.
  • Changes in bowel or bladder control.
  • Difficulty breathing.

Diagnosis: how does the doctor confirm it?

The doctor starts with a clinical examination of the back, shoulders, pelvis, balance and muscle strength, and may use a small device to measure trunk rotation, called a scoliometer. To confirm, a standing spine X-ray is taken and the Cobb angle is measured. MRI is only requested in specific cases, such as neurological symptoms, unusual pain, or a suspicious curve pattern.

When is a brace needed? And when is surgery discussed?

The decision depends on the degree of the curve and on whether the child is still growing:

  • Under 20–25 degrees: usually periodic observation only.
  • About 25–45 degrees in a growing child: a custom-made brace, aiming to limit the progression of the curve during growth, not to guarantee a straight spine.
  • 45–50 degrees or more, or progression despite treatment: surgery is discussed.

Specialised physiotherapy, such as Schroth exercises, may help muscle control, but its effectiveness varies from case to case, and it does not replace a brace when one is needed.

Posture and schoolbags: what is true and what is not?

Many parents blame bad sitting posture and heavy schoolbags, but the Scoliosis Research Society explains that bad posture and heavy schoolbags do not cause structural scoliosis — although they can cause muscle pain and fatigue. Some studies have linked low activity and more screen time to higher prevalence, without proving a direct causal link. The bottom line: improving posture alone cannot treat or prevent a spinal curve.

Sport: helpful, but no guarantee of prevention

Regular sport strengthens muscles and improves fitness, but it does not guarantee prevention of idiopathic scoliosis or its correction. Most children can continue sport and school activities, unless their doctor advises otherwise for their specific case.

Advice

Make checking the back a habit during the years of rapid growth: one minute every few months — compare the shoulders, the shoulder blades and the waist, and try the Adam's test. If you notice any asymmetry, or one of the warning signs appears, do not wait for your child to complain of pain; see a doctor early, because early detection gives simpler and better treatment options.

The bottom line: a minute of attention can change the course of treatment

Scoliosis affects about 1.65% of children and adolescents worldwide, and it usually develops silently, without pain. The signs — a higher shoulder, a protruding shoulder blade, or an uneven waist — can be picked up by the simple Adam's test at home in one minute. The test is not a diagnosis, but it is an early signal worth taking to a doctor, because early action, with observation or a brace, may spare the child a worsening curve and the need for surgery.
